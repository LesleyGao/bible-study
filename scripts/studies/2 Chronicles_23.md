# 2 Chronicles 23 — Going Deeper

## Lens 1: The Historical World Behind the Text

Second Chronicles 23 narrates the overthrow of Athaliah and the public coronation of seven-year-old Joash, c. 835 BCE. The Kings parallel — 2 Kings 11:4-20 — covers the same events but with a strikingly different emphasis. In Kings the coup is a *military-political* operation: Jehoiada works with the Carites (foreign mercenary palace guards, *kārī*) and royal runners, deploys them in tactical formation, and engineers a palace-coup with an ecclesiastical legitimation layer. In Chronicles, by contrast, the coup is *recast as a Levitical-priestly operation*: the Carites disappear from the narrative, the Levites take their place, the temple's purity laws are scrupulously honored ("let no one enter the house of the LORD except the priests and ministering Levites... for they are holy," v. 6), and the entire operation is framed as covenant renewal in three explicit *bĕrīt* (covenant) movements (vv. 1, 3, 16).

The Chronicler — anonymous, post-exilic, fifth- or fourth-century BCE Yehud — is doing what Sara Japhet (*The Ideology of the Book of Chronicles*; *I & II Chronicles*, OTL) calls *priestly retelling*: events recorded in Kings as politically driven are reread as theologically and liturgically driven. Ralph Klein (*2 Chronicles*, Hermeneia) develops the comparison at length: the Kings narrative has Carite mercenaries and royal guards executing the coup; the Chronicler swaps these out for Levites and "all Judah." Klein notes that this is not merely cosmetic; it changes the *theological grammar* of the event from political legitimation to liturgical restoration. H. G. M. Williamson (*1 and 2 Chronicles*, NCBC) treats the chapter as a key example of the Chronicler's *Levitical-revisionism*: foreign mercenaries do not belong in a covenant story, so they are written out and the Levites take their function. Gary Knoppers (Anchor Yale) and Steven McKenzie (*1–2 Chronicles*, AOTC) note the Chronicler's careful retention of the *temple-purity* framework — even in the middle of a coup, no unclean person enters the holy precincts — as a marker of the Chronicler's distinctive priestly sensibility. Mark Boda (*1–2 Chronicles*, CBC) reads the chapter as a piece of post-exilic *Verfassungsgeschichte* — constitutional history written backwards — in which the Chronicler models how a faithful priestly leader (Jehoiada) restores the proper alignment of king, priest, temple, and people.

The vocabulary signals the theological framing. *Bĕrīt* (בְּרִית, "covenant") appears three times in close succession: (v. 1) Jehoiada makes covenant with the commanders; (v. 3) the assembly makes covenant with the king in the house of God; (v. 16) Jehoiada makes covenant between himself, the people, and the king "that they should be the LORD's people" (*lihyōt lā-ʿām la-YHWH*). Three covenants in twenty-one verses is not accidental — the Chronicler is constructing a *triple-covenant restoration* paradigm. *ʿĒdūt* (עֵדוּת, "testimony," v. 11) is what Joash receives along with the crown — most likely a copy of the Torah or the covenant document, echoing Deuteronomy 17:18-20's requirement that the king have a copy of the law. *ʿAm hā-ʾāreṣ* ("the people of the land," vv. 13, 20, 21) is the Chronicler's term for the engaged Judahite citizenry — the people who actually participate in the renewal.

The historical question of what *actually* happened in 835 BCE is debated. Most modern critical scholars accept the basic outline of a Jehoiada-led coup overthrowing Athaliah and installing Joash, with Kings preserving the more "raw" version closer to court records and Chronicles reframing for the post-exilic priestly audience. The textually fascinating detail is the *spears and shields of David* (v. 9) that Jehoiada distributes from the temple — these are the captured Aramean weapons David dedicated to Yahweh in 2 Samuel 8:7-12. The Chronicler is making the Davidic-restoration explicit: David's own weapons return to defend David's heir.

## Lens 2: The Literature Itself

The chapter is structured as a five-act drama with a tight chiastic frame.

Act one (vv. 1-3): *covenant convocation*. Jehoiada *takes courage* (*hithazzaq*, "strengthened himself," v. 1) — the same verb used in chapter 22 of Athaliah's earlier moves, redeployed now for the priest's faith. He summons "the commanders of hundreds" (*sārê ha-mēʾōt*) by name — five Hebrew patronymic naming clauses — and they "went about through Judah and gathered the Levites" and the family heads. The verb sequence — *yāsōbū bi-Yhūdāh*, *yiqbĕṣū hal-Lĕwiyyīm*, *yābōʾū yĕrūshālayim* ("they went around in Judah, gathered the Levites, came to Jerusalem") — is a covenant-pilgrimage pattern. The first covenant (v. 1) is between Jehoiada and the commanders; the second (v. 3) is between the assembly and the king. The Chronicler frames both as occurring *bĕ-bēyt hāʾĕlōhīm* ("in the house of God") — the temple is the legitimating space.

Act two (vv. 4-7): *operational instructions*. Jehoiada gives detailed instructions for the temple's defense during the coronation. The thirds — one third gatekeepers, one third at the king's house, one third at the Foundation Gate — fit the Levitical-priestly course system the Chronicler articulates in 1 Chr 23–26. The instruction *let no one enter the house of the LORD except the priests and ministering Levites* (v. 6) is the *kī qōdesh hēmmāh* clause ("for they are holy") — the temple's purity laws override any military convenience. Verse 7's *the Levites shall surround the king* (*sĕbībōt ha-melekh*) is a striking image: priests as armed bodyguard. Williamson notes that this is the Chronicler's most pointed inversion of the Kings narrative — where Kings has Carite mercenaries surrounding the king, the Chronicler has Levites with weapons drawn. The *priestly* king is guarded by *priestly* warriors.

Act three (vv. 8-11): *coronation*. The Levites "did according to all that Jehoiada the priest commanded" (v. 8). The transfer of David's spears and shields from the temple armory (v. 9) is a deeply symbolic act — the dynasty's own ancestral weapons return. The crown (*nēzer*, v. 11) and the testimony (*ʿēdūt*, v. 11) are placed on the seven-year-old. The proclamation *yĕḥī ha-melekh* ("long live the king") is the same phrase used at Solomon's coronation (1 Kgs 1:39). The Chronicler is re-anchoring the Davidic line at this moment.

Act four (vv. 12-15): *Athaliah's death*. Athaliah hears the noise, comes into the temple — and sees Joash *standing by his pillar* (*ʿōmēd ʿal-ʿammūdō*, v. 13), the trumpeters and singers around him, the people rejoicing. She tears her clothes and cries *qesher qesher* ("treason, treason"). The Hebrew is one of the chapter's strongest theological ironies: she who staged her own *qesher* against the Davidic line now calls *qesher* on the legitimate heir. Jehoiada's order is precise: *bring her out between the ranks*; *do not put her to death in the house of the LORD* (v. 14). Even at the climax of the coup the temple's holiness is honored. She is killed at the *Horse Gate* — the place where palace and city meet — symbolically returned to the political-military space where her power was exercised.

Act five (vv. 16-21): *covenant renewal and Baal-cult demolition*. The third covenant (v. 16) is the most theologically explicit: *lihyōt lā-ʿām la-YHWH* ("to be a people for the LORD") — the Sinai covenant formula. The people demolish the Baal temple, break the altars and images, and kill Mattan the Baal-priest (v. 17). Jehoiada then reorganizes temple worship "according to the order of David" (*ʿal-yĕdê Dāwīd*, v. 18) — the Chronicler's signature Davidic-liturgical framework. Verses 20-21 close: the king is brought from the temple to the throne through the upper gate; the people of the land rejoice; *the city was quiet* (*shāqĕṭāh*) — the same root used of Jehoshaphat's rest in 2 Chr 20:30. The chapter's literary architecture brings us back to covenantal *menūḥāh* (rest) after the violence.

## Lens 3: The Larger Story

In the Hebrew canon Chronicles closes the *Tanakh*, and chapter 23 has been read by Jewish tradition as a paradigm of *priestly leadership in royal vacuum*. The rabbinic tradition treats Jehoiada with extraordinary reverence — *b. Sanhedrin* 47b notes that Jehoiada was buried in the royal tombs with the kings (2 Chr 24:16) "because he had done good in Israel." Rashi (eleventh century) reads the chapter's tripled covenants as a model for *brit* renewal in any age: between leader and lieutenants, between the assembly and the heir, between the people and Yahweh. Radak (twelfth–thirteenth century) emphasizes the Chronicler's specific Levitical-priestly framing as legitimating post-exilic priestly authority. Abarbanel (fifteenth century) reads the chapter as a model of *legitimate revolution*: Athaliah's deposition is not rebellion but restoration, because the *bĕrīt* with David is the underlying constitutional order. Sara Japhet, in modern Israeli scholarship, reads chapter 23 as the Chronicler's *ideal-type* of constitutional restoration — priestly leadership, Levitical participation, popular acclaim, Davidic-throne legitimacy, covenant renewal, idolatry destruction, all in proper sequence. Isaac Kalimi catalogs the Chronicler's specific priestly amplifications as a deliberate program: the temple is not the *backdrop* of the coup; it is the *engine*.

Christian reception has been substantial. Jerome's Vulgate, Cassiodorus's *Expositio*, and the medieval lectionary all gave the chapter substantial coverage as a story of *the church preserving the throne* — Jehoiada the priest as a type of ecclesial leadership protecting the rightful king. Aquinas (*Summa Theologiae* I-II q. 105 on the Old Law's political ordering) treats Jehoiada's coup as evidence that priestly authority can legitimately depose a usurping ruler in defense of the divinely-instituted order; this is one of the Hebrew Bible texts the medieval *two-swords* tradition leaned on. Calvin's 1561 lectures on Chronicles read the chapter as a model of *legitimate magistracy under God*: the *amphictyonic* gathering of priests, Levites, commanders, and people of the land all participating in covenantal restoration. Calvin's insistence that this was *not* private rebellion but covenant-renewal under proper authority shaped Reformed political thought (cf. Knox in Scotland, the *Vindiciae contra tyrannos*). Modern Christian commentary (Williamson, Klein, Selman, Pratt, Boda, Hill) has tended to read chapter 23 as a Christological foreshadowing — the hidden true king is brought out, crowned, and acclaimed; the usurper is removed; the people enter covenant. The Christ-and-the-church typology is exegetically defensible but should not flatten the text's specific claims about priestly leadership and Davidic legitimacy.

The New Testament does not directly cite 2 Chr 23, but the *yĕḥī ha-melekh* ("long live the king") tradition reaches forward through every Christian acclamation of Christ as king — *Maranatha*, the Palm Sunday *hosanna*, the Revelation 11:15 *the kingdoms of this world have become the kingdom of our Lord and of his Christ*.

## Lens 4: Theological Intent

The Chronicler's agenda in chapter 23 is *legitimate covenantal restoration mediated through priestly leadership*. Where the Kings narrative tells a coup story with theological dressing, the Chronicler tells a covenant-renewal story with strategic detail. Three covenants, three movements of the people, three guardposts at the temple, three classes (priests, Levites, people of the land) participating — the chapter is densely structured around the covenantal architecture the Chronicler thinks all of Israel's restoration needs.

Three tensions deserve careful engagement.

First: *priestly versus political framing*. The Kings-Chronicles divergence here is one of the clearest in the entire Chronicler's work. Three positions: (1) The *Chronicler-as-historian* reading (older confessional commentary, Selman, Pratt): the Chronicler had access to additional priestly source material (perhaps the temple-archive records he frequently cites) that legitimately fills out what Kings compresses; both accounts are historically valid, with different emphases. (2) The *Chronicler-as-revisionist* reading (Williamson, Klein, Knoppers, Boda): the Chronicler is *intentionally rewriting* the Kings account to swap Carites for Levites, foregrounding priestly agency for theological reasons. The historical event was probably closer to Kings; the Chronicler's version is theologically true even where it is historically loose. (3) The *both-true* reading (Japhet, Jonker): the Chronicler's reframing is itself a theologically legitimate act of historical interpretation; ancient Near Eastern historiography was always interpretive, and the Chronicler's interpretive frame — that the coup was, theologically considered, a Levitical-covenantal restoration — captures something the Kings narrative misses. The steelman of position 3: it preserves both the historical-critical insight (the Chronicler is doing more than reporting) and the theological insight (the Chronicler's reading captures the deeper meaning of the event). Where this leaves us: we read a chapter that tells us *what Yahweh's covenantal restoration looks like even if the actual coup was messier than this*. That is a legitimate theological-narrative function.

Second: *the child-king tradition*. Joash is seven years old at his coronation. He becomes the youngest king of Judah. The tradition of *vulnerable rulers* — children, the disabled, women, the foreign-born — stands in tension with the ancient Near Eastern norm of the strong adult male warrior-king. Three positions: (1) The *providential-instrumentation* reading: God uses what he chooses; Joash's youth highlights Yahweh's preservation more than human capability. (2) The *covenant-priority* reading: the king is what he is by virtue of the *bĕrīt*, not by virtue of personal qualities; a seven-year-old can hold the office because the office is itself covenantal. (3) The *delegated-regency* reading (more historically minded): the actual ruling power for years was Jehoiada's; the Chronicler is showing that Davidic legitimacy is preserved even when actual governance is necessarily delegated. Modern Christian and Jewish reception of the *vulnerable-ruler* tradition has been productive. The honest engagement: the chapter celebrates the survival of the line through a child, but the next chapter (2 Chr 24) shows the cost — Joash is faithful as long as Jehoiada lives, and apostatizes immediately after. The chapter's celebration must be read alongside its sequel's caution.

Third: *the violence of the coup*. Athaliah is killed. Mattan the Baal-priest is killed. The Baal temple is demolished. Jehoiada's instruction *anyone who follows her is to be put to death with the sword* (v. 14) is unambiguous. The Chronicler does not soften this. The honest engagement is to acknowledge that the chapter's celebration of restoration includes acts of legitimate-revolutionary violence that contemporary readers will weigh differently. Three positions: (1) The *just-revolution* reading (Calvin, the *Vindiciae* tradition, Reformed political thought): Athaliah was a usurper whose deposition was theologically and politically legitimate; her execution was a judicial act, not murder. (2) The *covenantal-anti-idolatry* reading (Knox, Edwards, the broader Puritan tradition): the demolition of the Baal cult was itself a covenant act commanded by Deut 13; the killing of Mattan was a covenant-judicial execution. (3) The *honest-with-violence* reading (much modern critical and pastoral commentary): the chapter celebrates a real act of violence; we should neither sanitize it nor flatten it; we should let it stand as part of the Chronicler's frank acknowledgment that covenantal restoration in a fallen world sometimes involves violence we cannot rest in. Where this leaves us: the chapter does not romanticize bloodshed, but it does not apologize for it either. Reading it well requires holding the *yĕḥī ha-melekh* and the bodies at the Horse Gate together.

Closing question: when our institutions need restoration after long usurpation, what would it mean to recover the Chronicler's specific architecture — covenant before action, priestly leadership rather than mercenary force, popular participation, the honoring of holy space even at the climax of struggle — rather than the cheaper alternatives our age tends to reach for?

## The Thread

— The Davidic *bĕrīt* tradition reaches from 2 Sam 7 / 1 Chr 17 through 2 Chr 23:3 to the messianic acclamations of the New Testament.
— The *yĕḥī ha-melekh* coronation cry connects 1 Kgs 1:39 (Solomon) to 2 Chr 23:11 (Joash) and forward to Christological acclamations.
— Anti-idolatry covenant-renewal reaches from Deut 13 and 2 Kgs 23 (Josiah) into 2 Chr 23:17.
— The temple-as-holy-precinct framework runs from Exod 19, Lev 16, 1 Chr 23–26 into 2 Chr 23:6, 14.
