# 1 Samuel 3: The Boy Who Heard What the Old Priest Could Not

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

The chapter is set inside the Shiloh sanctuary at night, with the *ner Elohim* — the lamp of God — still burning but burning low. The detail is not incidental archaeology. The Tabernacle prescriptions in Exodus 27:20-21 and Leviticus 24:1-4 require the *menorah* to burn continually from evening to morning; the lamp is "not yet gone out" places the action in the small hours, when the lamp's oil would have been nearly depleted. Israel Finkelstein's excavations at Khirbet Seilun confirmed substantial pillared structures at Iron I Shiloh that could plausibly correspond to the kind of architectural complex the chapter describes — a building with doors that Samuel can open in the morning (v. 15), inner rooms where Eli sleeps "in his own place" and where Samuel sleeps near the ark. This is not the portable tent of meeting of Pentateuchal description; it is a permanent sanctuary, a *heikhal* (called such already in 1:9), and the archaeology supports the reality of such a structure at Shiloh in the eleventh century BCE before its destruction.

The political-religious situation is signaled in the programmatic opening: "the word of the LORD was rare in those days; there was no frequent vision." The Hebrew is *u-devar YHWH hayah yaqar ba-yamim ha-hem; ein ḥazon nifrats* — and the word "rare" (*yaqar*) literally means "precious, costly, scarce." The narrator is making a historical-theological claim: the Iron I tribal period was a time of prophetic dearth. Whether one takes this as accurate historical memory or as deuteronomistic framing, the implication is the same — Israel had institutional priesthood at Shiloh but had lost living prophetic communication, and a child's ears were the conduit by which it returned.

The dating of the call narrative is debated. Hertzberg (OTL) and Klein (WBC) both place the underlying tradition in the late pre-monarchic period, with editorial framing by the deuteronomistic redactor. McCarter (Anchor Bible) treats it as an integral part of the Birth Narrative of Samuel (chs. 1-3), one of Rost's proposed pre-deuteronomistic sources. The textual situation is again messy: 4QSam-a preserves a notably different reading at v. 13 — where the MT has Eli's sons "blaspheming God" (*me-qallelim lahem*, possibly emended; the MT actually reads *lahem*, "to themselves," widely treated as a *tiqqun sopherim*, a scribal correction softening "blaspheming God" to "blaspheming for themselves"), 4QSam-a and the LXX have *me-qallelim Elohim*, "blaspheming God," directly. This is a paradigmatic case where the Qumran fragments preserve the original blasphemy that the Masoretes softened for piety's sake. The standard Tov and Ulrich treatments cite this verse as a textbook example of the *tiqqun sopherim* phenomenon.

A few Hebrew terms repay attention. **תְּצִלֶּינָה** (*tetsillenah*, "will tingle," v. 11) is a rare verb of dread — used elsewhere only of cataclysmic divine judgments (2 Kgs. 21:12 and Jer. 19:3 both echo this verse, suggesting it became a stock prophetic formula for unbearable news). **לֹא יָסוּף** (cognate of *nasaf*, "blasphemy/curse") is the issue at v. 13 — and the textual variants make clear that ancient scribes found the implication of cursing God too direct and softened it. **כִּלְיוֹתָיו** is not in this chapter, but **מִדָּן וְעַד בְּאֵר שָׁבַע** (*mi-Dan ve-ad Be'er Shava*, "from Dan to Beersheba," v. 20) is — and it is the *only* use of this idiom in the Samuel-era narratives. Elsewhere it appears in Judges 20:1 and in Saul/David/Solomon contexts. Its appearance here is editorial: the dtr framer is signaling that Samuel's prophetic authority extends across the entire tribal territory, anticipating the unified kingdom that is about to begin. **נֶאֱמָן** (*ne'eman*, "established/faithful," v. 20) is the same root as the "faithful priest" of 2:35 — the dtr framer is connecting the dots: Samuel is the *kohen ne'eman* the man of God promised, at least provisionally. The promise will later attach to Zadok as well.

## Lens 2 — The Literature

Genre first. This is a **prophetic call narrative**, one of the cleanest examples in the Hebrew Bible. Norman Habel's classic 1965 article "The Form and Significance of the Call Narratives" identified a recurring six-element pattern: divine confrontation, introductory word, commission, objection, reassurance, and sign. Samuel's call follows the pattern but with modifications — there is no objection, and the reassurance comes from Eli rather than from God directly. Susan Niditch and Robert Wilson have both contributed important refinements; Robert Gnuse's *The Dream Theophany of Samuel* (1984) is the standard monograph specifically on this chapter, arguing that the call form here belongs to a sub-type — the auditory dream-call — that is paralleled in Mesopotamian sources (the dream-calls of Gudea and others). The Hebrew Bible has comparable call narratives in Exodus 3 (Moses), Isaiah 6, Jeremiah 1, and Ezekiel 1-3; comparing them sharpens what is distinctive about Samuel's call.

What is distinctive: the threefold mis-attribution. Samuel hears "Samuel!" and three times runs to Eli, three times Eli says "I did not call, my son; lie down again." Robert Alter calls this the "three-times-and-then" pattern of Hebrew narrative, used to build narrative tension before a decisive reversal. Bar-Efrat treats the threefold structure as deliberate retardation — the narrator slows the action so that the reader feels the dawning recognition along with Eli. The fourth call, with the formulaic "Speak, LORD, for your servant hears" (the LXX adds "LORD" where the MT has only "Speak, for your servant hears"; Klein and McCarter both prefer the longer LXX/4QSam-a reading), is the moment the boy crosses the threshold from priestly attendant to prophet.

The literary device that organizes the chapter is **vision and blindness**. Eli's eyes have "begun to grow dim so that he could not see" (v. 2) — the narrator's first detail about him in the chapter. Samuel's vocation is to receive a *ḥazon*, a vision (v. 15). The chapter opens with the absence of *ḥazon nifrats* (v. 1, "frequent vision") and closes with Samuel as established prophet. The blind priest gives the seeing prophet his commissioning instructions. This is a literary inversion as sharp as anything in the chapter that follows. Polzin in *Samuel and the Deuteronomist* notices that Eli's blindness is also moral: he could not see what his sons were doing. The literal blindness is the symptom of the moral blindness, and the moral blindness is what the prophetic word now indicts.

The placement of the chapter is structurally crucial. Chapters 1-3 form a narrative arc — Samuel's birth, growth, call — that culminates in v. 20: "all Israel from Dan to Beersheba knew that Samuel was established as a prophet of the LORD." The next sentence begins the Ark Narrative (Rost's proposed source, chs. 4-6). The editor has used Samuel's call as the bridge between two source-blocks: the Birth Narrative ends here, and the Ark Narrative begins — but Samuel's prophetic authority is what holds them together. Notice that Samuel does not appear in the Ark Narrative at all (chs. 4-6 are entirely about the ark, Eli's sons, the Philistines); he reappears at 7:3. This is a strong argument for the source-critical hypothesis that 4-6 was originally an independent ark-cycle that the editor of Samuel inserted, with chs. 1-3 functioning as the prophetic prologue.

The textual variants are again revealing. The LXX has a longer, more developed reading of Eli's response in v. 18 — including a more elaborate self-abasement formula. 4QSam-a preserves a notable variant in v. 14: where the MT has the iniquity of Eli's house "shall not be atoned for by sacrifice or offering forever," 4QSam-a and the LXX add an emphasis on the impossibility of atonement that the MT omits. The textual history of Samuel is, in microcosm, visible in this chapter.

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

**Jewish readings.** Josephus (*Antiquities* 5.10.4) tells the call narrative with characteristic embellishment, making Samuel twelve years old (matching the age of the boy Jesus in the temple in Luke 2 — possibly not coincidence). The rabbis (Berakhot 7b, Yoma 22b) treat Samuel as the supreme prophet of his generation and read the call as the model for prophetic vocation: the prophet does not initially recognize the divine voice, the older mentor must teach him to listen, and the first prophetic word is one of judgment against the very institution that trained him. Pesikta de-Rav Kahana 9 expands the threefold mis-attribution into a meditation on patience — God calls three times because the soul needs time to learn to hear. Rashi reads the chapter for its narrative details and notes the textual oddness of v. 13. Radak treats Samuel's age (he was probably about twelve, in rabbinic chronology) as theologically significant: a child can hear what the adults have stopped hearing. Ralbag focuses on the philosophical question of how a child can receive prophetic vision; he treats it as a sign of Samuel's exceptional natural disposition cooperating with divine grace. Abarbanel reads the chapter as the indictment of Eli's failure to discipline his sons: the entire judgment hangs on the phrase "the iniquity that he knew, because his sons were blaspheming God, and he did not restrain them" (v. 13). For Abarbanel, this is the political-theological lesson — leaders who know of corruption and do not restrain it bear the corruption themselves. Avivah Zornberg's reading is psychologically penetrating: she treats Eli's "Speak, for your servant hears" as the moment when the blind priest hands the boy the language of his own destruction. Yair Zakovitch on the parallels with other call narratives is essential.

**Christian readings.** The patristic tradition almost universally read Samuel's call as the model for monastic vocation. Origen treats Samuel as the type of the soul awakened by God; Chrysostom's homilies use the call as the model for catechetical training (the older priest teaches the younger to recognize the divine voice). Augustine in several sermons uses Samuel's "Speak, for your servant hears" as the formula of true contemplation. Gregory the Great's *Moralia* and *Pastoral Rule* both invoke Eli as a warning to bishops and Samuel as the model of the receptive soul. Aquinas treats the call in the *Summa* (II-II q.171-178 on prophecy) as a textbook case of how prophetic grace is given. Calvin's sermons read the chapter with characteristic attention to the corruption of Eli's house: the call of Samuel is YHWH's response to institutional failure, and the message Samuel must deliver is the heart of the story — not the dramatic vocation but the painful indictment. Luther focuses on the boy's obedience and the older priest's faith ("It is the LORD. Let him do what seems good to him") as twin models of the believer's posture. Eugene Peterson in *Leap over a Wall* (his pastoral reading of David, but with reflections on Samuel's call) treats the chapter as a parable of how God speaks in the silences of corrupted institutions. Ellen Davis on the call as paradigm of attentive listening is widely cited. John Goldingay in his *Old Testament Theology* (vol. 2, *Israel's Faith*) treats Samuel's call as a programmatic statement that the prophetic word can break through any institutional decay.

The critical tradition has done careful work on the form. Wellhausen treated the chapter as the boundary between the Birth Narrative and the Ark Narrative — a position that has held up well. Rost's source-critical analysis (1926) made the case that chs. 1-3 function as a deliberate Samuel-prologue, and that the Ark Narrative was originally independent. Cross and McCarter develop the textual case using 4QSam-a. Polzin's literary reading (the blind priest, the seeing prophet) is the standard contemporary reading. Brueggemann's commentary treats the chapter as a study in how prophetic speech "enters the world" — disruptively, painfully, against the institutions that should have welcomed it.

**Feminist readings.** The chapter has fewer feminist treatments than chs. 1-2 because Hannah is absent — but Phyllis Trible and Cheryl Exum both note that the absence is itself significant: Hannah has handed over her son, and the narrative has moved on without her. The price of her vocation as mother of a prophet is silence in the prophetic narrative itself. Tikva Frymer-Kensky reads this as the limit of Hannah's role: she gave Samuel his voice, but the voice now speaks without her.

Where do the traditions converge? On Samuel as the model of receptive prophetic vocation, and on Eli's "It is the LORD; let him do what seems good to him" as the model of pious resignation in the face of just judgment. Where do they diverge? On whether Eli's response is admirable (Christian and most Jewish readings) or culpably passive (Abarbanel, some critical readings — Eli still does nothing about his sons after hearing the judgment). The text leaves the ambiguity open.

## Lens 4 — The Theological Intent

What did the author believe God was doing? Two genuine tensions.

**Tension 1: A child as the bearer of prophetic judgment against his mentor.** The narrative places the burden of the worst possible message on the smallest possible bearer. The Catholic tradition has tended to read this as evidence of God's freedom to choose unlikely instruments, with the moral burden on Samuel softened by his obedience and Eli's reception. The Reformed tradition reads it more starkly: the prophetic word does not respect age or station, and the bearer of judgment must speak even against those who raised him. The Orthodox tradition treats the chapter as a study in the *kenotic* nature of prophetic vocation — the prophet empties himself of attachment in order to speak. Jewish tradition is more ambivalent: the rabbis worry about the *halakhic* status of a child as bearer of prophecy and develop elaborate distinctions, but they accept Samuel's case as exceptional. The position I find least intuitive — and which I think is actually the most morally serious — is the Jewish concern: there is something wrong with placing the burden of an institution-destroying judgment on a child, and the text neither denies it nor defends it. It just reports it. Where this leaves us: a divine economy in which the messenger is selected by readiness to hear, not by capacity to bear the message.

**Tension 2: The iniquity that "shall not be atoned for by sacrifice or offering forever" (v. 14).** This is one of the strongest unconditional curses in the Hebrew Bible. The Levitical sacrificial system exists precisely to atone for sin, including priestly sin (Lev. 4:3-12 specifies the offering for an anointed priest who sins). And yet here YHWH swears that the iniquity of Eli's house *cannot* be atoned for — not by *zevach* (sacrifice), not by *minḥah* (grain offering), forever. Catholic theology has historically treated this as a particular judicial curse that does not nullify the general sacramental economy; the sins are atonable through direct repentance even when sacrifice fails. Reformed theology reads it more rigorously: there are sins whose temporal consequences are sealed even when their eternal consequences are not — the house will fall, even if individual members are saved. Orthodox theology treats it within the framework of corporate judgment: the *house* is judged; individual members may still find mercy. Jewish theology (the rabbis in Yoma 87a) reads the verse as referring to a specific class of sins — desecration of the sacred — and treats the unforgiveness as practical (the sacrifice cannot help if the offerer despises sacrifice itself) rather than absolute. The position I find least intuitive — the rabbinic one — is actually the most coherent: the curse is on a sin that consists precisely in despising the means of atonement, and so the sin is self-foreclosing. Where this leaves us: a doctrine of sin that includes the possibility of putting oneself outside the sacramental economy by the very nature of the sin itself.

One unresolved question to leave with: is the "faithful priest" of 2:35 Samuel, as the chapter seems to suggest — or is the editor pointing past Samuel toward Zadok, whose installation in 1 Kings 2 closes this loop?

## The Thread

When you get to **1 Kings 19**, the call of Elijah at Horeb, remember Samuel at Shiloh. Both prophets are summoned at the moment of Israel's institutional failure; both receive the word in a quiet, almost domestic setting (lamp, cave); both are commissioned to deliver judgment against the existing order. The Elijah narrative is the deliberate echo of Samuel's call. When you get to **Jeremiah 1:6** ("Ah, Lord GOD! Behold, I do not know how to speak, for I am only a youth"), remember Samuel — the *na'ar* (youth) is the recurring biblical type of the unlikely prophetic voice, and Jeremiah self-consciously claims the type. When you get to **Luke 2:41-52**, Jesus at twelve in the temple, remember Samuel: the boy in the sanctuary, growing in stature and in favor with God and man (Luke quotes Samuel almost verbatim), already addressing the elders with words they cannot quite receive. And when you get to **Hebrews 1:1-2** ("Long ago, at many times and in many ways, God spoke to our fathers by the prophets, but in these last days he has spoken to us by his Son"), remember the opening of 1 Sam 3 — "the word of the LORD was rare in those days" — and notice that the New Testament is making the symmetrical claim: the word is rare no longer, because the Son has come.
