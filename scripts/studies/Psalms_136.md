# Psalm 136 — Going Deeper

## Lens 1: The Historical World

Psalm 136 is anonymous in the MT, with no superscription. It sits in **Book V** (Pss 107–150) immediately after Psalm 135 (with which it shares overlapping content) and is paired with it as a hymnic diptych closing the post-Ascents anthological hymnody. Psalm 136 is one of the most distinctively-shaped pieces in the entire Psalter, defined by its single relentless feature: **every one of its twenty-six verses ends with the refrain *ki le-olam chasdo*** (כִּי לְעוֹלָם חַסְדּוֹ, "for his steadfast love endures forever"). The psalm is thus the longest sustained antiphonal-responsion text in the Bible.

Hermann **Gunkel** classified Psalm 136 as a **hymn** (*Hymnus*), though specifically as an **antiphonal hymn** with a distinctive refrain-form. **Mowinckel** read it as the cult-functional liturgy par excellence: one voice (priest, leader, soloist) recites the deed; the congregation responds with the *ki le-olam chasdo* refrain. The form is so unmistakably designed for call-and-response that there is little dispute about its Sitz im Leben — this is **temple liturgy preserved in canonical form**. **Hossfeld and Zenger** treat it as a deliberately composed post-exilic responsion-piece, possibly the exemplary text of the Second-Temple choral tradition.

In Jewish tradition Psalm 136 is known as the **Hallel ha-Gadol** (הַלֵּל הַגָּדוֹל, "the Great Hallel") — distinct from the Egyptian Hallel of Psalms 113–118 (recited at Pesach, Sukkot, Shavuot, Rosh Hodesh, and Hanukkah) and from the Hallel ha-Yom of Psalm 145. The Babylonian Talmud (b. Pesachim 118a) preserves a debate about the precise scope of the Great Hallel, with Rabbi Tarfon and Rabbi Akiba arguing over whether it consists of Psalm 136 alone or a longer cluster (Pss 135–136 or 120–136). The dominant tradition identifies the Great Hallel as Psalm 136 in particular. The psalm is recited at the **Passover Seder** (after the meal, before the fourth cup), in the Sabbath morning Pesukei d'Zimra in some rites, and at festival morning prayers.

Dating: the psalm's intertextual reliance on Psalm 135's content (Egypt, Sihon, Og, conquest) and on Genesis 1's creation language (vv. 5–9 — sun, moon, stars, dividing waters) places it firmly in the post-exilic period. The cosmological strophe (vv. 5–9) shows clear dependence on the **Priestly creation account** of Genesis 1, which most scholars date to the exilic or early post-exilic period; the historical strophe (vv. 10–22) draws on Pentateuchal-historical-Psalter language. The psalm thus belongs to the late-Persian or early-Hellenistic period, when the Psalter was reaching its canonical form.

The Hebrew opening: *hodu la-Yahweh ki tov, ki le-olam chasdo* (הוֹדוּ לַיהוָה כִּי טוֹב כִּי לְעוֹלָם חַסְדּוֹ, "give thanks to YHWH, for he is good, for his steadfast love endures forever," v. 1). The opening *hodu* is a covenantal-thanksgiving summons (cf. Pss 105:1, 107:1, 118:1, 1 Chr 16:34). The pivotal Hebrew word is *chesed* (חֶסֶד) — typically rendered "steadfast love," "lovingkindness," "covenant loyalty," or "mercy" — which signifies God's covenant-grounded faithful commitment to his people. **Nelson Glueck**'s mid-twentieth-century study and the subsequent work of **Katharine Doob Sakenfeld** (*The Meaning of Hesed in the Hebrew Bible*) established that *chesed* is fundamentally a *relational-covenantal* term, not a generic emotional one — God's *chesed* is God's covenantal-loyal love, committed and durable. The repetition of *ki le-olam chasdo* twenty-six times across the psalm is the longest sustained meditation on *chesed* in the entire Bible.

The number twenty-six is theologically charged in Jewish tradition: it is the gematria-value of the Tetragrammaton YHWH (10 + 5 + 6 + 5 = 26). Whether the psalmist deliberately structured the psalm around twenty-six refrains for this reason is uncertain, but the rabbinic tradition has long noted the correspondence.

ANE parallels: refrain-driven antiphonal hymns are widely attested in Mesopotamian liturgy (e.g., the *eršemma* prayers and *šuilla* incantations) and in Egyptian temple hymnody. Ugaritic ritual texts also preserve antiphonal patterns. What is distinctive in Psalm 136 is the *single refrain* sustained across twenty-six verses — extant ANE parallels typically vary their refrains.

Textual transmission: 11QPs-a at Qumran preserves portions of Psalm 136 in its idiosyncratic arrangement; the LXX matches MT closely. No significant text-critical issues.

## Lens 2: The Literature

The psalm divides into **five strophes**, all governed by the *ki le-olam chasdo* refrain:

- **Strophe 1 (vv. 1–3)** — opening summons (three invocations: *hodu la-Yahweh*; *hodu lelohei ha-elohim*; *hodu la-adonei ha-adonim*).
- **Strophe 2 (vv. 4–9)** — the creation recital (cosmic deeds, Genesis 1).
- **Strophe 3 (vv. 10–16)** — the Exodus recital (Egypt, Red Sea, wilderness).
- **Strophe 4 (vv. 17–22)** — the conquest recital (Sihon, Og, the heritage).
- **Strophe 5 (vv. 23–26)** — the closing — God's remembrance, rescue, food-for-all-flesh, and the closing summons.

The arc: cult → cosmos → Exodus → conquest → personal deliverance + universal provision → closing benediction. The architecture is the same as Psalm 135's — but where Psalm 135 was a single-voice cento, Psalm 136 is a two-voice antiphony.

**Lowth's parallelism** in the psalm has an unusual character: the two halves of every verse are not parallel in the usual sense. The first half varies (the deed), and the second half is invariant (the refrain). This is what some commentators call **vertical parallelism** or **refrain-parallelism**, where the parallel runs across verses rather than within them.

Within the first halves, classical parallelism still operates. Synonymous parallelism: "to him who struck down great kings... and killed mighty kings" (vv. 17–18). Synthetic / climactic parallelism: the Red-Sea sequence builds across vv. 13–15. Antithetic parallelism: "led Israel through... but overthrew Pharaoh in the Red Sea" (vv. 14–15).

**The refrain.** *Ki le-olam chasdo* — twenty-six iterations. This is not redundancy; it is **liturgical-theological saturation**. Every cosmic act, every historical deliverance, every personal rescue, every universal provision is marked with the same divine attribute: God's covenantal-loyal love endures forever. The refrain functions as:

1. **Liturgical scaffolding** — the congregation's response to the soloist's recitation.
2. **Theological commentary** — every divine deed is interpreted through the lens of *chesed*.
3. **Mnemonic anchor** — the worshipper memorizes Israel's entire salvation-history through the refrain.
4. **Doxological completion** — every line of recitation is sealed with praise.

**Inclusio.** The opening *hodu la-Yahweh ki tov, ki le-olam chasdo* (v. 1) is matched by the closing *hodu lelohei ha-shamayim, ki le-olam chasdo* (v. 26) — *hodu* opens and closes; the refrain runs throughout. The opening triplet (vv. 1–3) is paralleled by no exact closing triplet, but v. 26 returns to the divine-name-and-thanksgiving structure of v. 1.

**Imagery.** The cosmological strophe (vv. 4–9) uses **Genesis 1 vocabulary** — God who "made the heavens by understanding," who "spread out the earth above the waters," who made the "great lights" (sun, moon, stars). The historical strophe (vv. 10–22) uses concrete proper nouns (Egypt, Pharaoh, Sihon, Og). The closing strophe (vv. 23–26) shifts register entirely: from cosmic-historical recital to personal-confessional ("he who remembered us in our low estate," v. 23) and universal-providential ("he who gives food to all flesh," v. 25). The arc thus broadens from particular Israel to all flesh — and then closes with the universal title "God of heaven" (*elohei ha-shamayim*, v. 26).

## Lens 3: The Larger Story

**Book V placement.** Psalm 136 is paired with Psalm 135 as a closing diptych on the post-Ascents anthological hymnody. The two psalms cover overlapping material; Psalm 135 is single-voice cento, Psalm 136 is antiphonal-responsion. The editorial logic positions them as the canonical-liturgical climax of the post-Ascents Hallel cluster.

**Jewish trajectory.** The Talmudic identification of Psalm 136 as the **Hallel ha-Gadol** (Great Hallel, b. Pesachim 118a) places it at the structural heart of Jewish liturgical life. It is recited at the Passover Seder after the meal, just before the fourth cup of wine. The placement is exegetically apt: the psalm rehearses the Exodus deliverance with the *chesed*-refrain, making the entire seder a meditation on God's covenantal love operating across creation, redemption, and the present generation.

*Midrash Tehillim* on Psalm 136 dwells on the *chesed* refrain, treating it as the key to all of Israel's history: every deed of God is an instance of *chesed*. The Midrash also takes the threefold opening summons (vv. 1–3) — to YHWH, to the God of gods, to the Lord of lords — as a graded recognition of divine sovereignty across the cosmos, the heavens, and the earth.

**Rashi** glosses the Genesis-1 dependence in vv. 5–9 explicitly: the psalm is summarizing the priestly creation account in liturgical form. **Ibn Ezra** treats the psalm's structure as a recitation of God's universal and particular benefits — first cosmic (vv. 4–9), then particular to Israel (vv. 10–22), then personal (vv. 23–25). **Radak** elaborates the historical reading and notes the gematria connection (twenty-six = YHWH).

In modern Jewish reception the psalm has had an extraordinary career. The *ki le-olam chasdo* refrain is the most-sung Hebrew liturgical line in the world, sung at every Passover Seder, in countless daily and Sabbath services, and embedded in the Pesukei d'Zimra of Sephardic and many Ashkenazi rites. **Heschel**'s reflections on covenant and time draw heavily on the *chesed* theme. **Levenson** treats the psalm as a key piece of evidence for the post-exilic crystallization of Israel's covenantal self-understanding. **Sarna** and **Buber** both have rich engagements with the *chesed* concept that the psalm anchors.

**Christian trajectory.** **Augustine**'s *Enarratio* on Psalm 136 develops the *chesed*-refrain as a meditation on God's *misericordia* (the Latin Vulgate translates *chesed* as *misericordia* throughout). For Augustine the refrain is a sustained confession that every divine act, from creation to personal rescue, flows from God's eternal mercy. He reads the cosmological strophe Christologically — the Word of John 1 making the heavens "by understanding" (v. 5) — and the closing universal-provision (v. 25) as anticipating the Eucharistic table.

**Cassiodorus** systematizes Augustine's reading. **Aquinas** treats the psalm in the *Summa Theologica*'s discussion of God's mercy and providence; he reads the *misericordia*-refrain as a doxological summary of the divine attribute of mercy. **Luther** preached on Psalm 136 frequently, treating the *chesed*-refrain as the most concentrated witness to *gratia* in the entire Old Testament. **Calvin** built much of his Psalms-commentary doctrine of God's providence around the psalm.

In Catholic liturgy Psalm 136 is part of the Liturgy of the Hours rotation. In Anglican BCP it appears in the cycle of evening psalms. The Reformed tradition has used it in call-and-response services — the worship-leader reciting the historical recital; the congregation responding with "for his steadfast love endures forever." Modern congregational hymnody draws extensively on the refrain — Matt Redman's "Forever," Chris Tomlin's "Forever Reign," and many others all quarry the *chesed*-refrain.

Modern critical scholarship: **Gunkel** classified the psalm an antiphonal hymn. **Mowinckel** read it as the cult-functional liturgy par excellence. **Westermann** treated it as descriptive praise with refrain. **Kraus** worked out the strophic structure. **Hossfeld and Zenger** read it as the post-exilic responsion-liturgy that closes the post-Ascents Hallel cluster. **John Goldingay** treats the *chesed*-refrain as theological commentary on each recited deed. **Gordon Wenham** notes the gematria correspondence (twenty-six refrains = YHWH). **Walter Brueggemann** in *Theology of the Old Testament* reads Psalm 136 as the canonical synthesis of Israel's testimony to God's *chesed*. **Patrick Miller** and William **Brown** track the *chesed* concept through the Psalter. **Beth Tanner** highlights the closing shift to universal scope (v. 25 — "food for all flesh"; v. 26 — "God of heaven").

**The *chesed* theology of God's steadfast love.** Modern scholarship on *chesed* — beginning with **Glueck** (1927), through **Sakenfeld** (1978), and the work of **Walther Eichrodt**, **Gerhard von Rad**, and **R. Laird Harris** — has established that *chesed* is fundamentally a covenant-relational term: it denotes the loyalty, faithful commitment, and steadfast love that one party shows another within a covenanted relationship. When the psalm says God's *chesed* "endures forever," it is making a covenantal-temporal claim: God's covenant-loyal love is irreducibly faithful, across every age. This is not generic divine benevolence; it is covenant-bound, and the covenant is what makes the love irrevocable.

**NT use.** Psalm 136 is not directly cited in the New Testament, but the *chesed*-refrain is paralleled in many places. The **Lord's Supper** institution, recited at Passover (Matt 26:26–29 par.), takes place in the liturgical context of the Great Hallel — Jesus and his disciples almost certainly recited Psalm 136 together that night. The **Magnificat** and the **Benedictus** (Luke 1:46–55, 67–79) draw on *chesed*-vocabulary throughout (Greek *eleos*). Romans 11:32 ("God has consigned all to disobedience, that he may have mercy on all") echoes the universal scope of v. 25's "food for all flesh."

## Lens 4: The Theological Intent

**Tension 1: The form — liturgy or literature?** Psalm 136 is so unambiguously antiphonal that it seems hardly a "literary" text in the conventional sense. Its twenty-six refrain-verses make it almost unreadable as silent literature; the form demands liturgical performance.

*Position A — the psalm is liturgy first, literature second.* Mowinckel's strict cult-functional reading. The psalm is what it does: a temple antiphony preserving the call-and-response of post-exilic Israelite worship. To "read" it silently is to misread it.

*Position B — the psalm is literature precisely because it is liturgy.* The deliberate compositional design — twenty-six refrain-verses, gematria-keyed to the divine name, structurally mapping creation, Exodus, conquest, and personal rescue — is a literary achievement of high order. The form is the meaning. **Goldingay** reads it this way.

*Position C — the psalm refuses the dichotomy.* Liturgy and literature are not opposed; ancient Israel's liturgy was its highest literary achievement. Psalm 136 is what canonical-liturgical writing looks like when it succeeds: a text that performs its theology in its very form.

**Steelman.** All three positions converge in practice. The psalm is liturgy. The psalm is literature. The psalm is canonical-theological writing in which form, content, and use are inseparable.

**Tension 2: The 26-fold *chesed* refrain — saturation or monotony?** The refrain repeats twenty-six times. To modern ears trained on the prized originality of expression, this can read as monotonous. The traditional liturgical answer is that the repetition is the point: in saturating the worshipper's mind with one truth, the psalm forms the worshipper.

*Position A — repetition is formation.* The *chesed*-refrain functions catechetically: by hearing and singing "his steadfast love endures forever" twenty-six times in a single sitting, the worshipper internalizes the claim. This is how liturgy shapes theology, not by argument but by saturation. **Calvin** and the Reformed tradition's emphasis on the formative power of liturgical repetition fits this reading.

*Position B — repetition is testimony.* Each iteration of the refrain is a separate testimony to a separate divine deed. Twenty-six deeds, twenty-six testimonies. The repetition is not redundant but cumulative: the more deeds, the more witnesses to *chesed*'s scope.

*Position C — repetition is doxological completion.* In ANE liturgical convention, no act of God is sung without doxological closure. The *ki le-olam chasdo* refrain is the doxological seal on each recitation. The psalm refuses to recite a single deed without immediately confessing the *chesed* that grounded it. This is a theological discipline as much as a liturgical practice.

**Where this leaves us.** Psalm 136 is the Great Hallel — the Bible's most extended single meditation on God's covenant-loyal love. Its twenty-six refrains stretch from cosmos to personal rescue to food-for-all-flesh, and every divine deed is sealed with the same confession. The form is itself the theology: in every act of God's, the same *chesed* is at work, the same fidelity, the same enduring love. The psalm refuses to let any part of Israel's history (or human history) be told without that confession. This is the canonical answer to the question "why this and not that?" — *for his steadfast love endures forever*. It does not explain why; it confesses what.

The Passover-Seder placement of Psalm 136 makes the theological claim concrete: the same *chesed* that delivered Israel from Egypt is present at this table; the same *chesed* will sustain the next generation; the same *chesed* governs cosmos, history, and food for all flesh. The Christian reception of the psalm — at the Eucharistic table, in monastic vigils, in Reformed call-and-response — has carried the same claim forward. The psalm is the Bible's most sustained answer to the question of what kind of love grounds the universe.

**Closing question.** Can a love be confessed twenty-six times in a row without becoming routine? The Great Hallel says yes — but only if the love is real enough to bear the weight of repetition. Whether modern liturgical communities still know how to repeat without being bored is, in a small way, a test of whether the *chesed* the refrain confesses still has its grip on the singers.
