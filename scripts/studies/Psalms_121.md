# Psalm 121 — Going Deeper

## Lens 1: The Historical World Behind the Text

Psalm 121 is the second of the **Songs of Ascents** (*Shir HaMa'alot*, שִׁיר הַמַּעֲלוֹת) — the fifteen psalms (Pss 120–134) that constitute one of the most carefully shaped sub-collections in the Psalter. The superscription, present on every member of the group except possibly the slight variant on Ps 121 itself (which reads *shir la-ma'alot*, "a song *for* the ascents," with the prepositional *lamed* — a textual peculiarity discussed below), most plausibly identifies these as **pilgrim songs** sung by Israelites traveling up to Jerusalem for the three Deuteronomic festivals (Passover, Pentecost/Shavuot, and Tabernacles/Sukkot, per Deut 16:16). The verb *alah* (עָלָה) — to go up — is the standard biblical idiom for pilgrimage to Zion (cf. 1 Sam 1:3; Isa 2:3), and Jerusalem's elevation in the Judean hills makes this a literal as well as metaphorical ascent. *Mishnah Sukkah* 5:4 preserves a separate but compatible tradition: the Levites sang these fifteen psalms on the **fifteen semicircular steps** that led from the women's court to the men's court (Court of Israel) in the Second Temple during the all-night water-drawing celebration of Sukkot (*Simchat Beit ha-Sho'evah*) — one psalm per step. Sigmund Mowinckel's cult-functional reading interpreted the Songs of Ascents as actual liturgical music for processions; Hermann Gunkel placed them in the broader history of Israelite festival hymns.

If Psalm 120 begins the journey in lament — exile in Meshech and Kedar — Psalm 121 is **the pilgrim's confidence song**, sung either on the road or at first sight of Zion's hills. Gunkel's form-critical genre is **psalm of trust** (*Vertrauenspsalm*), with strong elements of priestly **assurance oracle** (*Heilsorakel*). The structural clue is the shift in person between the first strophe and the rest: vv. 1–2 are first-person ("**I** lift up my eyes... **my** help"); vv. 3–8 are second-person, addressed to a singular "you." This has generated a small literature of its own. The most widely accepted reading (Hossfeld–Zenger, Goldingay, Brueggemann) treats the psalm as a **dialogue** — perhaps between pilgrim and priest at a city-gate liturgy of blessing, or between two pilgrim companions, or between the pilgrim's anxious self and the trusting self answering back. The shift from "my help" (v. 2) to "your keeper" (vv. 3–8) marks a movement from interior question to received word.

The opening line is one of the most famous in the Psalter: *essa einai el-he-harim me-ayin yavo ezri* (אֶשָּׂא עֵינַי אֶל־הֶהָרִים מֵאַיִן יָבֹא עֶזְרִי, "I lift up my eyes to the hills — from where will my help come?", 121:1). The Hebrew interrogative *me-ayin* (מֵאַיִן, "from where?") is the crux. ESV, NRSV, and most modern translations punctuate v. 1 as a question; the King James and many older versions punctuated it as a statement ("I lift up mine eyes unto the hills, *from whence* cometh my help"). The Masoretic accentuation supports a question. This affects the theological reading enormously, as we will see in Lens 4.

The dating is post-exilic. The pilgrimage collection presupposes a functioning Jerusalem cult, and the editorial logic of Book V (Pss 107–150) — the post-exilic-shaped final book of the Psalter — places the Songs of Ascents after the great Torah meditation of Ps 119 and before the concluding Hallel doxology. The **Dead Sea Scrolls** (11Q5, the Great Psalms Scroll from Cave 11) preserve Ps 121 in a recognizable form, though the scroll's overall arrangement differs from the MT. The **Septuagint** numbers it Ps 120 (one behind MT) and translates the superscription *ōdē tōn anabathmōn* (ᾠδὴ τῶν ἀναβαθμῶν, "song of the steps") — preserving the staircase imagery that informed the Mishnaic tradition.

The keyword of the psalm is the verb *shamar* (שָׁמַר), "to keep, watch, guard." It and its cognates appear **six times** in eight verses: *yishmor* in vv. 3, 7, 8 (twice in v. 7, once each in 3 and 8); the participle *shomer* in vv. 3, 4, 5; and the noun phrase *shomer Yisrael* (שׁוֹמֵר יִשְׂרָאֵל, "the keeper of Israel") in v. 4. In a psalm of fifty-four Hebrew words, six instances of one root constitutes saturation. The verb is the same one used of the priestly Aaronic blessing in Numbers 6:24 (*y'varekhekha Yahweh ve-yishmerekha*, "the LORD bless you and keep you") and of the cherubim *keeping* the way to the Tree of Life in Genesis 3:24. *Shamar* names protective vigilance — the watchman on the wall, the shepherd over the flock, God over Israel.

The image *shomer Yisrael lo yanum ve-lo yishan* (שׁוֹמֵר יִשְׂרָאֵל לֹא יָנוּם וְלֹא יִישָׁן, "the keeper of Israel will neither slumber nor sleep," v. 4) carries pointed ANE polemic. **The contrast is with Baal**, the Canaanite storm god whom Elijah mocked on Mount Carmel (1 Kgs 18:27) for being possibly "asleep and must be awakened." Mesopotamian and Ugaritic mythology routinely depicts gods sleeping, waking, and being roused; *Enuma Elish* and the Baal Cycle both feature divine slumber. Psalm 121 places Israel's God in explicit polemical contrast: the LORD does not nod off, does not need to be awakened, never goes off duty.

The **sun-and-moon** imagery of v. 6 (*yomam ha-shemesh lo-yakekkah ve-yareach ba-laylah*, "the sun shall not strike you by day, nor the moon by night") similarly recasts ANE astral piety. In Mesopotamian religion, Shamash and Sin were great gods to be feared and propitiated; in Egyptian religion, Ra and the moon-god Khonsu were astral deities. The Hebrew verb *nakah* (נָכָה, "strike, smite") evokes both sunstroke (a real desert hazard for pilgrims walking up to Jerusalem in summer heat) and the demonic dread of the moon (the Akkadian *ardat-lilî* night demons, the lunar madness later named *seleniasmos* in Greek and *lunaticus* in Latin). The pilgrim's God controls both luminaries; neither can strike the one whom the LORD keeps.

## Lens 2: The Literature Itself

The psalm divides into **four strophes of two verses each**, each strophe bound by paired Hebrew terms and developing the *shomer* theme. Strophe 1 (vv. 1–2) poses the question and gives the answer in synonymous parallelism: "from where my help" / "my help from YHWH." The closing tag *oseh shamayim va-aretz* (עֹשֵׂה שָׁמַיִם וָאָרֶץ, "maker of heaven and earth") is a creator-confession formula that recurs in the Songs of Ascents (cf. Pss 124:8; 134:3) and serves as the collection's theological signature.

Strophe 2 (vv. 3–4) deploys negative parallelism: "he will not let your foot be moved" / "he who keeps you will not slumber" / "he who keeps Israel will neither slumber nor sleep." The graded repetition is characteristic of the Songs of Ascents — phrases tighten and intensify across lines. Note the climactic move from generic "he who keeps you" (singular addressee) to "the keeper of Israel" (corporate); the individual pilgrim is taken up into the people. The merism *yanum ... yishan* — both verbs of sleep, with *nahum* meaning a brief drowse and *yashan* a deeper sleep — covers all sleep states.

Strophe 3 (vv. 5–6) introduces the *tzilkha* (צִלְּךָ, "your shade") metaphor. *Tzel* (shade, shadow) is a standard biblical metaphor for protection (cf. Ps 91:1; Isa 25:4). The phrase *al-yad yeminekha* ("at your right hand") evokes the position of a defender or advocate — the right hand is the sword hand and the place of the helper. The sun-and-moon merism of v. 6 employs **antithetic temporal parallelism** (day / night) wrapped within a unified protection — the LORD's keeping covers all hours.

Strophe 4 (vv. 7–8) closes with another graded intensification: "from all evil" → "your life" → "your going out and your coming in" → "from this time forth and forevermore." The phrase *tzeitkha u-vo'ekha* (צֵאתְךָ וּבוֹאֶךָ, "your going out and your coming in") is a Hebrew **merism** for the totality of life's activity (cf. Deut 28:6; 2 Sam 3:25) — going-out covers all departures (battle, work, travel), coming-in all returns. Combined with *me-attah ve-ad-olam* ("from this time forth and forevermore"), the merism is spatial-temporal: every motion, every moment.

Beyond the *shamar* repetition, the psalm exhibits a striking **chiastic-ish pattern of inclusio**: the eyes lifted to the hills (v. 1) are answered by the LORD's keeping eyes (vv. 3–4 imply the watchman's eye, "neither slumber nor sleep"). The pilgrim looks; the LORD looks back, and never blinks.

The psalm's brevity — eight verses, fifty-four words — is part of its rhetorical power. It is a credo that fits in the mouth and on the breath of a person walking uphill.

## Lens 3: The Larger Story (Jewish and Christian Trajectories)

Within Book V's editorial logic, Ps 121 is the second step of the Songs of Ascents staircase. Where Ps 120 begins in exile, Ps 121 lifts the eyes — the pilgrim has begun to see the destination, and the question of where help comes from is the question of every traveler crossing dangerous terrain. The road from Jericho up to Jerusalem ascended nearly 3,500 feet through brigand-infested wadis; the road from the coastal plain wound through hills that hid robbers (the Good Samaritan parable assumes precisely this geography). The pilgrim's question in v. 1 is not abstract: it is the prayer of a person looking at hills that may hide help or hide danger.

In **Jewish reception**, Ps 121 occupies extraordinary liturgical territory. It is recited at multiple traditional moments: as part of the *Tachanun* supplication; as a psalm for protection during travel (the *Tefillat HaDerekh*, the traveler's prayer, draws on its themes); at the bedside of the seriously ill; at funerals; at the *brit milah* and the first hair-cutting (*upsherin*) of children; and in the daily evening service in many communities. The phrase *shomer Yisrael lo yanum ve-lo yishan* is sung as a refrain in the *Selichot* penitential service. **Rashi** reads v. 1's "hills" as the patriarchs and matriarchs whose merit (*z'khut avot*) the pilgrim invokes; **Ibn Ezra** reads the hills as the mountains of Jerusalem itself (Zion, Moriah, Olivet); **Radak** reads them as the high places where pagan gods were worshipped — and the pilgrim's question is then a renunciation: "from *those* hills my help does not come." The interpretive disagreement among the medieval Jewish commentators directly anticipates the modern critical debate. **Midrash Tehillim** on Ps 121 develops the *shomer Yisrael* motif extensively, contrasting the LORD's vigilance with the inattentive deities of the nations. **Buber**, **Heschel**, and **Levenson** treat the psalm as the central Jewish text of *bitachon* (trust), and **Sarna** highlights the *shamar* repetition as the psalm's literary spine.

In **Christian reception**, Ps 121 has been a constant companion of the church's prayer life. **Augustine**'s *Enarrationes* on Ps 120 (Latin numbering) reads the hills as the prophets and apostles — the elevated witnesses who point upward to Christ, from whom alone help comes. **Cassiodorus** elaborates the Christological reading. **Aquinas** treats v. 4 as proof of the divine attribute of providence. **Luther**'s 1532–35 *Operationes in Psalmos* lectures on the Songs of Ascents make Ps 121 a centerpiece of his theology of the wayfaring church (*ecclesia viatorum*). **Calvin** (1557 commentary) reads it as the inner-life confession of every faithful soul under threat. The psalm has been set to music in nearly every Christian tradition: the Genevan Psalter, the Anglican chant tradition, the Reformed psalters; **Felix Mendelssohn**'s *Elijah* (1846) sets v. 4 as the magnificent trio "Lift Thine Eyes" followed by the chorus "He, watching over Israel" — among the most beloved sacred choral works in the Western canon. The psalm is a fixture of the **Catholic Liturgy of the Hours** (recited in the daytime offices), of **Anglican Compline**, of Reformed psalm-singing traditions, and of the Eastern Orthodox office (where it appears in the Hours and in the funeral service). Modern scholarly commentary — **Gunkel**, **Mowinckel**, **Westermann**, **Kraus** (BKAT), **Hossfeld–Zenger** (Hermeneia), **Goldingay** (Baker), **Wenham** (on the Psalter's shape), **Brueggemann** (the lament-trust dialectic), **Miller**, **Brown**, and **Tanner** — converges on the psalm of trust genre while disagreeing on the dialogue structure and the v. 1 translation crux. **Eugene Peterson**'s *A Long Obedience in the Same Direction* devotes an entire chapter to Ps 121 under the title "Help" — naming the psalm as the discipleship-affirmation that follows the Ps 120 confession of exile.

The **New Testament** does not directly cite Ps 121, but its theology of God as ceaseless keeper informs Jesus' teaching that the Father knows when a sparrow falls (Matt 10:29), Paul's confidence that nothing separates from the love of God (Rom 8:38–39), and 1 Peter's exhortation to cast all anxiety on the one who cares (1 Pet 5:7). Jude's closing doxology to the one "able to keep you from stumbling" (Jude 24) draws directly on the *shamar* tradition of Ps 121.

## Lens 4: The Theological Intent

**Tension 1 — Where do the eyes look, and where does the help come from?** The translation crux at v. 1 is theologically loaded. If the line is read as a **statement** ("I lift up my eyes to the hills, *from where* my help comes"), the hills themselves are sources of help — and given that Jerusalem and Zion sit on hills, the line affirms that help comes from God-on-Zion. If the line is read as a **question** ("I lift up my eyes to the hills — from where will my help come?"), the answer in v. 2 is a **correction**: the help does not come from the hills (which in ANE religion housed pagan high places, *bamot*, where Canaanite Baal and Asherah were worshipped) but from the LORD who *made* the hills along with all heaven and earth. Goldingay (*Psalms 90–150*) and Hossfeld–Zenger argue forcefully for the question reading on contextual and Masoretic accentuation grounds — the *me-ayin* interrogative is most naturally a question, and the v. 2 answer's emphasis on the **maker** of heaven and earth (rather than what is made) makes best sense as a corrective. *Steelman for the statement reading:* The hills around Jerusalem are the hills of Zion, where the Temple stands; lifting one's eyes to them is lifting one's eyes to the place where God dwells. The poetry is more devotional than polemical. *Steelman for the question reading:* The Hebrew syntax favors a question, the polemic against ANE high-place religion is well-documented in the prophets, and the psalm's distinctive contribution is precisely the move from looking-at-the-hills to looking-past-the-hills to the Maker.

**Tension 2 — Does "the LORD will keep you from all evil" mean what it seems to say?** Verse 7 makes a sweeping promise: *Yahweh yishmorkha mi-kol-ra* ("the LORD will keep you from all evil"). Read flatly, this contradicts virtually every other psalm of lament in the Psalter, the book of Job, the cross of Christ, and the empirical experience of every honest believer. Pilgrims to Jerusalem were robbed and killed on the Jericho road; Israelites suffered exile; saints were martyred. **Three positions** stand in the literature. **(1) The eschatological reading** (Augustine, much Reformed tradition): the keeping is ultimate, not penultimate — God keeps the soul, the life, the eternal destiny, even when the body suffers. **(2) The covenantal-narrative reading** (Brueggemann, Goldingay): the promise is communal-corporate, addressed to "Israel" (v. 4), and fulfilled across the long arc of God's faithfulness to the covenant people, not a guarantee of individual immunity. **(3) The pilgrim-specific reading** (Mowinckel, Hossfeld–Zenger): the promise is liturgically specific — it is the priestly assurance for *this* pilgrimage, this journey to Zion and back, not a universal claim about all evils at all times. *Steelman for each:* The eschatological reading takes seriously the full shape of biblical theology, including the cross. The covenantal-narrative reading honors the corporate "Israel" of v. 4 and refuses the prosperity-gospel misuse of v. 7. The pilgrim-specific reading honors the genre and Sitz-im-Leben — this is a traveler's psalm, not a metaphysical theodicy. All three converge in refusing to read v. 7 as a personal-protection guarantee.

**Where this leaves us:** Psalm 121 names the keeping that the pilgrim depends on — vigilant, personal, communal, cosmic. The keeping is not the absence of the journey's dangers; the journey is real, the hills hide things, the sun strikes by day. The keeping is the unsleeping attention of the One who made the hills and is therefore not afraid of them. The promise's sweep ("from all evil... your going out and your coming in... from this time forth and forevermore") is real but eschatologically held — the LORD keeps the *life* (Hebrew *nefesh*, often "soul" but here the whole living self) even through what the LORD does not prevent. Mendelssohn's chorus had the literary instinct right: *He, watching over Israel, slumbers not, nor sleeps.* The pilgrim sleeps; the keeper does not.

The psalm leaves us with a question shaped by the gap between v. 7's universal promise and the world's actual evils: *what is it that the LORD does not let stop, even when many other things happen?*

What in your week needs the kind of help that does not come from the hills you can see?
