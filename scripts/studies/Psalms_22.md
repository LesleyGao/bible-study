# Psalm 22: My God, My God — The Lament That Becomes Praise

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

The superscription reads *lamenatzeach al-ayyelet ha-shachar mizmor le-David* (לַמְנַצֵּחַ עַל־אַיֶּלֶת הַשַּׁחַר מִזְמוֹר לְדָוִד), "To the choirmaster: according to *The Doe of the Dawn*. A Psalm of David." *Ayyelet ha-shachar* (אַיֶּלֶת הַשַּׁחַר) literally means "the doe of the dawn," and the most likely reading is that it names a melody — singers were instructed to set the psalm to a known tune, the way a hymnal indicates "tune: ST. ANNE." Nahum Sarna and John Goldingay both treat it this way. Older Jewish tradition occasionally read it allegorically (the doe = Israel, the dawn = redemption breaking), but the musical-rubric reading dominates contemporary scholarship.

Form-critically, Psalm 22 is the textbook example of an *individual lament* (Gunkel's *Klagelied des Einzelnen*) that resolves into a *thanksgiving song* (*Danklied*). Westermann observed that lament psalms typically follow a structure of address-complaint-petition-confession of trust-vow of praise; Psalm 22 contains all of these elements but in a uniquely intensified form, and the transition from complaint to praise (vv. 21–22) is so abrupt it has generated a literature of its own. Some scholars (notably Begrich) hypothesized a missing oracle of salvation between v. 21a and v. 21b, the priest's word that catalyzed the shift. Others (Goldingay, Hossfeld-Zenger) prefer to see the shift as inherent to the genre — lament does not resolve through evidence but through trust, and the psalm enacts the trust it confesses.

The cult-functional setting is most likely temple worship — an individual sufferer, brought before the sanctuary, prays and is heard, then offers thanksgiving in the *qahal gadol* (קָהָל גָּדוֹל, "great congregation," v. 25). The mention of paying vows (v. 25) and the shared meal (v. 26, "the afflicted shall eat and be satisfied") points to the *todah* (תּוֹדָה) thanksgiving offering ritualized in Leviticus 7. The psalm is liturgically anchored.

ANE parallels exist: Mesopotamian *shuilla* prayers and Egyptian penitential hymns share the lament-to-praise arc. What distinguishes Psalm 22 is the directness of accusation. The sufferer addresses God not with placating language but with *lama azavtani* (לָמָה עֲזַבְתָּנִי, "why have you forsaken me?") — a question demanding an answer, the prayer of a covenant partner who has the right to ask.

A famous textual issue lives in v. 16 (Hebrew v. 17). The Masoretic Text reads *ka'ari yadai ve-raglai* (כָּאֲרִי יָדַי וְרַגְלָי), "like a lion my hands and my feet" — a syntactically awkward phrase. The Septuagint has *ōryxan*, "they pierced/dug," presupposing a Hebrew *Vorlage* of *karu* (כָּרוּ) "they pierced," with a final *waw* rather than a *yod*. The Vulgate (*foderunt*) and the Peshitta agree with the LXX. Crucially, a Dead Sea Scroll from Naḥal Ḥever, **5/6ḤevPs** (a fragment of Psalms found in the Cave of Letters, sometimes labeled 4QPs-f in older literature), reads *karu* — confirming that the "pierced" reading was a real Hebrew variant in pre-Christian Judaism, not a Christian retrojection. Emanuel Tov, Hans-Joachim Kraus, and the contemporary critical-textual consensus treat *karu* as likely original; the MT's *ka'ari* is probably a scribal error (*yod* for *waw* are easily confused) or a deliberate alteration. The "pierced" reading is what the church has read for two millennia, and the manuscript evidence supports it.

Key Hebrew terms:

- *azavtani* (עֲזַבְתָּנִי, v. 1) — "you have forsaken me"; the verb *azav* implies abandonment of someone who had a claim on you
- *tola'at* (תּוֹלַעַת, v. 6) — "worm," a specific small grub used for crimson dye, possibly evoking shame and lowliness simultaneously
- *karu yadai ve-raglai* (כָּרוּ יָדַי וְרַגְלָי, v. 16) — "they pierced my hands and feet" (LXX/DSS reading)
- *qahal gadol* (קָהָל גָּדוֹל, v. 25) — "great congregation," the worshipping assembly
- *todah* (תּוֹדָה) implied by v. 25–26 — thanksgiving sacrifice

## Lens 2 — The Literature

Psalm 22 has a clear bipartite shape, and the parallelism in each half is some of the richest in the Psalter.

- **vv. 1–21**: Lament. Three movements of complaint each followed by a confession of trust — vv. 1–5 (forsakenness vs. the fathers' trust), vv. 6–11 (mockery vs. God-from-the-womb), vv. 12–21 (animal-imagery suffering vs. urgent petition).
- **vv. 22–31**: Thanksgiving and praise, expanding outward — from the personal vow (v. 22), to the congregation of Israel (vv. 23–26), to the nations (vv. 27–28), to the dead and the unborn (vv. 29–31).

Lowth's category of synonymous parallelism captures the surface; Robert Alter's "what's more" and Adele Berlin's *seconding* and James Kugel's "sharpening" capture more. V. 1's two lines — "Why have you forsaken me? Why are you so far from saving me?" — are not just repetition. The first is interior abandonment; the second is geographic distance and active rescue withheld. The progression is from felt absence to functional silence.

The animal imagery in vv. 12–21 is sustained and terrifying — bulls of Bashan (the lush region east of the Jordan, famous for cattle), ravening lions, dogs (a Near Eastern image of contemptible enemies, scavengers), wild oxen with horns. The body of the speaker becomes the inverse: poured out like water, bones out of joint, heart melted, strength dried like a potsherd, tongue stuck to jaws. The contrast — predator beasts vs. dissolving body — is form following content. The man being eaten alive describes himself in the language of his dissolution.

The structural pivot is at vv. 21b–22. The Hebrew of v. 21b is unusual: *anitani* (עֲנִיתָנִי, "you have answered me") in some manuscripts, or *aniyyah* (a noun) in others — the LXX reads "my lowliness" — but the MT and most modern translations have "you have rescued me." In any case, the lament *suddenly stops*. V. 22 begins the *todah*. There is no transition. The person who in v. 21 was being saved from the lion's mouth is in v. 22 telling of God's name to his brothers.

The closing arc (vv. 27–31) is one of the most expansive endings in the Psalter. The praise spreads centrifugally: from this congregation to all Israel to all the families of the nations to those going down to the dust to a people yet unborn. The final words *ki asah* (כִּי עָשָׂה, "for he has done it") are sometimes heard by Christian readers as resonant with John 19:30 (*tetelestai*, "it is finished") — though that link is theological-typological, not philological.

Westermann's analysis: the psalm is a praise-of-God-who-acts in narrative form, where the lament is included as the necessary backstory of the act. This is what makes the psalm a model for Christian-Jewish lament theology alike — praise without lament becomes saccharine, and lament without praise becomes despair.

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

### Book I placement

Psalm 22 sits in Book I of the Psalter, between Psalm 21's royal thanksgiving and Psalm 23's pastoral trust. The editorial sequence — royal triumph, individual desolation, pastoral confidence — has been read as a deliberate theological progression. Brueggemann calls Psalm 22 the "Friday" of the Psalter, with Psalm 23 as Sunday and Psalm 24 as the entrance into the temple. The Christian reception did not invent this sequence but found in it a resonance with the paschal triduum.

### Jewish Trajectory

Psalm 22 is preserved in multiple Dead Sea Scroll fragments. Beyond the textual significance of *karu* in 5/6ḤevPs, the psalm's prominence at Qumran indicates active liturgical use. *Midrash Tehillim* and the Talmud connect Ps 22 with Esther — *ayyelet ha-shachar* identified as Esther herself, the dawn-doe of deliverance, with the lament read as her prayer at the moment of crisis. This is a striking re-application: the suffering-individual psalm becomes the prayer of a Jewish woman in a hostile court.

Rashi reads the psalm as David's lament during persecution, projected forward to Israel's exilic suffering. Ibn Ezra holds to the Davidic-historical level. Radak hears messianic-eschatological notes without committing to the specifically Christian reading. Nachmanides preserves the corporate-Israel reading: the psalm is Israel's prayer in galut.

The Christological reading was the focus of Justin Martyr's *Dialogue with Trypho* in the second century — Justin appealed to Ps 22 as proof of Christian claims; Trypho responded that the psalm referred to David or to the Jewish people in exile. The disagreement is not over whether the psalm is messianic — both sides allowed messianic readings — but over which messiah and which suffering. Modern Jewish scholarship (Jon Levenson's *The Death and Resurrection of the Beloved Son*; Daniel Boyarin's *The Jewish Gospels*) has reopened this dialogue with new sophistication, taking seriously that early Christianity's reading of Ps 22 was *a* Jewish reading, not a non-Jewish imposition. Levenson and Boyarin disagree on much, but both insist that the partition between "Jewish" and "Christian" readings is post-hoc.

In synagogue practice, Ps 22 is recited on the Fast of Esther in some traditions, picking up the *Midrash Tehillim* connection. Its place in the daily liturgy is more modest.

### Christian Trajectory

Augustine's *Enarrationes* on Psalm 22 reads it whole-cloth as the prayer of Christ on the cross, every verse mapped to the Passion. Athanasius and Cassiodorus follow. Aquinas in his Psalms commentary holds the literal-Davidic and Christological readings together but treats the Christological as the deeper *sensus plenior*. Luther's *Operationes* (1519–1521) reads Ps 22 as the prayer of Christ-and-the-church, hearing in v. 1 not merely Christ's individual cry but the cry of every believer in extremity, joined to Christ. Calvin in 1557 emphasizes David's typological function — David's suffering as a real prefiguration of Christ's, not merely a verbal coincidence.

Modern critical commentary — Gunkel's foundational form-critical treatment, Mowinckel's cult-functional reading, Kraus's *Psalmen* commentary, Westermann's structural analysis, Hossfeld-Zenger, Goldingay's three-volume *Psalms*, Marvin Tate, Patrick Miller, Walter Brueggemann, William Brown, Beth Tanner — has largely set aside unmediated Christological reference and asked what the psalm meant in its first-millennium context, while taking the New Testament's use as a separate hermeneutical question. Richard Hays in *Reading Backwards* and *Echoes of Scripture in the Gospels* makes the case that the Gospel writers read Ps 22 backwards from the cross — finding in it not predictions but a script that the Passion enacted. Joel Marcus in his *Mark* commentary has argued especially carefully for Markan dependence on Ps 22 throughout the Passion narrative, not just in 15:34. Susan Gillingham's *Psalms Through the Centuries* traces the reception across two thousand years.

### NT Use

Psalm 22 is quoted or alluded to more in the Passion narratives than any other psalm:

- **22:1**: Mark 15:34 (*Eloi Eloi lema sabachthani*) and Matthew 27:46 (*Eli Eli lema sabachthani*) — Jesus's cry from the cross. The form is Aramaic in Mark, slightly Hebraicized (*Eli*) in Matthew. The bystanders' confusion ("he is calling Elijah," Mark 15:35) presupposes the *Eli* form, suggesting Matthew's version may preserve the original Hebraicized pronunciation. Whether Jesus prayed in Hebrew or Aramaic is an open historical-Jesus question; both forms are attested in first-century Palestinian Jewish piety.
- **22:7–8**: Matthew 27:39–43, Mark 15:29–32 — passersby wagging their heads, mocking "He trusts in God; let God deliver him."
- **22:18**: Matthew 27:35, Mark 15:24, Luke 23:34, John 19:24 — dividing his garments and casting lots. John 19:24 quotes the verse explicitly.
- **22:16**: "they have pierced my hands and feet" — the textual reading the church has applied to the crucifixion, supported by LXX, Vulgate, and the Naḥal Ḥever Hebrew fragment.
- **22:22**: Hebrews 2:12 — "I will tell of your name to my brothers; in the midst of the congregation I will sing your praise" — quoted as Christ's own words, identifying the praising voice with the risen Jesus.

The cumulative force is that the New Testament writers, especially Mark, used Psalm 22 as the script by which the Passion was narrated.

## Lens 4 — The Theological Intent

Two tensions deserve full attention.

**Tension 1: What does Jesus citing Ps 22:1 from the cross mean?** This is one of the most contested questions in New Testament theology, and the positions are not symmetrical.

1. **Full despair, full forsakenness** (some Reformed readings, classical Penal Substitution accounts): Jesus on the cross is genuinely abandoned by the Father; the psalm names a real divine forsakenness that occurs in the moment of atonement.

2. **The whole psalm is implied** (N.T. Wright, often Anglican-Catholic readings; recent essays by C.D. Stanley): citing the first verse of a psalm in first-century Jewish practice could invoke the entire psalm. Jesus, citing v. 1, intends vv. 22–31 — the vindication, the praise, the universal worship. The cry is not despair but faithful protest *anticipating* the resolution the psalm itself announces.

3. **Faithful protest with unresolved tension** (Raymond Brown's *Death of the Messiah*; many contemporary commentators): Jesus cites the psalm because it is the prayer of a covenant sufferer. The cry is real, the pain is real, the abandonment is felt — and the psalm itself does not resolve until v. 22. Jesus is praying through the psalm, not summarizing it. Brown's two-volume *Death of the Messiah* is the most thorough treatment.

*Steelman each*: Position 1 takes the cry seriously as cry — not as prooftexting. Position 2 takes seriously that Jesus knew the whole psalm and that first-century citation conventions often meant the whole. Position 3 refuses to resolve the cry too quickly; Friday is real before Sunday.

**Where this leaves us**: probably Position 3, with humility toward 1 and 2. The cry is real; the psalm's vindication is also real; the cry anticipates the vindication without erasing itself. The Christian tradition has often rushed past Friday. The psalm doesn't.

**Tension 2: The Christological-Christian reading vs. the Davidic-Jewish reading.** Modern Jewish-Christian dialogue has had to be honest about Ps 22. Christian readings have at times claimed the psalm as exclusively predicting Christ in a way that erases its Jewish context and Jewish co-claim. Jewish readers have at times reduced the psalm to David's biography in a way that ignores its messianic-eschatological reach within Jewish reception itself.

The honest position, advanced by Levenson, Boyarin, Hays, and others: the psalm is *a Jewish psalm* with messianic-eschatological resonance that *was* read messianically by some Jews before the Common Era and that early Christians (themselves Jewish) read as fulfilled in Jesus. The disagreement is not about whether the psalm is messianic but about which messiah, and that is a real disagreement that two traditions can hold without erasing each other.

**Closing question**: When the psalm pivots at v. 22 from forsakenness to praise, does the praise depend on the rescue having already occurred — or can you praise from inside the lion's mouth?

## The Thread

- **Mark 15:34 and Matthew 27:46**: Jesus citing Ps 22:1 from the cross — the New Testament's most explicit invocation of this psalm.
- **Hebrews 2:12**: Christ as the speaker of Ps 22:22, identifying himself with the one whose praise rises from the great congregation.
- **John 19:24**: explicit citation of Ps 22:18 (dividing garments, casting lots) as fulfillment.
- **Psalm 23**: the immediate sequel in the Psalter — the same speaker who was forsaken in 22 now walks through the valley of the shadow with the shepherd. Read 22 and 23 together to feel the editor's hand.
