# Exodus 14: Walls of Water

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

The political and chronological horizon of Exodus 14 almost certainly belongs to the late Ramesside period of the Egyptian New Kingdom, most plausibly the thirteenth century BCE. Kenneth Kitchen, whose work in *On the Reliability of the Old Testament* remains the most sustained Egyptological defense of a thirteenth-century exodus, points to the store-cities of Pithom and Raamses (Ex 1:11) as bearing Ramesside names — Pi-Ramesses was Ramesses II's delta capital, abandoned by the mid-twelfth century, making a post-Ramesside memory unlikely. James Hoffmeier's *Israel in Egypt* and *Ancient Israel in Sinai* reinforce this from the side of Egyptian military geography: the Migdol-and-sea string of toponyms fits the eastern frontier defense system attested in Papyrus Anastasi III and the Karnak war reliefs. Bryant Wood has argued for an earlier fifteenth-century exodus based on the 480 years of 1 Kings 6:1; most Egyptologists resist this because the archaeological landscape of the Late Bronze Age IIA does not fit the picture of collapse the biblical narrative implies. The Merneptah Stele (c. 1208 BCE), which names "Israel" as a people (not a land) in Canaan, functions as a terminus ante quem: by the very end of the thirteenth century an entity called Israel exists in the Levant.

The material culture of the chapter is the eastern frontier of the Delta and the marshy corridor connecting it to the Sinai land bridge. Pi-Hahiroth, Migdol, and Baal-Zephon are listed in verse 2 as if the audience knew them. Each has been tentatively located. Migdol (Hebrew *migdōl*, "tower, fortress") appears in Egyptian texts as a string of frontier watchtowers; one candidate is Tell el-Borg, excavated by Hoffmeier. Baal-Zephon ("Lord of the North") is the Canaanite storm god whose cult is attested at Ras Shamra and in a Phoenician letter from Saqqara; a shrine to him near the frontier would have served Semitic-speaking mercenaries and traders. Pi-Hahiroth ("mouth of the canals") points to the network of waterways through the marsh.

The geography of the crossing turns on *yam sûp* (יַם סוּף), literally "sea of reeds" or "sea of ends/extremity." The Septuagint translators, working in Alexandria, rendered it *erythra thalassa*, "Red Sea," and this gave the Latin tradition its name. But *sûp* almost certainly derives from Egyptian *twf(y)*, "papyrus," and in most Hebrew Bible occurrences *yam sûp* designates the Gulf of Aqaba or a broader category of waters east of Egypt. The main modern options:

- The Gulf of Suez (traditional southern route).
- The Bitter Lakes region, the lake system north of Suez.
- Lake Ballah or Lake Menzaleh, shallow papyrus marshes in the eastern Delta (Hoffmeier's preferred zone).
- The Gulf of Aqaba (the modern "Jabal al-Lawz" hypothesis; archaeologically thin).

I name the options without picking a side. Hoffmeier's papyrus-marsh reading fits the philology of *sûp* and the military-geography logic; Kitchen accepts a more southerly route through the Bitter Lakes. What the biblical author means by *yam sûp* in Exodus 15 versus 1 Kings 9:26 (Solomon's port at Ezion-Geber on Aqaba) is not necessarily the same body of water.

Authorship and dating follow the classical test case for source criticism. Richard Friedman's *The Bible with Sources Revealed* walks Exodus 14 verse by verse as the textbook example of J and P interwoven. The J strand emphasizes the east wind *rûaḥ qādîm ʿazzâ* drying the sea overnight (v. 21a) and the Egyptians fleeing into it; the P strand emphasizes Moses' stretched-out staff, the walls of water on right and left (vv. 22, 29), and the formulaic "know that I am YHWH" (vv. 4, 18). E elements (Elohim, the angel of God in v. 19a) complicate the binary. William Propp in the Anchor Bible volume is somewhat more cautious about the source divisions but agrees the narrative is composite. Umberto Cassuto, writing from a holistic literary standpoint, reads the doublets as deliberate amplification. The doubled actions — sea divided by staff, sea driven back by wind; Egyptians thrown into panic, Egyptians drowned — are either two strands braided or one narrator's rhetoric of superabundance, depending on one's hermeneutic.

Archaeologically, the Merneptah Stele, the Beni Hasan tomb painting (Middle Kingdom, showing a Semitic caravan of 37 entering Egypt — a window on pastoralist traffic rather than the exodus itself), and the Serabit el-Khadim turquoise mines with their Proto-Sinaitic inscriptions (early alphabetic script used by Semitic laborers under Egyptian supervision) frame what we know of the Late Bronze movement of Northwest Semitic populations across the frontier. The Ugaritic Baal Cycle — in which Baal defeats Yam (Sea) and is enthroned — is the mythic substrate against which Israel's narrative of YHWH defeating the sea and its chariots reads as both continuous and polemically transformed.

Key Hebrew terms:

- *yam sûp* (יַם סוּף) — sea of reeds/end.
- *rûaḥ qādîm ʿazzâ* (רוּחַ קָדִים עַזָּה) — strong east wind (v. 21).
- *ḥōmâ* (חוֹמָה) — wall (v. 22), standard noun for a fortification wall.
- *yĕšûʿat YHWH* (יְשׁוּעַת יְהוָה) — salvation of YHWH (v. 13).
- *wayĕḥazzēq* (וַיְחַזֵּק) — "and he hardened" (v. 8), Piel of *ḥzq*, the P vocabulary for Pharaoh's hardening, distinct from the J/E *kābēd* and *qāšâ* elsewhere in the plague cycle.

## Lens 2 — The Literature

Genre: prose narrative with composite strata. The chapter is neither annal nor myth in the strict sense; it is theological historiography organized around the rhythm of a deliverance oracle (vv. 13–14), a divine command (vv. 15–18), the execution (vv. 21–29), and a concluding faith-formula (v. 31). The crossing sequence is bracketed by inclusio: verse 13's "the salvation of the LORD" and verse 30's "the LORD saved Israel."

Literary devices are dense. The pillar of cloud and fire, which has gone *before* Israel since chapter 13, now moves *behind* them (vv. 19–20), a single sentence with enormous spatial weight: the column that led becomes the column that shields. The rhetorical doubling of "on their right hand and on their left" (vv. 22, 29) forms a bracket around the Egyptian pursuit in verses 23–28. The verb *yāṣāʾ* ("go out"), the keyword of the exodus, is echoed in reverse by the Egyptians' pursuit *after* Israel. The clogged chariot wheels of verse 25 — the Hebrew reads *wayĕsar ʾēt ʾōpan markĕbōtāyw* ("he removed/turned aside the wheels of his chariots"); the Samaritan and LXX read *wayeʾĕsōr* ("he bound") — either way the chariot, Egypt's ultimate military technology, is the thing that fails. Robert Alter notes in his translation that the narrative's irony is precise: the instrument of pursuit becomes the instrument of drowning.

The hardening of Pharaoh's heart threads through the plague cycle, and here in 14:4, 8, 17 it reaches its final iteration with *wayĕḥazzēq* — God actively hardens, but the narrative has already shown Pharaoh hardening his own heart through the early plagues. This dual agency is one of the interpretive knots Lens 4 takes up.

Textual variants: the LXX of Exodus 14 generally tracks the Masoretic Text closely but softens theological difficulties (v. 25's "clogging" is rendered more smoothly). The Samaritan Pentateuch harmonizes some repetition. Qumran fragments of Exodus (4QExodc, 4QpaleoExodm) preserve portions but the Song of the Sea fragment at 4QpaleoExodm is more significant for chapter 15 than 14.

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

Exodus 14 is the hinge of the Torah. Everything before builds toward it: the patriarchal promises, the descent into Egypt, the plagues. Everything after proceeds from it: Sinai, tabernacle, wilderness, conquest. Jon Levenson in *Sinai and Zion* treats it as the originating act from which Israel's cosmology of covenant radiates.

**Jewish Interpretive Trajectory.** In the Second Temple period, Josephus (*Antiquities* 2.338–349) rationalizes the crossing through Strabo-style natural philosophy: the wind, the tides, the analogy of Alexander crossing the Pamphylian sea — while insisting on divine providence as ultimate cause. Philo (*Life of Moses* 1.175–180) reads allegorically: the sea is the stream of passions, Egypt the body, the crossing the soul's ascent. Wisdom of Solomon 10:18–19 personifies Wisdom as Israel's guide through deep waters. Pseudo-Philo's *Liber Antiquitatum Biblicarum* 10 expands the narrative with dialogue among the tribes at the shore. The Mekhilta de-Rabbi Ishmael, Tractate Beshallach, is the classical rabbinic treatment: the famous dispute between Rabbi Meir and Rabbi Judah (and elsewhere between Rabbi Akiva and Rabbi Eliezer) over whether the sea split because of Israel's faith, because of the merit of the ancestors, or for the sake of Moses' rod. Bavli Sotah 37a preserves the beloved Nachshon ben Aminadav legend: as the tribes argued about who would enter first, Nachshon of Judah leaped into the water up to his nostrils, and only then did the sea split — a haggadic reading that emphasizes human initiative as catalyst to divine action.

Medieval: Rashi follows the Mekhilta closely, often citing it phrase by phrase; his reading of verse 14 ("the LORD will fight for you, and you have only to be silent") stresses the paradox of active passivity. Ibn Ezra is more linguistic and realistic, noting the east wind as a natural means. Ramban (Nachmanides) insists on the miracle's openness — there was natural means, but no natural explanation would suffice without divine timing.

Modern Jewish scholarship: Nahum Sarna's *JPS Commentary* surveys the Egyptian and wilderness geography evidence. Jon Levenson treats the crossing as cosmogonic repetition — the victory over the sea is a recapitulation of YHWH's primordial victory over the waters of Genesis 1. Ilana Pardes in *The Biography of Ancient Israel* reads the crossing as Israel's birth narrative (waters parting, nation emerging). Avivah Zornberg's *The Particulars of Rapture* reads it psychoanalytically: Israel's terror at the shore is the necessary precondition for seeing. James Kugel in *How to Read the Bible* steps back and describes how the crossing has accumulated interpretive layers without displacing the earlier ones.

**Christian Interpretive Trajectory.** Paul makes the typology explicit in 1 Corinthians 10:1–2: "our fathers were all under the cloud, and all passed through the sea, and all were baptized into Moses in the cloud and in the sea." This single verse structures the entire patristic tradition. Origen's *Homilies on Exodus* 5 reads the Red Sea as the water of baptism drowning the old man. Ambrose, in *On the Sacraments* and *On the Mysteries*, builds his catechesis of the baptismal liturgy on it: the candidate is asked "Do you believe?" and then crosses the water. Augustine's tractates and Enarrations on Psalms return to it repeatedly. Gregory of Nyssa's *Life of Moses* makes the crossing the second stage of the soul's ascent — after the burning bush's illumination, the sea's purgation.

Medieval: Aquinas (*Summa Theologiae* III q. 46) treats the crossing as one of the principal Old Testament types of Christ's passion. Reformation: Luther in the Lenten sermons preaches it as the sacrament of baptism and the death of the old Adam. Calvin's commentary is more restrained on typology but insists on its fulfillment in Christ.

Critical scholarship: Martin Noth treated the crossing as a cultic legend attached to a particular tribal group and only later universalized. Brevard Childs's *The Book of Exodus* reads the composite narrative canonically, insisting that whatever the pre-history, the final form is what speaks. Frank Moore Cross in *Canaanite Myth and Hebrew Epic* argues that the exodus tradition transforms the Canaanite combat myth of god vs. sea into a historical-covenantal idiom. Walter Brueggemann emphasizes the political dimension — the crossing is a counter-imperial act, the destruction of Pharaoh's chariot economy. Terence Fretheim in his Exodus commentary reads the natural and the supernatural as complementary rather than competing. William Propp's Anchor Bible volume is the standard philological reference.

**Convergence and divergence.** Jewish tradition reads the crossing as a formation-of-the-nation event with ongoing liturgical reappropriation: the Song of the Sea is recited daily in *shacharit*. Christian tradition reads it typologically — baptism, the death of the old self, the Church passing through the waters. Paul in 1 Corinthians 10 makes this explicit, and the patristic tradition extends it systematically. Levenson and other modern Jewish scholars have pushed back against supersessionist readings that treat the crossing as merely a shadow of something else; both traditions can hold that the event is theologically generative in its own right without requiring the other's framework. Critical scholarship has reopened older questions about the archaic core of the tradition (Cross) and about how much history lies beneath the theological narrative (Childs's distinction between event and testimony).

## Lens 4 — The Theological Intent

**Tension 1: Divine hardening vs. human responsibility.** The chapter states flatly in verse 4 that YHWH will harden Pharaoh's heart, and in verse 8 that YHWH did. Is Pharaoh a free agent or a puppet?

- Catholic (CCC 600, 601, 2012; Aquinas): Aquinas distinguishes God's antecedent permission from his consequent will. God "hardens" by withdrawing grace, not by creating wickedness *ex nihilo* in Pharaoh. Ratzinger in *Jesus of Nazareth* reads the hardening as God's respect for the will already set against him.
- Reformed (Calvin, *Institutes* 2.4; Louis Berkhof): Calvin reads the hardening as genuinely God's act, part of a decree that uses Pharaoh's rebellion for the manifestation of divine glory. Geerhardus Vos's *Biblical Theology* places the hardening within the typology of divine judgment on imperial power.
- Lutheran (Luther, *Bondage of the Will*; Gustaf Wingren *Creation and Law*): Luther pushes the hardest — God is the hidden cause even of hardening, though Pharaoh is guilty because his will genuinely consents. Wingren locates this within creation and law rather than redemption.
- Wesleyan/Arminian: Pharaoh hardened his own heart first (chapters 7–10 use *kābēd* and *qāšâ* of Pharaoh's own action); God's hardening in chapter 14 is a ratification of the direction Pharaoh has already chosen.
- Orthodox (John Chrysostom, Homilies on Romans 16; Schmemann): divine hardening as pedagogy, not tyranny. God's action on Pharaoh reveals rather than creates what was already there.
- Jewish (Rashi on Exodus 7:3; Maimonides *Guide* III.32, *Hilchot Teshuvah* 6.3): Maimonides argues that Pharaoh forfeited his capacity for repentance through repeated rejection — the hardening is punishment-as-consequence.
- Critical: most modern critical readers describe the hardening as an ancient Near Eastern theological convention — the total sovereignty of the patron deity over the foreign king — without treating it as a metaphysical puzzle to be solved.

The least intuitive position, worth steelmanning, is Luther's. Luther refuses to split the hardening between "permission" and "causation"; he insists that when Scripture says God hardens, God hardens, and that the comfort of the gospel depends on not flinching from that. The move preserves the absolute gratuity of salvation in 14:13 ("stand firm and see the salvation of the LORD") — if God's action on Pharaoh were merely permissive, God's action on Israel at the same sea could also be merely permissive, and Israel's deliverance would ultimately rest on Israel's merit. Luther's position keeps the deliverance entirely as gift.

**Where this leaves us:** The text gives agency to both Pharaoh and God without a systematic reconciliation; the traditions diverge on which agency to foreground and why, but each answers to a different pastoral danger — triumphalism on one side, fatalism on the other.

**Tension 2: "Stand firm and see" vs. "go forward."** In verse 14 Moses tells Israel they have only to be silent. In verse 15 YHWH rebukes Moses: "Why do you cry to me? Tell the people to go forward." Is salvation passive reception or active movement?

- Catholic (CCC 2007, 2008 on merit; Aquinas *ST* I-II q. 111): cooperation with grace — Israel is commanded to walk, and the walking is itself made possible by the prior divine action.
- Reformed (Calvin on Exodus 14; Horton *The Christian Faith*): *sola gratia* dominant. Israel's walking is response, not cause. Michael Horton's covenant theology reads verses 13–15 as law-gospel dialectic.
- Lutheran (Luther; Bonhoeffer *Discipleship*): the call to go forward is the call of obedience that follows, not precedes, the promise of deliverance.
- Wesleyan/Arminian (Wesley's Notes): synergy — grace enables, but the walking is genuinely Israel's.
- Orthodox (Gregory of Nyssa *Life of Moses* 2.123–129): the two commands correspond to two stages of the soul's life — first stillness (*hesychia*), then motion into the cloud.
- Jewish (Mekhilta; Rashi; Nachshon ben Aminadav tradition, Sotah 37a): the sea split only when Nachshon stepped in. Human initiative is the precondition, though not the cause, of divine action.
- Critical (Brueggemann *The Prophetic Imagination*): the movement from "stand still" to "go forward" is the movement from imperial paralysis to liberated action — a political theology of resistance.

The Nachshon tradition is the least intuitive for a Protestant ear: it reads the text as if human boldness triggers the miracle. Steelmanned, it is not works-righteousness but an insistence that revelation meets the one who moves toward it; the sea is not a stage effect performed for a passive audience.

**Where this leaves us:** "Stand still" and "go forward" are not contradictory but sequential. The first dismantles Israel's reflex to save themselves by their own means (returning to Egypt); the second commands the walking that is already response to promise.

*Unresolved question:* If the pillar that led is the same pillar that shielded — if the same God who commanded the pursuit also caused the drowning — what exactly is the theological status of Egyptian death in verse 30 ("Israel saw the Egyptians dead on the seashore")? The text does not grieve, but the rabbinic tradition (Megillah 10b) has God rebuking the angels: "My handiwork is drowning in the sea, and you want to sing?" The narrative's silence and the tradition's discomfort open a question the chapter itself will not close.

## The Thread

- **Genesis 1:2 and the *rûaḥ* over the waters.** The same *rûaḥ* that hovered over the primordial deep now drives back the sea. Creation and exodus are structurally linked: cosmos emerges from divided waters; Israel emerges from divided waters. Levenson's *Creation and the Persistence of Evil* makes this connection load-bearing.
- **Joshua 3–4, the Jordan crossing.** Deliberate echo — priests stand in the water, waters stand in a heap (*nēd*, the same rare word as Exodus 15:8), the people cross on dry ground. The exodus generation's crossing *out* of Egypt is rhymed by the conquest generation's crossing *into* the land. The motif is repurposed for initiation, not escape.
- **2 Kings 2:8, 14, Elijah and Elisha.** Elijah strikes the Jordan with his mantle; the waters part. Elisha does the same. The sign of prophetic succession is recapitulation of the sea crossing — the prophetic office is attested by the ability to divide waters.
- **Revelation 15:2–3, the "sea of glass mingled with fire" and the song of Moses and of the Lamb.** The apocalyptic tradition places the victors at the end of history standing by a transfigured sea, singing the same song Exodus 15 will sing. The crossing is not only remembered; it is projected as the shape of final deliverance.
