# Numbers 5: Water of Bitterness

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

Numbers 5 is still set at Sinai in the second year after the Exodus — part of the book *Bĕmidbar* ("in the wilderness"), spanning Numbers 1:1 to 10:10 before Israel's departure. The chapter contains three distinct legal units: expulsion of the ritually impure from the camp (vv. 1–4), restitution for wrongs (vv. 5–10), and the *sōṭāh* ordeal, the ritual for a wife suspected of adultery (vv. 11–31). The three units are held together by a concern for the camp's purity: that which would defile it must be removed, made right, or tested. Politically, this is the pre-monarchic camp-theocracy of Numbers 1–4 continued. The tabernacle is at the center, the Levites encircle it, the tribes encircle the Levites, and now the chapter specifies who must be placed outside the outer ring: those with *ṣāraʿat* (skin disease traditionally rendered "leprosy," though it is not Hansen's disease), those with genital discharges, and those polluted by contact with the dead.

The material culture of the chapter is strikingly vivid. The *sōṭāh* ritual prescribes a specific liturgical apparatus: an earthenware vessel of *mayim qĕdōšîm* ("holy water," v. 17), dust from the tabernacle floor, a tenth of an ephah of barley flour (no oil, no frankincense — v. 15), a scroll on which the priest writes curses, and *mê hammārîm hamĕʾārĕrîm* ("the water of bitterness that brings the curse," v. 18) into which the written curses are washed. The woman's hair is unbound, she holds the grain offering, and she drinks the inscribed water after swearing "Amen, Amen" (v. 22). This is the only ritual ordeal in the Torah and arguably the most distinctive single rite in the Hebrew Bible.

The ANE parallels are dense and important. The Code of Hammurabi §§131–132 prescribes two different procedures for suspected wifely adultery: if the husband accuses without witnesses, she swears an oath (§131); if a neighbor accuses, she is subjected to a river ordeal — thrown in to see if she sinks or floats (§132). The Middle Assyrian Laws (MAL A §§17, 22–24) include river ordeal provisions. Mesopotamian trial texts describe *šuʾila* or *id* rituals involving the river-god, with the accused either surviving (innocent) or drowning (guilty). Nuzi and Mari texts reference oath-ordeals. Tikva Frymer-Kensky (*In the Wake of the Goddesses*, 1992, and "The Strange Case of the Suspected Sotah," *Vetus Testamentum* 34, 1984) demonstrated in detail both the ANE context and the distinctive features of the Israelite rite. Michael Fishbane (*Biblical Interpretation in Ancient Israel*, 1985, pp. 121–129) traced the scribal and legal background. The comparison is illuminating. Unlike Hammurabi's ordeal, the *sōṭāh* is non-lethal on its face: the woman drinks water (not a river) and survives the drinking. The ordeal's outcome (uncertain, delayed, embodied — "thigh falls away, belly swells") is left to divine action rather than to water's physics. Unlike the river ordeal, the Israelite rite is priestly, not judicial; it takes place in the sanctuary, not the river.

The most singular feature: the ritual requires the priest to write curses containing the divine name on a scroll and then dissolve the writing into the water. This is the only case in the Torah where the divine name is *intentionally erased*. The rabbinic tradition (Bavli Sotah 17a) derived from this a remarkable principle: "So great is peace between husband and wife that the Holy One, blessed be He, says, 'Let my name that is written in holiness be erased in the water to restore peace.'" This is a theological datum of the first order and unique in the ANE.

Authorship and dating: Wellhausen (*Prolegomena*, 1883) placed the chapter in P, though its archaic elements troubled later source critics. Milgrom (*JPS Numbers*, 1990), Levine (*Numbers 1–20*, AB, 1993), and Frymer-Kensky all argued that the *sōṭāh* ritual preserves pre-exilic material — the rite is too strange, too bodily, and too close to ANE analogues to be a Second Temple invention. Wenham (*Numbers*, Tyndale, 1981), Cole (*NAC*, 2000), and Budd (*WBC*, 1984) concur. Judith Hauptman (*Rereading the Rabbis*, 1998) and Michael Satlow have explored the rabbinic reception. Noth (*Numbers*, 1968) treated it as old material embedded in a priestly framework.

Hebrew terms worth pausing on:

- *sōṭāh* (סוֹטָה), "the woman who goes astray" — not used as a noun in the Torah text but derived from the verb *sāṭāh* ("turn aside," v. 12), becomes the rabbinic name of the rite and the namesake tractate of the Mishnah.
- *qinʾāh* (קִנְאָה), "jealousy" (vv. 14, 15, 18, 25, 29, 30), the same word used for God's "jealousy" over Israel in Exodus 20:5 — the emotional category is theologically freighted.
- *mê hammārîm hamĕʾārĕrîm* (מֵי הַמָּרִים הַמְאָרְרִים), "water of bitterness that brings the curse" (v. 18), a triple alliteration/assonance in Hebrew that marks the ritual's climactic moment.
- *ṣāraʿat* (צָרַעַת), "skin disease" (v. 2), conventionally "leprosy," actually a range of flaking skin conditions. The boundary of the camp is defined by its exclusion.
- *māʿal* (מָעַל), "break faith" (v. 6, 12, 27), the same verb used of sacrilege against holy things (Lev 5:15). The wife's alleged adultery is framed as *māʿal* against her husband, paralleling sacrilege against God.

## Lens 2 — The Literature

Numbers 5 braids three genres: priestly law of purity (the exclusion of impure persons), priestly civil law (restitution), and ritual ordeal (the *sōṭāh*). The three are held together thematically: each concerns the camp's integrity in the presence of a God who dwells "in the midst" (v. 3). The most arresting literary feature is the interruption of the censuses and transport logistics of chapters 1–4 with this swerve into purity and marital law. Mary Douglas (*In the Wilderness*, 1993) read this as theologically intentional: the camp's geometry is incomplete without the rules for its inner life. The tabernacle can be moved, the Levites can be numbered, but the holiness that justifies it all requires ongoing protective rites.

The *sōṭāh* pericope (vv. 11–31) is structurally elaborate. It opens with the juridical situation (vv. 12–14): a husband's jealousy, no witnesses, either true suspicion or false. It proceeds through the preparation (v. 15), the ritual setting (vv. 16–18), the oath formula (vv. 19–22), the written curses and their dissolution (vv. 23–24), the grain offering (vv. 25–26), the drinking and its consequences (vv. 27–28), and the summary formula (vv. 29–31). The double oath — "If no man has lain with you … be free" / "But if you have gone astray … may this water" — creates a ritual bifurcation with only two possible outcomes: vindication (with conception promised, v. 28) or curse (thigh falls, belly swells).

Literary devices cluster in the oath and the water. The alliterative *mê hammārîm hamĕʾārĕrîm* is a signature phrase. The scroll of curses is *written to be erased*, an anti-scribal inscription, a text whose theological work is done precisely by its dissolution. Nili Sacher Fox and Michael Fishbane both observed that the writing-and-erasure structure is a ritual transposition of the oath-swearing common in ANE legal texts, but with a theological radicalism: the divine name itself is subject to dissolution for the sake of peace.

The chapter's treatment of the woman is literarily pointed. She is led forward, her hair unbound (v. 18 — a gesture variously read as shaming, as preparation, or as loosening her from her prior status), given the grain offering to hold, and made to swear. The husband's role is minimal after the accusation; the priest and the woman occupy the ritual center. The text's gaze is on her body: "may this water make your womb swell and your thigh fall away" (v. 22). Judith Hauptman (*Rereading the Rabbis*, 1998) and Tikva Frymer-Kensky both drew attention to the literary asymmetry — a husband's jealousy puts a woman's body on display — while also arguing that the ritual's specific shape contains and regulates what would otherwise be uncontrolled accusation.

Textual transmission shows moderate variation. 4QNum-b (4Q27) preserves parts of Numbers 5 with some Samaritan-like expansions; Eugene Ulrich's work identifies several variant readings in the oath formula. The LXX translates *mê hammārîm* straightforwardly but differs in some details of the curse. Judith Sanderson's broader work on pre-Masoretic Hebrew manuscripts places the chapter among the relatively stable sections of Numbers, with variation at the margins rather than the core.

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

### Jewish Interpretive Trajectory

The *sōṭāh* ritual received more sustained rabbinic attention than almost any other single passage in Numbers. The Mishnah tractate *Sotah* (nine chapters) and its Babylonian Talmud expansion (Bavli Sotah 2a–49b) comprise hundreds of pages on the rite — remarkably, since by the time the Mishnah was compiled (c. 200 CE) the ritual had been formally abolished for over a century. Mishnah Sotah 9:9 records: "When adulterers multiplied, the rite of the bitter water was abolished, and Yohanan ben Zakkai abolished it." The abolition, occurring sometime after 70 CE, preserved the ritual's memory while ending its practice, and the rabbis' continuing attention to it is a striking case of theological preservation through legal archive.

Bavli Sotah 2a opens with a principle that governs the tractate: the *sōṭāh* rite operates only when the husband's jealousy has proper basis and the woman has received a warning. The rabbinic reframing installs procedural protections around the ritual that reduce its frequency to near-zero in practice. Mishnah Sotah 1:1–4 stipulates warnings before witnesses; 3:4–5 limits who may serve; 4:1–5 exempts many categories of women; 9:9's abolition seals the matter. Bavli Sotah 17a produces the extraordinary theological teaching about the divine name being erased for the sake of marital peace. Bavli Sotah 36a connects the dust-from-the-floor element to Adam's creation from earth: the woman is tested by the same material from which humanity was formed. Rashi, on verse 15, glosses the absence of oil and frankincense: the grain offering is one of memory and shame, not of joy — a reading developed extensively in medieval commentary.

Maimonides codified the abolished ritual in *Mishneh Torah*, *Hilchot Sotah* ("Laws of the Suspected Adulteress"), preserving it as theoretical Jewish law even while acknowledging its suspension. Ramban (Nachmanides) treated the ordeal as a miraculous exception: God directly intervenes in the woman's body to reveal truth, a divine accommodation to human inability to know private sin. Ibn Ezra focused on the ritual's legal and procedural features. Sforno read the rite as pedagogical — teaching the community the seriousness of marital fidelity even when judicial proof is impossible. Bekhor Shor and Radak developed the moral-typological readings.

Modern Jewish commentary has engaged the ritual with unusual directness. Nehama Leibowitz (*Studies in Bamidbar*, 1980s) worked through the medieval commentators and offered her own reading: the ritual's strangeness is the point — it marks the limits of ordinary human knowledge and refers suspicion to God. Jonathan Sacks (*Covenant and Conversation: Numbers*, 2017) treated the rite as a sacred containment of male jealousy, protecting women from mob violence by displacing private suspicion into a public priestly frame. Aviva Zornberg (*Bewilderments*, 2015) read the rite psychologically and literarily, emphasizing the woman's bodily centrality and the ritual's bidirectional possibility — vindication (conception) or curse. Tikva Frymer-Kensky's extensive work on the rite — in *In the Wake of the Goddesses* (1992), "The Strange Case" essay, and *Reading the Women of the Bible* (2002) — holds together historical analysis, feminist critique, and theological engagement.

### Christian Interpretive Trajectory

Patristic engagement with Numbers 5 was less intensive than with the encampment chapters, but not absent. Origen's *Homilies on Numbers* 9 treats the chapter. On the expulsion of the unclean (vv. 1–4), Origen reads the three categories (skin disease, discharge, contact with the dead) as figures of three kinds of spiritual sin — pride, lust, and the corruption of dead works — which the Church must put outside its communion through excommunication. On the *sōṭāh* (vv. 11–31), Origen allegorizes the ritual as the soul's testing by doctrine: the bitter water is the divine word that tastes bitter to the false believer and sweet to the true. This allegorical reading became standard in patristic commentary; Cyril of Alexandria (*Glaphyra on Numbers*) develops it further. Augustine, in *Questions on the Heptateuch* 4, treated the ritual more soberly as a figure of the final judgment, where hidden sin is disclosed.

Medieval commentary followed Origen's allegorical track. Hugh of St. Victor and Rupert of Deutz read the ritual as a figure of the sacrament of penance, where concealed sin is exposed. Aquinas (*Summa Theologiae* I-II q. 102 a. 5) addressed the ordeal specifically as a ceremonial precept fulfilled in Christ: the bitter water is the chalice of judgment, the cursed woman is the soul condemned, the vindicated woman is the soul justified. This typological reading provided a framework for Catholic engagement with the ritual's strangeness.

The Reformation largely ignored the ritual or treated it briefly. Calvin (*Commentary on the Four Last Books of Moses*, 1563) treated the *sōṭāh* with notable reserve, acknowledging its ANE background and treating it as an accommodation to Israel's hardness of heart, a ritual containment of marital jealousy that was provisional and is now transcended. Luther, in scattered remarks, treated it as a figure of divine judgment.

Modern Christian scholarship has done more with the chapter in the past half-century than the previous eighteen centuries combined. Brueggemann (*Theology of the Old Testament*, 1997) briefly. Fretheim (*The Pentateuch*, 1996). Wenham (*Numbers*, Tyndale, 1981) read the ritual as a humane restraint on male violence. Cole (*NAC*, 2000) and Budd (*WBC*, 1984) treated the historical and theological questions. Ephraim Radner (*Brazos Numbers*, 2019) offered a sustained theological engagement, reading the ritual as a sober meditation on how private sin becomes visible. Dozeman (*Eerdmans*, forthcoming) emphasizes its ANE context and feminist critique.

### Where They Converge and Diverge

Jewish and Christian traditions converge in finding the ritual strange, serious, and theologically freighted. They diverge on its handling. Jewish tradition preserved the ritual textually and legally, developed extensive procedural restraints that drove its frequency to near-zero, and ultimately abolished its practice while retaining its memory — a sophisticated theological archivism. Christian tradition largely allegorized it, reading it as a type of sacramental penance, eschatological judgment, or the soul's trial by doctrine, with the ritual itself treated as superseded. Contemporary feminist critique — Frymer-Kensky, Hauptman, Sakenfeld — presses both traditions: the ritual's gendered asymmetry (only women are tested, only male jealousy triggers it) is not a contingent feature but a structural one. Defensive readings from Milgrom, Douglas, and Sacks argue that the ritual protects women from mob violence by displacing private suspicion into priestly process; critics like Alice Bach and Athalya Brenner respond that protective readings can still be patriarchal readings.

## Lens 4 — The Theological Intent

**Tension 1: Does the *sōṭāh* ritual protect women from worse violence, or enact a patriarchal violence of its own?**

- *Catholic*: The CCC treats the Old Testament's moral instruction as preparatory. Aquinas (*ST* II-II q. 95 a. 8 on superstition and divination; q. 154 on adultery) read the ordeal as fulfilled and abolished in Christ but as revealing enduring truth: adultery is a *māʿal* against both spouse and God, and its concealment is itself a spiritual wound.
- *Reformed*: Calvin treated the ritual as an accommodation to Israel's hardness, fulfilled in the judgment of the cross. The regulative principle rules out contemporary ordeal; the ethical teaching — the seriousness of marital fidelity and the limits of human knowledge of hidden sin — survives the rite's abolition.
- *Lutheran*: Luther treated the ritual as old-covenant judgment-under-law, displaced by the gospel's direct address. The ethical point persists; the ritual does not.
- *Orthodox*: The ordeal is read typologically as a figure of the eucharistic chalice — the cup that is life to the faithful and judgment to the unworthy (1 Cor 11:29). The continuity is theological, not procedural.
- *Jewish*: Rashi, Ramban, Maimonides all preserved the ritual's legal and theological coherence while recognizing its effective abolition by Yohanan ben Zakkai. Jonathan Sacks reads the rite as protective — containing male jealousy in a priestly frame to prevent mob violence. Nehama Leibowitz emphasizes its pedagogical function. Aviva Zornberg focuses on the woman's embodied centrality.
- *Feminist*: Tikva Frymer-Kensky offers perhaps the most careful reading: the rite is protective *and* patriarchal. It assumes the woman's body is the proper site of truth-disclosure; it gives the husband disproportionate power to initiate; it exempts the husband from comparable testing. But within the ANE context of river-ordeals and mob violence, it represents a significant moderation. Judith Hauptman and Michael Fishbane concur.
- *Critical*: Milgrom and Douglas read the rite as a ritual mechanism for containing otherwise destructive social forces. The ordeal's outcome is indeterminate and delayed, which Milgrom reads as a feature: the ritual is designed to defuse the husband's jealousy by displacing it into a process he does not control.

The steelman for the least intuitive position — the feminist critique that the ritual enacts a patriarchal violence even in its protective mode — is this. The rite assumes an asymmetric economy: male jealousy triggers it, female bodies are tested, the husband "shall be free from iniquity, but the woman shall bear her iniquity" (v. 31). That last verse alone makes clear that the ritual takes the husband's accusation as cost-free and the woman's defense as costly. Even if the ritual is a moderation of something worse, it encodes a hierarchy that cannot be rationalized into equity. Frymer-Kensky's move — to hold protection and patriarchy together as both true — is analytically honest and resists the temptation to either condemn or defend.

**Where this leaves us**: The ritual's protective function and its patriarchal structure are both real; no tradition that takes the chapter seriously can deny either.

**Tension 2: What does it mean that the divine name is written to be erased?**

- *Catholic*: The CCC §2143 and Aquinas (*ST* II-II q. 122 on honoring God's name) affirm the divine name's sanctity, but the rite's erasure for the sake of marital peace is read as a profound accommodation: God values the concrete peace of his people above the strict preservation of his own written name. The Eucharist — God's real presence given for the restoration of relationships — is read as the fulfillment of this logic.
- *Reformed*: Calvin treated the erasure as a provisional sign, fulfilled in Christ, who bears the curse for his people (Gal 3:13). The divine name's temporary submersion in the bitter water figures the cross.
- *Jewish*: Bavli Sotah 17a's teaching that "the Holy One, blessed be He, says, 'Let my name that is written in holiness be erased in the water to restore peace'" is one of the most striking theological statements in the Babylonian Talmud. Peace between husband and wife is weighted above the permanence of the divine name's inscription. This reading has generated an extensive Jewish tradition on *shalom bayit* ("peace in the home") as a supreme value.
- *Orthodox*: The erasure is read iconographically — the name disappears into the water only to be found again in the drinker, either as vindication or as curse. The liturgical typology is the chalice.
- *Feminist*: The erasure is theologically profound but ethically complicated. God's name is erased for the sake of peace, but the peace in question is achieved by subjecting a woman to a bodily ordeal. The theological claim (God values peace above his own name) coexists with the procedural claim (the peace is purchased at the woman's expense).
- *Critical*: Milgrom treats the erasure as a ritually distinctive Israelite innovation. The ANE ordeal traditions do not include this specific feature; it is a distinctively Israelite theological move, later developed by the rabbis into the *shalom bayit* principle.

The steelman for the least intuitive position — the Jewish reading that God's name is erased for the sake of peace — is that it discloses something unusual in the ANE religious imagination. Divine names in Mesopotamian, Egyptian, and Ugaritic texts are protected, written carefully, invoked reverently; erasure would be blasphemy. The Israelite ritual prescribes the erasure as a feature. This is either a shocking failure of reverence or a profound theological claim: the name is holy, but the name is not a talisman. The name serves God's purposes, including the restoration of marital peace, even at the cost of its own written form.

**Where this leaves us**: The erasure is a theologically radical gesture that both traditions (Jewish and Christian) have struggled to domesticate; it remains a datum that resists easy integration.

**Unresolved question**: If the ritual was abolished by Yohanan ben Zakkai because "adulterers multiplied" (Mishnah Sotah 9:9), does this mean that the ritual only worked when the social matrix around it was intact, and that the rite's efficacy depended on a moral economy that was already deteriorating? And if so — does this tell us something about the fragility of any ritual system's claim to disclose truth?

## The Thread

1. **John 7:53–8:11 — the woman caught in adultery**. The pericope echoes the *sōṭāh* structure with deliberate reversals. The woman is brought to a place of judgment (the Temple); a text is at issue (the law of Moses, per the accusers); Jesus writes on the ground (an echo of the dust-from-the-floor in Num 5:17 and the written curses of 5:23); the woman is not made to drink but is addressed directly. Raymond Brown (*The Gospel According to John*, 1966) and more recently Michael Fishbane have both noted the Numbers 5 subtext. The thread: where Numbers 5 tests the woman and leaves the husband "free from iniquity," John 8 tests the accusers and leaves the woman free.

2. **Hosea 2 — Yahweh as the jealous husband of faithless Israel**. The prophetic allegory turns the *sōṭāh* structure inside out: now God is the husband, Israel the suspected wife, the covenant the marriage. The ritual's imagery of jealousy, testing, and potential vindication becomes the frame for Hosea's entire oracle. The chapter's gendered asymmetry is preserved but theologically repurposed. Nili Sacher Fox and Phyllis Trible both traced this intertextual thread.

3. **1 Corinthians 11:27–32 — the unworthy reception of the Eucharist**. "Whoever eats the bread or drinks the cup of the Lord in an unworthy manner will be guilty concerning the body and blood of the Lord … that is why many of you are weak and ill, and some have died." Paul's warning echoes the *sōṭāh* ritual's logic: a consumable (bread/wine, or bitter water) discloses hidden sin by physical consequence. Patristic commentators from Cyprian onward noticed the parallel. The thread: the ordeal's logic — that what enters the body can test the soul — is preserved in Christian sacramental theology even as the specific ritual is abolished.

4. **Bavli Sotah 17a and the *shalom bayit* principle**. The rabbinic teaching that God's name may be erased for the sake of peace has become a foundational principle in later Jewish ethics. It is cited in rabbinic rulings on marriage, divorce, and domestic relations. The thread running from Numbers 5 through this Talmudic teaching is that ritual, law, and even divine honor are ordered toward concrete human peace — a theological priority that still informs Jewish ethical reasoning two millennia after the ritual was abolished.
