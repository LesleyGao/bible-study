# Psalm 143 — Go Deeper

## Lens 1: The Historical World Behind the Text

Psalm 143 carries the simple superscription *mizmor le-David* (מִזְמוֹר לְדָוִד, "a psalm of David") and stands as the sixth psalm in the **final Davidic cluster** (Pss 138-145, the second-to-last collection of Davidic psalms in the Psalter, gathered before the closing hallel-cascade of 146-150). It is the fourth and concluding member of the cluster's lament-sequence (140-143), and — most consequentially for its reception history — it is **the seventh and final psalm** of the traditional Christian sequence of **Penitential Psalms** (6, 32, 38, 51, 102, 130, 143). The grouping was given canonical form by Cassiodorus in his *Expositio Psalmorum* (sixth century AD) and definitively codified by the Medieval Western Church for use in Lent, the Liturgy of the Hours, and at the deathbed. To pray Psalm 143 in the Christian tradition is to pray the *closing* note of the great penitential cycle.

In Gunkel's form-critical taxonomy, Psalm 143 is an **individual lament** (*Klagelied des Einzelnen*) with a strong **penitential** accent. The standard lament structure is fully present: invocation (vv. 1-2), complaint (vv. 3-4), historical-meditative reflection (v. 5), petition (vv. 6-12). But the opening is what places this psalm specifically in the penitential category: *al-tavo be-mishpat et-avdekha* (אַל־תָּבוֹא בְמִשְׁפָּט אֶת־עַבְדֶּךָ, "enter not into judgment with your servant," v. 2a), followed by the great theological foundation: *ki lo-yitzdaq lefanekha kol-chai* (כִּי לֹא־יִצְדַּק לְפָנֶיךָ כָל־חָי, "for no one living is righteous before you," v. 2b). This is the high-water mark of pre-Christian penitential theology in the Hebrew Bible: a confession of universal human unrighteousness *before* the divine judgment.

Dating is debated. Older scholarship dated the psalm post-exilically on theological grounds (the *no-one-living-is-righteous* doctrine being read as a later development of Israelite reflection). Hossfeld and Zenger read it as part of the post-exilic editorial shaping of the Davidic cluster, deliberately concluding the lament-sequence with a theological-anthropological climax. Goldingay and Wenham allow for older liturgical material; the psalm's heavy borrowing from earlier Davidic laments (especially Pss 25, 27, 28) suggests a composition that draws on long-established Davidic-prayer vocabulary rather than later innovation.

Hebrew vocabulary is dense and theologically textured. The opening *Yahweh shema tefillati ha'azinah el-tachanunai* (יְהוָה שְׁמַע תְּפִלָּתִי הַאֲזִינָה אֶל־תַּחֲנוּנַי, v. 1) deploys the standard lament-vocabulary in synonymous parallelism. *Be-emunatekha aneini be-tzidqatekha* (בֶּאֱמוּנָתְךָ עֲנֵנִי בְצִדְקָתֶךָ, "in your faithfulness answer me, in your righteousness") pairs the two great divine-character attributes — *emunah* (faithfulness, the constancy of covenant) and *tzedaqah* (righteousness, the saving-justice God enacts on behalf of his people). The pairing is decisive for what follows in v. 2: the worshiper appeals not to his own righteousness but to *God's*.

The famous v. 2 line — *ki lo-yitzdaq lefanekha kol-chai* — uses the verb *yitzdaq* (qal of *tzadaq*, "to be righteous / to be vindicated / to be in the right"). The construction is specifically *forensic*: in a lawsuit (*mishpat*), the verb *yitzdaq* is what one does when one prevails, when one's case is upheld. *Lefanekha* ("before your face / in your presence / before you") is the courtroom-ready phrase. *Kol-chai* — "all living" — universalizes: not just this worshiper, not just Israel, but every living human being. The psalm enacts a universal-anthropological theology of sin in the most spare, judicial Hebrew possible.

Verses 3-4 develop the lament-complaint with funereal imagery: *hoshibani be-machashakkim ke-metei olam* (הוֹשִׁיבַנִי בְמַחֲשַׁכִּים כְּמֵתֵי עוֹלָם, v. 3, "he has made me sit in darkness like those long dead"). *Mete olam* — "the dead of antiquity / the long dead" — places the worshiper among the ancient dead in Sheol's depths. *Tit'attef alai ruchi* (v. 4, "my spirit faints within me") echoes Ps 142:3 verbatim, knitting the two psalms tightly. *Be-tokhi yishtomem libbi* ("my heart within me is appalled / desolate") uses *shamem* — the same root behind *shemamah* (desolation), the curse-word for ruined cities.

Verse 5's triplet of remembrance is poetically beautiful: *zakharti yamim mi-qedem hagiti be-khol-pa'olekha be-ma'aseh yadekha asocheach* (זָכַרְתִּי יָמִים מִקֶּדֶם הָגִיתִי בְכָל־פָּעֳלֶךָ בְּמַעֲשֵׂה יָדֶיךָ אֲשׂוֹחֵחַ, "I remember the days of old, I meditate on all you have done, I ponder the work of your hands"). The three verbs — *zakhar* (remember), *hagah* (meditate, recite-aloud-to-oneself), *suach* (ponder, muse) — name three modes of theological reflection. The worshiper exits the present despair through deliberate cognitive-spiritual practice: remembering, meditating, pondering.

Verse 6's *parashti yadai elekha nafshi ke-eretz-ayefah lekha* (פֵּרַשְׂתִּי יָדַי אֵלֶיךָ נַפְשִׁי כְאֶרֶץ־עֲיֵפָה לְךָ, "I stretch out my hands to you; my soul thirsts for you like a parched land") gives the psalm one of its most memorable images. *Eretz ayefah* — "land of weariness / parched land" — is desert-imagery; the soul as drought-stricken earth.

Verse 8's *hashmieini ba-boqer chasdekha* (הַשְׁמִיעֵנִי בַבֹּקֶר חַסְדֶּךָ, "let me hear in the morning of your steadfast love") and v. 10's *ruchakha tovah tancheini be-eretz mishor* ("let your good Spirit lead me on level ground") give the psalm its pneumatological notes — the *ruach Yahweh* leading the worshiper into level / upright / safe ground. The phrase *ruach tovah* (good Spirit) is one of the rare OT phrases that approaches a theology of the Spirit as good-and-leading.

The closing v. 12 contains a brief imprecation: *u-vechasdekha tatzmit oyvai veha'avadta kol-tzorerei nafshi* ("and in your steadfast love you will cut off my enemies, and you will destroy all the adversaries of my soul, for I am your servant"). The imprecation is markedly restrained — note that it is grounded *in your chesed*, in covenant-love, not in vengeful rage.

ANE parallels include Mesopotamian *eršahunga* ("cry-of-petition") and *šigû* prayers, where the suppliant confesses sins broadly without specific enumeration and pleads for the deity's mercy. What is distinctive in Ps 143 is the explicitly forensic *lo-yitzdaq lefanekha kol-chai* — the universal-anthropological claim about the impossibility of vindication by works.

Textually, Psalm 143 is well preserved. 11QPsa contains it. The LXX (142) follows the MT closely; v. 2's *kol-chai* is rendered *pas zōn* ("every living one"), and Paul will draw on the LXX form in Romans and Galatians.

## Lens 2: The Literature in Front of Us

The psalm is structured in a balanced eight-step movement that traces the classic lament-arc but with sustained theological depth:

1. **Vv. 1-2**: invocation and the universal-unrighteousness confession
2. **Vv. 3-4**: lament about the enemy and inner desolation
3. **Vv. 5-6**: turning-through-remembrance to longing
4. **V. 7**: urgent re-petition (*answer me quickly*)
5. **Vv. 8-9**: morning-petition for hearing of *chesed*, guidance, deliverance
6. **V. 10**: pneumatological petition (*your good Spirit lead me*)
7. **V. 11**: petition grounded in Yahweh's name and righteousness
8. **V. 12**: closing imprecation grounded in *chesed*

The structural movement is theologically remarkable. The psalm opens with universal-anthropological confession (no one is righteous), descends into present lament, pivots through remembrance, ascends through urgent prayer, and culminates in the petition that the *Spirit* lead the worshiper on level ground. The arc is repentance-to-renewed-walking — exactly the trajectory the penitential cycle is meant to enact.

Lowth's parallelism is rich. **Synonymous**: v. 1's tripled invocation ("hear my prayer // give ear to my pleas // in your faithfulness answer me, in your righteousness"). **Synthetic / climactic**: v. 5's triplet (remember // meditate // ponder). **Antithetic**: v. 2's *enter not into judgment* opposed to *answer me in faithfulness*. **Chiastic**: vv. 11-12 form a small chiasm — preserve my life // bring my soul out of trouble // cut off my enemies // destroy adversaries of my soul.

A distinctive literary feature is the **water-and-drought imagery**. Verse 6's *eretz ayefah* (parched land) is answered in v. 10 by *eretz mishor* (level / upright / fit-for-walking land); the journey of the psalm is from drought to firm ground. The journey runs through v. 8's *boqer* (morning) — the time when desert-dew arrives and night-prayer is answered.

The psalm is densely **intertextual** with earlier Davidic Psalms. Verse 8's *make me know the way I should go* echoes Ps 25; the morning-mercy theme echoes Ps 5; the Spirit-leading echoes Ps 51. Psalm 143 functions as a kind of *summa* of Davidic penitential prayer, gathering motifs from across the cluster.

## Lens 3: The Psalm Within the Larger Story

**Book V placement.** Psalm 143 stands as the sixth psalm in the final Davidic cluster (138-145), the fourth and concluding lament in the lament-sequence (140-143), and the deepest theological point of the cluster. The full cluster-arc: 138 (thanksgiving) → 139 (omniscience-meditation) → 140 (cry against violent men) → 141 (evening prayer of self-guarding) → 142 (cave-lament of total isolation) → **143 (penitential bottom — *lo-yitzdaq lefanekha kol-chai*)** → 144 (war-prayer recovery) → 145 (alphabetic doxology). 143 is where the cluster reaches its theological floor; from this confession of universal unrighteousness, the cluster begins its ascent into doxology.

**Jewish trajectory.** *Midrash Tehillim* on Psalm 143 reads it against the backdrop of David's fugitive-suffering, attaching it to various Davidic narratives but also reading it universally as the prayer of every penitent. Rashi attends carefully to the Hebrew of v. 2, reading *lo-yitzdaq lefanekha kol-chai* as a profound humility-theology: even a righteous person, when judged in absolute strict-justice rather than in mercy, cannot stand. Ibn Ezra develops the philosophical-theological reading. Radak harmonizes the various interpretive moves. In modern Jewish scholarship: Heschel reads v. 2 as a paradigmatic statement of the prophetic-theological recognition of the gap between divine *tzedaqah* and human capacity. Levenson notes that the verse stops short of a metaphysical doctrine of total human inability — it asserts that no one *would* be vindicated under strict judgment, leaving open the possibility that under *chesed* the relation is different. Sarna reads the morning-of-mercy theme as covenantal renewal-theology. Liturgically, Psalm 143 appears in some Sephardic *Tahanunim* cycles and in penitential contexts.

**Christian trajectory.** Psalm 143's reception is dominated by its **penitential-canonical** function. **Cassiodorus's *Expositio Psalmorum*** is the foundational text: he formally groups the seven (Pss 6, 32, 38, 51, 102, 130, 143) and treats Ps 143 as the climactic culmination — the prayer in which the penitential cycle confesses its unrighteousness and pleads for the *good Spirit* to lead. The grouping was authoritative for Augustine before Cassiodorus formalized it: Augustine reportedly had the seven penitential psalms inscribed on the wall facing his deathbed, and prayed them as he died. **Aquinas** in his Psalms commentary develops v. 2 as a fundamental text for the doctrine of justification: no one is righteous before God's strict justice; salvation is by mercy alone. **Luther** treats Psalm 143 as one of the most pastorally crucial psalms in the Psalter — his *Operationes in Psalmos* dwells on v. 2 as a foundational text for *sola fide*: if no one living is righteous before God, then justification cannot be by works; it must be by faith in God's righteousness imputed to the believer. **Calvin** similarly treats v. 2 as a foundational sola-fide text in his *Institutes* (3.11) and in his Psalms commentary.

The Penitential Psalms shaped Western Christian devotional life for over a millennium. They were assigned for daily recitation in monastic Liturgy of the Hours; they framed Lent in the Roman Rite; they were appointed for the deathbed; they were the basis of countless musical settings — including most famously the seven *Penitential Psalms* of **Orlando di Lasso** (1584) and **Andrea Gabrieli**, and innumerable Anglican and Lutheran settings. **Pope Innocent III** (1198-1216) prescribed their recitation during Lent; the **Reformed** tradition kept them in the Anglican *Book of Common Prayer*'s Lenten provision (the seven psalms are appointed for Ash Wednesday in the BCP) and in Reformed devotional manuals.

Among modern critical scholars: Gunkel classifies it as *Klagelied des Einzelnen* with penitential features; Mowinckel reads it cultically against temple penitential rites; Westermann notes its developed lament-form; Hans-Joachim Kraus reads it as a late post-exilic composition reflecting developed sin-theology; Hossfeld and Zenger emphasize its editorial-canonical climax-function in the cluster; Goldingay attends to the psalm's intertextual richness; Wenham reads vv. 8-10 as ethical-formation petition; Brueggemann reads it as paradigmatic of his "psalms of disorientation moving toward new orientation"; Patrick Miller emphasizes the prayer-as-theology dimension; William P. Brown attends to the imagery; Beth Tanner reads it as the cluster's penitential-theological climax.

**NT use.** Psalm 143 is among the most theologically consequential psalms in the New Testament. **Romans 3:20** — *dioti ex ergōn nomou ou dikaiōthēsetai pasa sarx enōpion autou* ("for by works of the law no flesh will be justified before him") — is a direct citation of Psalm 143:2 in its LXX form (with *pasa sarx* substituted for the LXX's *pas zōn*, perhaps under influence from Gen 6:12, but the conceptual core is straight Ps 143:2). **Galatians 2:16** — *ou dikaiōthēsetai ex ergōn nomou pasa sarx* — repeats the citation as the foundational scriptural warrant for Paul's doctrine of justification by faith. The psalm's *lo-yitzdaq lefanekha kol-chai* becomes, in Pauline hands, the OT scriptural foundation for the entire doctrine of justification by faith apart from works of the law. No psalm-citation is more consequential for Reformation theology.

## Lens 4: The Theological Intent — Tensions, Positions, and Where This Leaves Us

**Tension 1: The Pauline use of v. 2 and the doctrine of justification.** The transformation of Ps 143:2 in Pauline hands — from a personal confession into a universal-soteriological doctrine — has shaped Western Christianity decisively. The tension is over what the verse means in itself, what Paul made of it, and what the Reformation made of Paul.

*Position A: Reformation *sola fide* (Luther, Calvin, the Westminster Confession).* The verse establishes that no human being can be vindicated under strict divine judgment by works of any kind. Paul's use confirms this. Justification is by faith alone, on the basis of Christ's righteousness imputed to the believer. The Penitential cycle is the fitting devotional shape for the Christian life: the believer learns continuously to confess universal unrighteousness and to receive grace by faith. *Steelman:* this honors Paul's actual rhetorical use of the verse, the verse's own forensic-judicial framing, and the Reformation's pastoral concern that no believer be left in anxious self-justification.

*Position B: Catholic teaching (Council of Trent, *Decree on Justification* 1547).* Trent affirmed that humans cannot be justified by their natural strength or by works of the law (DS 1521-1525), but defined justification as both forensic (forgiveness of sin) *and* transformative (regeneration and sanctification). Faith is the beginning, root, and foundation of justification, but it is not alone — it works through love. The penitential cycle remains foundational to Catholic devotional life; Ps 143 is prayed not merely as forensic confession but as the entry-point into ongoing sanctification. *Steelman:* this honors the integration of justification and sanctification in the broader biblical witness, takes seriously the language of "transformation" alongside "imputation," and preserves the long Catholic devotional reception of the penitential psalms.

*Position C: The *Joint Declaration on the Doctrine of Justification* (1999, signed by the Lutheran World Federation and the Roman Catholic Church; later joined by the World Methodist Council and the World Communion of Reformed Churches).* The Joint Declaration affirmed that there is a real consensus on the basic truths of justification by grace alone through faith in Christ, without denying the genuine differences that remain. The previous condemnations of the sixteenth century are no longer applicable in the way they were originally intended. *Steelman:* this represents the most serious recent ecumenical engagement with the doctrine; it does not paper over remaining differences but locates them within a recovered shared confession; it allows Ps 143:2 to function as a shared scriptural foundation for the Christian doctrine of justification across confessional lines.

*Position D: The *New Perspective on Paul* (E. P. Sanders, James Dunn, N. T. Wright).* Paul's *erga nomou* (works of the law) referred specifically to Jewish boundary-marker observances (circumcision, Sabbath, food laws), not to "good works in general"; Paul's argument is about the inclusion of the Gentiles, not primarily about an abstract anthropological doctrine of total inability. Ps 143:2 in Paul's hands functions to ground a covenantal-historical argument about who counts as the people of God. *Steelman:* this reading attends carefully to the Pauline-rhetorical context, preserves the Jewishness of Paul's argument, and resists imposing later Reformation categories onto first-century apostolic writing. It need not displace traditional readings but enriches them with covenantal-historical depth.

**Tension 2: The seven Penitential Psalms as devotional cycle.** The grouping is not biblically explicit; it is a Christian-traditional construction.

*Position A: Pro the cycle (Catholic, Anglican, Lutheran traditional).* The seven-psalm cycle is a profound devotional shape that has formed Christians for over a millennium; its grouping is theologically apt (six laments and a confession at the climax); its prayer in Lent and at the deathbed has carried countless souls through repentance into peace. The cycle should be retained and taught.

*Position B: Reformed-Puritan caution.* The grouping is non-biblical and risks reducing the Psalter's penitential dimension to seven texts when in fact the entire Psalter shapes penitential life. The cycle is permissible but should not be exalted above the broader Psalter use. *Steelman:* this preserves the canonical primacy of the entire Psalter and resists the overspecialization of devotional traditions.

*Position C: Historical-critical caution (Hossfeld-Zenger, Goldingay).* The cycle is a post-biblical Christian construction that imposes a particular theological reading on a diverse set of laments; it is liturgically valuable but should not be confused with the Hebrew Bible's own structuring. *Steelman:* this honors the integrity of the original psalms-as-individual-units while allowing their canonical-Christian reception its due weight.

**Where this leaves us.** Psalm 143 closes the cluster's lament-sequence with the deepest theological note in the entire Psalter — the universal-anthropological *lo-yitzdaq lefanekha kol-chai*. From this confession, two trajectories run forward. One is the trajectory through Pauline hands into the doctrine of justification, where this verse becomes the scriptural warrant for the entire Reformation reorientation of Christian salvation-theology. The other is the trajectory through Cassiodorus's hands into the seven-psalm penitential cycle that has shaped Christian devotional life for fifteen centuries. Both trajectories — the doctrinal and the devotional — converge on the same insight: that the Christian life begins, and continues, in the confession of unrighteousness before God, and in the receiving of God's *chesed* and *tzedaqah* as gift.

The closing pneumatology is poetically extraordinary: *ruchakha tovah tancheini be-eretz mishor* — "let your good Spirit lead me on level ground." After the descent into universal unrighteousness, after the present lament, after the morning-prayer for *chesed*, the psalm ends with the petition that the *Spirit* lead. The penitential cycle resolves not in the worshiper's resolved interior but in the Spirit's leading. This is — though the psalm could not name it — already proto-Trinitarian theology: the worshiper appeals to the Father's faithfulness, confesses universal sin, and is led by the good Spirit on the level ground that, in Christian reading, is Christ himself.

**Closing question.** Psalm 143 ends not with arrival but with petition for guidance — *teach me to do your will*. If you have prayed through the seven Penitential Psalms — six laments and this final confession — what is the *eretz mishor*, the level ground, that the good Spirit is now asking you to walk? And what does it mean that the Penitential cycle does not end in penance but in *being led*?
