# 2 Chronicles 31 — Hezekiah's Reform: Tithes, Levitical Support, and the Heaps that Filled the Temple

## Lens 1 — The Historical World Behind the Text

Second Chronicles 31 belongs to the Chronicler's Hezekiah cycle (chs. 29-32), one of the most expansive royal portraits in the work and almost certainly the climactic theological set-piece of the entire history. The Chronicler — anonymous, working in Yehud sometime in the late fifth or early fourth century BC, almost certainly attached to the Second Temple establishment in Jerusalem — frames his Hezekiah in a way that goes far beyond what 2 Kings 18 supplies. Where Kings devotes a single verse (2 Kgs 18:4) to Hezekiah's cultic reform, Chronicles devotes four chapters: the cleansing of the temple (29), the great Passover (30), the reorganization of the offerings and Levitical support (31), and the Sennacherib deliverance (32). Chapter 31 is unique to the Chronicler. There is no Kings parallel for the *heaps*, the chambers, the tithe-collection apparatus, or the Levitical genealogical enrollment. This is the Chronicler's own contribution, and reading it well requires asking what such a contribution is *for*.

Sara Japhet's commentary (*I & II Chronicles*, OTL, 1993) reads chapter 31 as the institutional payoff of the cult-renewal pattern. The temple has been cleansed (ch. 29), the people have come up to keep Passover (ch. 30), and now in chapter 31 the people take their renewed allegiance back into the countryside — first by destroying the *matsevot* (מַצֵּבוֹת, "pillars"), the *asherim* (אֲשֵׁרִים, sacred wooden poles), the *bamot* (בָּמוֹת, "high places"), and the altars throughout Judah and Benjamin and even in Ephraim and Manasseh (31:1) — and then by re-funding the priests and Levites who guard the renewed cult. Japhet sees the chapter as deeply post-exilic in concern. The Chronicler is writing for a community whose own temple is dependent on freewill offerings, tithes, firstfruits, and dedicated portions, and whose Levites and priests are constantly underfunded (cf. Neh 13:10-13, where Nehemiah finds the Levites have abandoned their service because they were not being paid). The "heaps" of 31:6-9 are not a quaint detail — they are an idealized image of what the post-exilic community could be if it actually obeyed the tithe legislation it had inherited.

Ralph Klein (*2 Chronicles*, Hermeneia, 2012) presses harder on the institutional reading. Klein notes the dense administrative vocabulary in 31:11-19 — the *lishkot* (לְשָׁכוֹת, "chambers"), the *peqiddim* (פְּקִידִים, "overseers"), the *machaloqot* (מַחְלְקוֹת, "divisions"), the *yachas* (יַחַשׂ, "genealogical enrollment"). Klein argues that the chapter functions as a *charter* for Second-Temple cultic administration. The list of officers in 31:12-15 (Conaniah, Shimei, Jehiel, Azaziah, Nahath, Asahel, Jerimoth, Jozabad, Eliel, Ismachiah, Mahath, Benaiah, Kore, Eden, Miniamin, Jeshua, Shemaiah, Amariah, Shecaniah) is too long and too specific to be free invention. Either the Chronicler had access to actual Levitical-house records or — more likely — he was retrojecting *his own day's* administrative structure onto Hezekiah, claiming royal precedent for what the post-exilic Levites were doing. The phrase *bne aharon ha-kohanim* (בְּנֵי אַהֲרֹן הַכֹּהֲנִים, "the sons of Aaron, the priests," 31:19) maps onto the priestly genealogical theory of Ezra-Nehemiah, where priestly identity is policed by lineage records (Ezra 2:62 / Neh 7:64 — those who could not produce records were "excluded from the priesthood as unclean").

H.G.M. Williamson (*1 and 2 Chronicles*, NCBC, 1982) emphasizes the Chronicler's all-Israel concern. The opening verse — "all Israel who were present went out to the cities of Judah and broke in pieces the pillars … in Ephraim and Manasseh" (31:1) — is striking. Hezekiah ruled only Judah; the northern kingdom had fallen to Assyria a few years before his accession (722 BC). For the Chronicler, however, Hezekiah's reform reaches into former-northern territory because *all Israel* belongs to the temple cult, and the cleansing of *bamot* in Ephraim and Manasseh anticipates the eventual reunification under one cult. This is one of the Chronicler's persistent themes: the twelve tribes are still one people, and the Davidic-Jerusalem cult is the proper home of all of them. Gary Knoppers (*1 Chronicles 1-9*, AB 12, 2003) develops this further by showing how the Chronicler consistently uses the phrase *kol-yisrael* ("all Israel") in cult-renewal contexts as a programmatic claim against any sectarian narrowing.

Mark Boda (*1-2 Chronicles*, Cornerstone Biblical Commentary, 2010) draws attention to the chronological note: "in the third month they began to pile up the heaps, and finished them in the seventh month" (31:7). The third month is *Sivan* (the month of Pentecost, *Shavuot*); the seventh is *Tishri* (the month of Booths, *Sukkot*). The heaps thus span the harvest cycle from grain-firstfruits through fruit-and-oil ingathering. The tithe is not a one-time event; it is the people's whole agricultural year handed over in proportionate gratitude. Steven McKenzie (*1-2 Chronicles*, AOTC, 2004) and Louis Jonker (*1 & 2 Chronicles*, Understanding the Bible Commentary, 2013) both read this temporal frame as theologically loaded — the entire harvest belongs to YHWH, and the tithe acknowledges that ownership.

A final historical note: the chapter's tithe details echo, but do not perfectly reproduce, Pentateuchal tithe legislation. Numbers 18 gives the Levite tithe; Deuteronomy 14 prescribes a tithe consumed at the central sanctuary; Leviticus 27 distinguishes the redeemable tithe of grain and fruit from the unredeemable tithe of cattle. The Chronicler in 31:5-6 mentions firstfruits of grain, wine, oil, honey, and produce, and the tithe of cattle and sheep — a composite drawing on multiple Pentateuchal sources. This composite quality is itself evidence that the Chronicler is writing late, when the Pentateuch is functionally one body of law and a careful reader could blend its strands.

## Lens 2 — The Literature of the Text

The chapter divides into four panels. Verses 1-2 form a transition from the Passover (ch. 30) to the new cultic order, with the destruction of the *bamot* and Hezekiah's reorganization of the priestly *machaloqot*. Verses 3-10 narrate the giving — Hezekiah's royal contribution, the people's overflow response, and the heaps. Verses 11-19 form an administrative section listing the officers and their duties. Verses 20-21 close with a theological summary statement that functions almost as a benediction over Hezekiah's reign-to-this-point: "he did what was good and right and faithful before the LORD his God … and prospered."

The literary heart of the chapter is the dialogue between Hezekiah and Azariah the chief priest (31:9-10). Hezekiah inspects the heaps, asks about them, and Azariah replies: "Since they began to bring the contributions into the house of the LORD, *we have eaten and had enough and have plenty left*" (31:10, ESV). The Hebrew is *akhol ve-savoa ve-hoter* (אָכוֹל וְשָׂבוֹעַ וְהוֹתֵר, literally "to eat and be satisfied and have leftover"). The triple verb is the Chronicler's way of dramatizing abundance. The same root *savoa* ("be satisfied") echoes Deuteronomy 8:10 ("you shall eat and be full and bless the LORD your God") and the wilderness manna tradition. The Chronicler is suggesting that obedient tithing produces a wilderness-style abundance: enough for the priests, enough for the Levites, and *enough left over* — the *yothereth*, the surplus, a sign of YHWH's blessing.

The phrase "laid them in heaps" (31:6) uses *aremot* (עֲרֵמוֹת, "piles, mounds"), repeated in 31:7-9 (six occurrences in three verses). This kind of word repetition is the Chronicler's preferred mode of emphasis. The heaps become a visual icon: the reader can almost see them piling up, growing through the harvest months, witnessed by king and princes. It is the Chronicler's theology made visible — *if you obey, this is what abundance looks like*.

The administrative section (31:11-19) deploys the Chronicler's catalog style. Names cluster, duties are specified, the *bne aharon ha-kohanim* are distinguished from the Levites, and the genealogical enrollment (*yachas*) extends down to children "from three years old and upward" (31:16). The phrase *al-yad* (עַל־יַד, "by the hand of," "under the supervision of") appears repeatedly, structuring the hierarchy. This is bureaucratic prose in the best sense: a paper trail that authorizes and protects the integrity of the cult.

The closing summary (31:20-21) deploys four key verbs that map onto the Chronicler's evaluative grid. Hezekiah did what was *tov* (טוֹב, "good"), *yashar* (יָשָׁר, "right"), and *emet* (אֱמֶת, "faithful," "true") before YHWH; he sought (*darash*, דָּרַשׁ) his God *be-kol levavo* (בְּכָל־לְבָבוֹ, "with all his heart"); and he prospered (*hitzliach*, הִצְלִיחַ). This trio — *darash + be-kol levavo + hitzliach* — is the Chronicler's signature formula for a faithful king (cf. 2 Chr 14:7 of Asa, 2 Chr 26:5 of Uzziah). Hezekiah meets the highest standard the Chronicler knows how to express.

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

In the Hebrew canonical order, Chronicles is the *last* book of the Tanakh, and the Hezekiah cycle within it functions as one of the Chronicler's positive pillars — alongside David, Solomon, Asa, Jehoshaphat, and Josiah — that hold up the Davidic ideal even as the people walk into exile. The community reading Chronicles in Yehud already knew exile had come, the Northern Kingdom was gone, and the Persian-period temple was a fragile institution dependent on the very tithes Hezekiah here organizes. Chapter 31 says: this is the model. When kings (or their post-monarchic equivalents — high priests, governors, the assembly itself) take responsibility for funding the cult, and when the people give in proportion to harvest, the *aremot* pile up.

The Jewish trajectory takes this seriously. Rabbinic Judaism inherited the tithe legislation and elaborated it in tractate Ma'aserot and Ma'aser Sheni in the Mishnah. The destruction of the Second Temple in 70 AD redirected the *terumah* and tithe obligations: most became theoretical or were redirected to the poor (*ma'aser ani*). Yet the principle that one's harvest belongs first to God and that the religious specialists (rabbis, scholars, the synagogue infrastructure) are sustained by the people's giving has remained foundational. The medieval institution of *kuppah* and *tamhuy* (the communal charity fund and soup kitchen) carry forward the Chronicler's vision in altered form: the community sustains those who study Torah and serve the community, and the surplus feeds the poor. Maimonides codifies this in *Mishneh Torah, Hilkhot Matnot Aniyim*, ranking the eight degrees of charity. The Chronicler's image of the heap that has *yothereth* — surplus — is not far from the rabbinic ideal of a community whose giving outruns its members' need.

The Christian trajectory takes a different turn. The New Testament generally inherits not the tithe legislation but the principle behind it. Paul in 1 Corinthians 9:13-14 invokes temple precedent — "those who serve at the altar share in the sacrificial offerings" — to argue that "the Lord has commanded that those who proclaim the gospel should get their living by the gospel," and yet in the same passage refuses to take payment himself. The image of *eaten and satisfied and left over* echoes in the feeding of the five thousand (Mark 6:42-43, *all ate and were satisfied … twelve baskets full of broken pieces left over*). Whether Mark meant to evoke 2 Chr 31:10 specifically is unprovable, but the cluster of motifs — abundance from obedience, leftover as sign — is the same theological grammar.

In Christian history the chapter has had a long afterlife in stewardship preaching. The Reformation reformers — Luther, Calvin, Bullinger — generally argued against any literal tithe obligation while holding up the Hezekiah pattern as a model of *willing*, *abundant* giving. John Wesley's "On the Use of Money" sermons draw extensively on this kind of imagery: earn all you can, save all you can, give all you can, and trust that the heap will not run out. Modern evangelical stewardship traditions (Tyndale, Crown Financial, Dave Ramsey) tend to teach a 10% tithe as a starting floor; modern mainline traditions tend to teach proportional giving without a fixed percentage. Both traditions look back to chapters like 2 Chronicles 31 for the *culture* of giving — the king who gives first, the people who give in abundance, the priests who eat and are satisfied, the heap that grows because everyone is doing what they should.

The Catholic tradition has institutionalized this differently through the universal church tax (in some European countries, the *Kirchensteuer*) and through diocesan support structures, but the underlying theology — that the people sustain the ministers and the surplus blesses the whole — is recognizably Chronicler-shaped. Eastern Orthodox traditions emphasize the Levitical pattern even more strongly in their theology of the priesthood.

## Lens 4 — The Chronicler's Theological Intent

The Chronicler is not a neutral reporter. He has an agenda, and chapter 31 makes it visible.

The agenda: *the post-exilic community should function as a tithing, Levitically-organized, all-Israel cult around the rebuilt Jerusalem temple, with the king (or his functional equivalent) taking responsibility for cultic order, and obedient giving will produce visible, tangible abundance — the heaps.*

This is a specifically *post-exilic* polemic. The community the Chronicler addresses is fragile, underfunded, internally contested. There are competing visions of what the restored people should be — the prophetic vision of Ezekiel 40-48 with its temple-centered theocracy, the Ezra-Nehemiah vision of a Torah-keeping endogamous community, the older Pentateuchal vision of tribal land and federated worship. The Chronicler's vision is *Davidic-Jerusalemite-Levitical*, and chapter 31 is one of his most concrete sketches of it.

**Tension 1: the cult-renewal pattern and modern stewardship.** The Chronicler presents Hezekiah's tithing reform as straightforwardly successful — heaps appear, priests are fed, surplus accumulates, Hezekiah prospers. But for many modern readers, this pattern is uncomfortable. It seems to promise a transactional return on giving — give, and you will prosper — that resembles the prosperity gospel more than New-Testament generosity. How seriously should we take the Chronicler's *prospered* (*hitzliach*) at the end of the chapter?

The classical evangelical position (Tyndale, Crown, Ramsey-style stewardship preaching) takes the Chronicler at face value: tithing is a spiritual discipline that releases blessing, and 2 Chr 31 is biblical evidence. The mainline Protestant position (Walter Brueggemann, Ellen Davis) reads the heap-image as covenantal rather than transactional — abundance is the natural shape of a community whose economy is ordered around mutual care, and "prosperity" means the *cult is sustained and the people are fed*, not personal wealth. The Catholic and Orthodox traditions read the chapter through their sacramental theology of stewardship — giving is offering, offering is sacrament, and the abundance is communion. The honest steelman of the Chronicler is that he genuinely *does* see obedient giving as triggering tangible blessing; this is part of his immediate-retribution theology and cannot be explained away. The honest counter is that the New Testament — and the book of Job, and Ecclesiastes — complicates this pattern even within the canon. Job is righteous and suffers. Jesus is faithful and is crucified. The Chronicler's pattern is one biblical voice among several.

**Tension 2: who is "all Israel" today?** The chapter opens with all Israel destroying *bamot* in Ephraim and Manasseh, territory under Assyrian control. The Chronicler is making a programmatic claim about who belongs to the temple cult — *everyone descended from Jacob, including those scattered*. For Jewish readers across history, this has been a claim about the unity of the Jewish people across diaspora. For Christian readers, the New Testament re-keys "Israel" through the church (Rom 9-11, Gal 6:16, debated). The post-supersessionist turn (Soulen, Levenson, Barclay, Wright in his later work) tries to honor both: Israel as the empirical Jewish people remains the chosen, while the gentile assemblies are grafted in (Rom 11:17). The Chronicler's *kol-yisrael* speaks first to its original referent — the twelve-tribe people of YHWH — and only secondarily, with care, to anyone else.

**Where this leaves us:** 2 Chronicles 31 gives us an image. The heaps grow, the priests eat, the surplus accumulates, the king inspects and blesses. It is a vision of a community whose economy is reordered around its worship. It is not transactional magic — it is what a properly-ordered covenant community looks like when each part does its work. The Chronicler is offering this image to a fragile post-exilic Yehud as a charter and a hope. Modern Jewish and Christian readers can receive it the same way: not as a formula for personal prosperity, but as a portrait of what generous, organized, joyful giving makes possible.

**Closing question:** What would it look like, in the institutions you actually belong to — congregation, synagogue, parish, civic association, family — for the *aremot* to start to grow? And who, in your context, is the *yothereth* meant to feed?

## The Thread

Chapter 31 is the institutional payoff of the Hezekiah reform. The temple is cleansed (29), the people have come up to keep Passover (30), and now the harvest is brought in and the cult is funded (31). The next chapter will test whether this reformed Judah can stand against Assyria when Sennacherib comes for it. The Chronicler is teaching us to read what comes next as the consequence of what has come now: a faithful, tithing, Levitically-ordered Judah faces the empire — and discovers what its faithfulness is worth.
