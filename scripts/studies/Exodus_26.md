# Exodus 26: A Tent That Becomes a Temple

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

The political setting of Exodus 26 depends heavily on the dating assumed for the material. On a pre-exilic reading, the chapter reflects late Bronze Age to early Iron Age realities: an Israel remembering — or imagining — its origins in the wilderness, in the orbit of a Ramesside Egypt whose court ideology and craft traditions left unmistakable fingerprints on the tabernacle design. On the critical reading inherited from Julius Wellhausen, the chapter is a late exilic or early post-exilic projection, a Second Temple liturgical architecture retrojected into the wilderness past in order to authorize the priestly establishment of the return. Both readings have evidence; neither is dogmatically secure. The middle path taken by Menahem Haran in *Temples and Temple-Service in Ancient Israel* (1978) is probably the most defensible: the P source has undergone late editing, but its underlying tabernacle tradition preserves features too archaic to be invented by a post-exilic scribe — in particular, the tent-shrine typology and the acacia-wood portability, which make poor sense as a fiction generated within a temple culture that had been building in stone for centuries.

The material culture is overwhelmingly ancient Near Eastern. Portable sanctuaries are not a Hebrew peculiarity. The Bedouin *qubba* — a small red leather tent-shrine carried on a camel — is attested across the Arabian peninsula into the Islamic period. Michael Homan's *To Your Tents, O Israel!* (2002) documents Egyptian tent-shrines with remarkable precision: Ramses II's Qadesh campaign pavilion, depicted at Abu Simbel, shows a rectangular tent with a 2:1 ratio, divided interior, and central throne — a morphological match to the *miškān*. Kenneth Kitchen pressed the point in *On the Reliability of the Old Testament*: a post-exilic scribe would not spontaneously reproduce the proportions of a thirteenth-century Egyptian royal tent. The Timna Egyptian mining-shrine (Beno Rothenberg), reused by Midianites, supplies another convergence — a small enclosure walled by fabric hangings on wooden poles, at home in the Late Bronze wilderness.

The textile specifications in 26:1 — fine twined linen with blue (*tĕkēlet*), purple (*ʾargāmān*), and scarlet (*tôlaʿat šānî*) yarns — describe the most expensive fabrics of the ancient world. *Tĕkēlet* and *ʾargāmān* are dyes extracted from murex sea snails (*Hexaplex trunculus*, *Bolinus brandaris*), processed in vast fermentation vats along the Phoenician coast at Tyre, Sidon, and Sarepta. Thousands of snails yielded a single garment's worth of dye; the trade was a Phoenician monopoly, and Tyrian purple commanded prices higher than gold. The tabernacle, using all three dyes with cherubim figures woven in, is imagined as clothed in royal fabric — and mixes linen and wool in a way Leviticus 19:19 forbids to ordinary Israelites. The tabernacle, like the high priest's garments, stands outside ordinary categories.

For authorship and dating: Wellhausen's *Prolegomena* (1878) treated chapters 25–31 and 35–40 as P, a retrojection of Solomonic and especially Second Temple liturgy. Haran argued for a pre-exilic P core. Milgrom's Anchor Bible *Leviticus* distinguished P from H (Holiness), placing most of Exodus 25–29 in P and pushing P earlier than Wellhausen allowed. Israel Knohl's *Sanctuary of Silence* (1995) kept the distinction but argued H post-dated P. Cassuto argued for substantial pre-exilic antiquity. Richard Elliott Friedman locates P in the reign of Hezekiah. William Propp in the Anchor Bible *Exodus 19–40* (2006) surveys these options without resolving them.

Archaeological evidence is indirect but suggestive. The Iron Age sanctuaries at Arad (Aharoni) and Dan (Biran) provide comparanda for sacrificial installations. The Kuntillet Ajrud inscriptions (late ninth to early eighth century BCE) show northern Israelite religious practice outside Jerusalem. Textually, 4QpaleoExod^m preserves a proto-Samaritan text type with expansions at Exodus 26; the LXX of Exodus 35–40 differs from MT in order and content — the "Second Tabernacle Account" problem studied by Anneli Aejmelaeus and John Wevers.

Key Hebrew terminology: *miškān* (מִשְׁכָּן), "dwelling-place," from the root *š-k-n*, "to dwell"; the same root gives *šĕkînâ*, the rabbinic term for divine indwelling presence. The word emphasizes inhabitation, not merely structure. *ʾōhel môʿēd* (אֹהֶל מוֹעֵד), "tent of meeting," is the chapter's alternative designation (implicit here, explicit elsewhere), connoting an appointed rendezvous rather than permanent residence. *pārōket* (פָּרֹכֶת), the veil at 26:31 separating the Holy of Holies, appears to be a Mesopotamian loanword (Akkadian *parāku*, "to bar, obstruct"). *kĕrubîm* (כְּרֻבִים), cherubim, woven into both the inner curtain and the veil, are hybrid guardian figures paralleled in ANE iconography by the *kāribu* lion-bodied guardians flanking temple entrances. *qōdeš* and *qōdeš qodāšîm* (קֹדֶשׁ / קֹדֶשׁ הַקֳּדָשִׁים), Holy Place and Most Holy, establish a graduated sacrality the whole rest of Leviticus will presuppose.

## Lens 2 — The Literature

The genre is priestly architectural instruction with symbolic-theological load. ANE parallels are dense. The Gudea Cylinders from Sumer (late third millennium BCE) describe in remarkable detail the Lagashite ruler Gudea's construction of the Eninnu temple for Ningirsu — divine command, architectural specifications, divine indwelling, dedication rites. The Ugaritic Baal Cycle (KTU 1.4) narrates the building of Baal's palace: Kothar-wa-Hasis the divine craftsman, cedar from Lebanon, precious metals, a debated window, and divine indwelling. Victor Hurowitz's *I Have Built You an Exalted House* (1992) catalogued this ANE temple-building pattern systematically: the Exodus tabernacle accounts and the Solomonic temple narrative of 1 Kings 6–8 fit the pattern precisely, down to sub-sequences of command, execution, and dedication.

The seven-speech structure identified first by Ramban and developed by Jon Levenson is one of the most important literary observations on this material. Across Exodus 25–31, the narrative is organized by seven divine speeches, each marked by "And YHWH said to Moses" (25:1; 30:11; 30:17; 30:22; 30:34; 31:1; 31:12). The seventh speech is about the Sabbath. The parallel to the seven days of creation in Genesis 1 — where the seventh day is Sabbath — is almost certainly intentional. Levenson, in *Creation and the Persistence of Evil* (1988), drew the conclusion: the tabernacle is a microcosm of the cosmos; its construction recapitulates creation; the *miškān* is where God, having made the world, comes to dwell within it. The formula of completion in Exodus 39:32 and 39:43 ("And Moses saw all the work, and behold, they had done it. As YHWH had commanded, so they had done it. And Moses blessed them") echoes Genesis 1:31 and 2:1–3. This is not homiletical flourish; it is the P source's central theological-literary move.

Literary devices within Exodus 26 itself: the inclusio *kĕkol ʾăšer ʾănî marʾeh ʾôtĕkā* / "according to the plan that you were shown on the mountain" (26:30) frames the chapter's instructions as the enactment of a revealed pattern, a *tabnît*. Numerology is dense: ten curtains of linen (26:1), eleven of goats' hair (26:7), fifty loops of blue (26:5), fifty clasps of gold (26:6), twenty frames on each long side (26:18, 20), six at the rear (26:22), eight total with corner frames (26:25), five bars on each side (26:26–27), a middle bar running "end to end" (26:28). These numbers are not random; ten is a completion unit, fifty is the Jubilee number (Leviticus 25), twelve is the tribal number (though not foregrounded here), and the chapter's arithmetic generates a structure whose proportions repeatedly align with the Holy of Holies as a perfect ten-cubit cube — a proportion that reappears in the Solomonic temple's inner sanctum (1 Kings 6:20) and reaches its eschatological limit in the cubic New Jerusalem of Revelation 21:16.

A chiastic pattern runs through the chapter: curtains (vv. 1–14), frames (vv. 15–30), textile dividers (vv. 31–37). The innermost and outermost are fabric — soft, figured, decorated with cherubim and colored yarns — while the structural middle is wood and metal. Fabric contains; wood supports. The veil (26:31–33) is explicitly said to be made "with cherubim skillfully worked into it" — the same specification as the inner curtain of 26:1. The continuity is not accidental. Behind the veil, cherubim also surmount the mercy seat (Exodus 25:18–22). The Holy of Holies is thus *saturated* with cherubim — on the walls, on the veil, on the ark. Ezekiel's vision of YHWH's chariot-throne (Ezekiel 1, 10) borne by four cherubim draws on precisely this iconography.

Textual variants compound the interpretive task. The LXX of Exodus 35–40 not only differs in order from the MT (placing the garments before the tabernacle proper in some reconstructions) but omits or compresses substantial blocks of material. Aejmelaeus's work on the Greek Exodus argued that the translator had a Hebrew *Vorlage* genuinely different from the MT, not merely a creative rearrangement. Wevers's *Text History of the Greek Exodus* catalogued hundreds of variants. The Samaritan Pentateuch, in characteristic fashion, harmonizes the command (Exodus 25–31) with the execution (35–40) by inserting execution formulae into the command section. DSS 4QpaleoExod^m agrees with the Samaritan on many of these expansions, showing that the proto-Samaritan text type was a genuine textual tradition in late Second Temple Judaism, not a late sectarian invention. The tabernacle text was alive and contested well into the first century.

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

The tabernacle is the paradigm for everything the Bible later says about sacred space. First Kings 6–8 describes the Solomonic temple using the tabernacle's vocabulary and proportions: the same graduated sacrality (court, Holy Place, Most Holy), the same veil (2 Chronicles 3:14), the same cherubim. Second Chronicles 3:14 makes the veil-to-temple translation explicit. Ezekiel 40–48 envisions a future temple in the most meticulous architectural detail the Bible ever provides, with the measuring reed reappearing from the tabernacle's cubit-grid. Revelation 21 pushes the logic to its limit: the New Jerusalem *is* the Holy of Holies, a cube "twelve thousand stadia" on each side (21:16) — the ten-cubit cube of the tabernacle's innermost chamber scaled to cosmic dimensions, a city where "I saw no temple in it, for its temple is the Lord God the Almighty and the Lamb" (21:22). The Johannine Jesus' "destroy this temple and in three days I will raise it up" (John 2:19) identifies his body with the tabernacle's function, and 1 Corinthians 3:16 ("you are God's temple") and 2 Corinthians 6:16 extend the identification to the church.

**Jewish Interpretive Trajectory.** Philo (*Life of Moses* 2.71–160, *Questions on Exodus* 2) read the tabernacle's every element symbolically: the four colors of the veil represent the four elements (linen = earth, purple = water, scarlet = fire, blue = air), a reading echoed by Josephus (*Antiquities* 3.179–187), who adds that the twelve loaves of the Table correspond to the twelve months and the seven lamps of the Menorah to the seven planets. The tabernacle is a cosmos-in-miniature. Ben Sira 45:6–22 treats the Aaronic line as eternal; the Temple Scroll 11Q19 provides an alternate temple plan; Jubilees assimilates tabernacle to Eden.

Rabbinic interpretation treats tabernacle and temple as overlapping but distinct. Mishnah Middot describes Herod's temple; Mishnah Tamid narrates the daily service; Mishnah Yoma narrates the Day of Atonement when the high priest alone penetrated the *pārōket*. The Mekhilta de-Rabbi Ishmael preserves R. Akiva's claim that the *šĕkînâ* went into exile with Israel. Rashi focuses on construction details and frame arithmetic; Ibn Ezra adds linguistic analysis. Ramban advances the crucial theological reading: the tabernacle is the continuation of Sinai — a portable Sinai organized into three graded zones (summit, slopes, camp) mapped onto Holy of Holies, Holy Place, and court. Maimonides' *Mishneh Torah* codifies the laws in *Hilchot Beit HaBechira* and *Hilchot Kli HaMikdash*, treating tabernacle and temple as legally continuous.

Modern Jewish scholarship is extraordinary. Sarna's JPS *Exodus* (1991); Milgrom's three-volume *Leviticus* (1991–2001); Haran, Levenson (*Sinai and Zion*, *Creation and the Persistence of Evil*), Knohl, Carol Meyers (especially on the menorah), and Benjamin Sommer's *The Bodies of God* (2009, controversial) form one of the richest bodies of religious scholarship anywhere.

**Christian Interpretive Trajectory.** Origen's *Homilies on Exodus* 9–13 is the foundational Christian allegory: the inner linen and blue/purple/scarlet is the righteousness of Christ, the goats' hair covering is the human weakness he assumed, the rams' skins dyed red are his blood. Gregory of Nyssa's *Life of Moses* 2 reads the tabernacle as the pattern of mystical ascent. Augustine's *Questions on the Heptateuch* supplies detailed exegesis; Jerome's Letter 64 to Fabiola; Chrysostom's homilies on Hebrews. Medieval: Aquinas's *Summa Theologiae* I-II q. 102 (twenty-one articles on ceremonial precepts) offers perhaps the most sophisticated rational framework ever attempted — every detail has both *ratio litteralis* and *ratio figuralis*. Bonaventure's *Itinerarium Mentis in Deum* uses tabernacle imagery for the soul's ascent.

Reformation reading shifts. Luther's Hebrews commentary reads typologically but warns against allegorical excess. Calvin (*Institutes* 2.8; *Commentary on the Four Last Books of Moses*) polemicizes against Origen's heirs — not because the tabernacle lacks typological meaning, but because he judges them to have imported Christological meanings the text cannot bear. The tabernacle points to Christ only in ways controlled by the NT's own typology (Hebrews 9–10). This "controlled typology" becomes standard Reformed exegesis.

Critical: Wellhausen's retrojection thesis; Julius Popper; Milgrom's response; Michael Morales's *Who Shall Ascend to the Hill of the LORD?*; Fretheim's Interpretation commentary; N. T. Wright on temple theology as the spine of Pauline Christology; Brueggemann; T. Desmond Alexander's *From Eden to the New Jerusalem*.

**Where they converge and diverge.** Both Jewish and Christian traditions agree the tabernacle is where God dwells with Israel and that Sinai is its counterpart. They diverge on what comes next. Jewish interpretation preserves the *miškān* as sacrament and symbol: the synagogue with Torah ark becomes the portable shrine of a people in diaspora; the *šĕkînâ* goes with the people. Christian interpretation reads the tabernacle as fulfilled in Christ's body and the Church — John 1:14's *eskēnōsen* (God "tabernacled" among us) deliberately invokes the *miškān*. Catholic sacramental theology preserves a strong tabernacle-in-the-tabernacle (the eucharistic reserve). Orthodox liturgical theology (Schmemann's *For the Life of the World*; Zizioulas on liturgical ontology) reads the earthly liturgy as participating in the heavenly sanctuary. Reformed theology reads typologically and sees the tabernacle as superseded in Christ. What no one can deny: the tabernacle's theology of graded holy space, of divine presence entering history, of a structure that models the cosmos, has never gone away.

## Lens 4 — The Theological Intent

**Tension 1: How literal is the "pattern shown on the mountain" (26:30)?** Is the tabernacle an earthly replica of a heavenly original, or a purely symbolic structure?

*Patristic-Platonic (especially Philo, Hebrews 8:5, Origen):* The tabernacle is a copy of a heavenly archetype. Philo reads this through Platonic Forms; Hebrews 8:5 cites Exodus 25:40 explicitly — "See that you make them according to the pattern which was shown to you on the mountain" — and concludes Moses saw a heavenly sanctuary of which the earthly was a "copy and shadow." Origen extends the logic: every earthly element has a heavenly counterpart.

*Reformed:* Calvin restrains the Platonic impulse. Yes, Hebrews says what it says, but the "pattern" is a blueprint, not a metaphysical archetype. The tabernacle is God's accommodation to human capacity, not a metaphysical two-story universe.

*Rabbinic (Ramban):* The pattern is revelation, but the referent is Sinai itself. The tabernacle replicates what Moses experienced on the mountain — gradations of approach, divine speech, covenant. The tabernacle is *extended Sinai*, not *imitated heaven*.

*Maimonidean:* In *Guide* 3.45, Maimonides offers a very different reading: the tabernacle is not a metaphysical archetype at all but a pedagogical concession to a people recently removed from Egyptian idolatry, who could not conceive of worship without a structured sacred space. God gave the people a sanctuary so they would not demand idols. This reading is startling and famously deflationary.

*Steelman the least intuitive (Maimonides):* If the tabernacle's theology were truly metaphysical — a heavenly archetype replicated on earth — we would expect more biblical and rabbinic engagement with that archetype itself. Instead, the texts repeatedly re-describe the *earthly* structure and its liturgy, while the heavens above remain largely unfurnished in Torah. Maimonides' pedagogical reading explains this textual reticence: the *miškān* is valuable precisely because God's people needed a focal point, not because the universe has two floors. It preserves divine transcendence more rigorously than the Platonic reading can.

**Where this leaves us:** Hebrews 8:5 is canonical for Christians, which rules out full Maimonidean deflation; but Calvin and Ramban are probably right that Christian Platonism has often over-read what is a pattern of *relationship*, not *ontology*. The tabernacle is not heaven's twin; it is the ordered means by which a holy God dwells in covenant among a people. How God can dwell on earth without ceasing to be holy is the question the tabernacle is built to answer.

**Tension 2: What is the theological weight of the veil?** Is the *pārōket* a barrier, an invitation, or both — and what changes when the temple veil tears at Christ's death (Matthew 27:51)?

*Catholic:* The veil marks the gradation of sacred approach; it protects holiness while making it locatable. Its rending signals the opening of the heavenly sanctuary in Christ (Hebrews 10:19–20), but does not abolish sacred space — the Mass still recapitulates temple approach. The CCC on liturgy (1174–1178) maintains graduated liturgical architecture.

*Reformed:* The veil is pure barrier, and its tearing is pure abolition. Christ is the new and living way; the old system is superseded. The regulative principle of worship (Westminster Confession 21) allows no new sacred architecture; all ordinances are as prescribed in Scripture.

*Orthodox:* The iconostasis is the veil's continuation. The veil was *never* pure barrier; it always both separated and made the holy present. Orthodox liturgy maintains this double function. Schmemann: the veil is not abolished but fulfilled; the Church celebrates inside the Holy of Holies.

*Jewish (Ramban, Levenson):* The veil safeguards what cannot otherwise be safely approached. The torn temple veil is a Christian claim that Judaism cannot accept; within the Jewish tradition, the *pārōket* remains, carried into synagogue architecture as the curtain over the Torah ark. The *šĕkînâ* is curtained because unmediated presence is lethal.

*Anabaptist:* All sacred architecture is idolatrous accommodation. The veil's tearing abolishes the category of sacred space altogether. The true temple is the gathered congregation.

*Steelman the Orthodox:* If the veil were purely barrier, its elaborate decoration (cherubim, fine linen, colored yarns) would be incomprehensible. One does not decorate a wall meant to exclude. The veil is *dressed* — which means it participates in what it also separates. Orthodox theology's claim that the iconostasis is simultaneously barrier and bridge reads the veil's actual textual presentation better than either a flat "barrier" or a flat "access" reading.

**Where this leaves us:** The veil is a theological object in tension with itself — made to separate, made to invite, made to guard, made to depict. Christians who take Matthew 27:51 seriously cannot return to a pure-barrier theology; Jews who preserve the *pārōket* in synagogue practice cannot accept a pure-abolition theology; the category of graduated sacred space probably cannot be wished away by any tradition without loss.

**Unresolved question:** If the tabernacle's architecture encodes both separation and invitation — if the veil is dressed with cherubim both to bar and to depict the throne-room it conceals — then what are we to make of the modern impulse, common in Protestant and secular worship spaces alike, to strip sacred architecture down to bare utility? Have we achieved a theological advance or lost a pedagogy the tabernacle was designed to teach?

## The Thread

Three non-obvious cross-references.

*First,* the measurements generate a Holy of Holies that is a perfect ten-cubit cube (half the tabernacle's twenty-cubit interior behind the veil). This cube recurs in Solomon's temple (1 Kings 6:20), recurs again in the New Jerusalem (Revelation 21:16, cubed to twelve thousand stadia), and is absent from Ezekiel's future temple, which keeps the graduated holiness but alters the proportions. The cube is where heaven meets earth. When Revelation abolishes the temple but keeps the cube, the whole city is the Holy of Holies.

*Second,* the cherubim on the inner curtain (26:1) reappear woven on the veil (26:31), surmount the mercy seat (Exodus 25:18–22), and guard the way to the tree of life in Eden (Genesis 3:24). The tabernacle is not a post-Edenic invention; it is a carefully ordered re-entry into Eden's geography. The priest going behind the veil reverses the expulsion, momentarily, under strict conditions. This is why the cherubim iconography saturates Ezekiel's throne-chariot vision: the tabernacle/temple is the world's navel, where Eden has been made approachable by sacrifice.

*Third,* the acacia wood (*ʿăṣê šiṭṭîm*, 26:15). Acacia grows in the Sinai and the Aravah, not in the forests of Lebanon; it is what the wilderness provides. When Solomon builds the temple, he imports cedar of Lebanon (1 Kings 5–6), and the shift is not purely practical. A portable shrine of wilderness wood becomes a permanent temple of imperial timber, and something is gained and something is lost. Isaiah 41:19 prophesies a coming wilderness where YHWH will plant "the cedar, the acacia, the myrtle" — wilderness and imperial wood growing together. The tabernacle's acacia frames remember that God's first house was built from what the desert gave.
