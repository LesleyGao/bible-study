# Psalm 113 — A Scholarly Study

## Lens 1: The Historical World

Psalm 113 is anonymous and untitled in the Masoretic Text apart from the opening *halleluyah* (הַלְלוּיָהּ), which functions as a liturgical incipit. It belongs to Book V of the Psalter (Pss 107–150), and it stands at a position of unusual liturgical weight: **Psalm 113 opens the Egyptian Hallel** (*Hallel Mitzri*), the cluster Pss 113–118 sung at the three pilgrimage festivals (Passover, Shavuot, Sukkot) and especially at the Passover Seder. By the late Second Temple period the rabbinic division was fixed: Pss 113–114 were sung *before* the meal (over the second cup, after the *maggid* recital of Exodus); Pss 115–118 were sung *after* the meal (over the fourth cup). The Mishnah Pesahim 10 codifies this practice, and the synoptic Gospels almost certainly refer to it when they note that Jesus and the disciples "sang the hymn" (*hymnēsantes exēlthon*, ὑμνήσαντες ἐξῆλθον) before going to the Mount of Olives (Mark 14:26; Matthew 26:30). The hymn was the Hallel — and Psalm 113 stood at its head.

This liturgical context is decisive for everything else. The psalm is a **descriptive hymn** (Gunkel's *Hymnus*) in his form-critical taxonomy, and one of the purest specimens of the genre in the Psalter. Gunkel, in *Die Psalmen* (1926), distinguishes the descriptive hymn — which praises Yahweh for his enduring attributes and characteristic actions — from the declarative hymn, which celebrates a specific saving event. Psalm 113 is descriptive: Yahweh is praised for *who he is* and for what he characteristically does (raises the poor, gives the barren a home), without anchoring those acts in a particular historical episode. Sigmund Mowinckel (*The Psalms in Israel's Worship*, ET 1962) located such hymns in the autumn New Year festival, though most contemporary scholars (Hossfeld–Zenger, Goldingay, Kraus) loosen that cultic anchor and place Pss 113 broadly in Second Temple liturgy.

The psalm is generally dated to the post-exilic period (fifth to third centuries BCE) on the grounds of (a) its Aramaic-influenced phrasing in v. 5 (*ha-magbihi la-shavet*, with the unusual *hireq compaginis* yod), (b) the universal scope of v. 3 ("from the rising of the sun to its setting"), which reflects the diasporic horizon of post-exilic Yahwism, and (c) its position at the head of a Hallel cluster whose redactional shape is plainly post-exilic. Erhard Gerstenberger (*Psalms 2*, FOTL, 2001) reads it as synagogue-style hymnic instruction.

The opening line, *halleluyah halelu avdei Yahweh halelu et-shem Yahweh* (הַלְלוּיָהּ הַלְלוּ עַבְדֵי יְהוָה הַלְלוּ אֶת־שֵׁם יְהוָה, "praise the LORD! Praise, O servants of the LORD, praise the name of the LORD!" 113:1), uses the verb *hll* five times in three lines. The intensity is liturgical performance, not literary excess: it summons a congregation. The phrase *avdei Yahweh* ("servants of the LORD") in the Hallel context refers most naturally to the worshipping assembly of Israel, especially the Levitical singers, but with overtones of the prophetic *eved Yahweh* tradition (Isaiah 40–55).

The psalm's central theological declaration — *mi ka-Yahweh Eloheinu ha-magbihi la-shavet ha-mashpili lir'ot ba-shamayim u-va'aretz* (מִי כַּיהוָה אֱלֹהֵינוּ הַמַּגְבִּיהִי לָשָׁבֶת הַמַּשְׁפִּילִי לִרְאוֹת בַּשָּׁמַיִם וּבָאָרֶץ, "who is like the LORD our God, seated on high, who stoops down to look on the heavens and the earth?" vv. 5–6) — encodes the Hebrew Bible's distinctive theology of divine transcendence-and-condescension. The very God enthroned above the heavens stoops to attend to the *dal* and the *evyon* (poor and needy) on the ash heap. The closing image, *moshivi 'aqeret ha-bayit em ha-banim semechah* (מוֹשִׁיבִי עֲקֶרֶת הַבַּיִת אֵם הַבָּנִים שְׂמֵחָה, "who gives the barren woman a home, the joyful mother of children," v. 9), echoes the Hannah narrative (1 Sam 1–2) and Sarah's late-life conception (Gen 21).

ANE parallels: the descriptive hymn was a major genre across the ancient Near East. Babylonian hymns to Marduk and Egyptian hymns to Amun-Re both praise the god's transcendence ("dweller in heaven") and his attention to the lowly, but the specific combination in Ps 113 — the *enthroned-yet-stooping* God who reverses the fortunes of the *dal* — is distinctively Israelite. The closer parallels are Hannah's prayer (1 Sam 2:1–10), Mary's Magnificat (Luke 1:46–55), and Ps 138:6 (*ki ram Yahweh ve-shafal yir'eh*, "though the LORD is high, he regards the lowly"). DSS evidence is fragmentary; the Hallel cluster appears in 4QPs(b) and is implied by the Mishnaic citations.

## Lens 2: The Literature

Psalm 113 is built with extraordinary architectural symmetry, and modern commentators (Auffret, Hossfeld–Zenger, Alter) have pointed to its three-part design. Verses 1–3 form an opening call to praise. Verses 4–6 form the central hymnic body declaring Yahweh's incomparability, transcendence, and condescension. Verses 7–9 form the closing description of his characteristic acts of reversal. The opening and closing *halleluyah* form a clear inclusio.

Robert Lowth's parallelism categories appear throughout. Synonymous parallelism dominates the opening summons (vv. 1–3): "Praise, O servants of the LORD, / praise the name of the LORD!" The central section (vv. 4–6) uses synthetic parallelism that builds upward then downward: high above the nations / above the heavens (v. 4) → seated on high (v. 5) → stoops down to look (v. 6). This vertical movement is the psalm's signature. The closing section (vv. 7–9) uses synonymous parallelism with concrete pairings: *dal* and *evyon* (poor / needy) in v. 7; *afar* and *ashpot* (dust / ash heap) in v. 7; *nedivim* and *nedivei amo* (princes / princes of his people) in v. 8.

Robert Alter (*The Book of Psalms*, 2007) calls attention to the unusual *hireq compaginis* yod on the participles in vv. 5–9: *ha-magbihi*, *ha-mashpili*, *meqimi*, *moshivi*. This archaic ending, frequent in poetic registers, gives the lines a stately, elevated cadence — a deliberate liturgical archaism appropriate to the Hallel's solemnity. The participles are present-iterative: Yahweh is the One who *characteristically* enthrones himself on high, *characteristically* stoops, *characteristically* lifts.

There is no *selah* in Psalm 113, consistent with its Book V Hallel context. The strophic structure (3+3+3 verses) is mathematically tidy, though v. 9 is metrically slightly longer than the surrounding verses, giving the psalm a long final tone — a closing flourish on the barren-woman-as-joyful-mother image.

A subtler chiastic pattern organizes the psalm at the macro level. Verses 1–3 move from worshipper to Name; verses 4–6 from heavens to earth; verses 7–9 from ash heap to home. The vertical axis (high → low → low-lifted-high) intersects with the temporal axis (now / forever, sunrise / sunset) and the social axis (servants / princes / mothers). The whole psalm is an exercise in showing what Yahweh's transcendence *looks like in practice*: it looks like reversal.

## Lens 3: The Larger Story

Within Wilson's canonical-shape reading of Book V (*The Editing of the Hebrew Psalter*, 1985), Pss 111–112 form a wisdom-acrostic prologue and Pss 113–118 form the Egyptian Hallel proper. Hossfeld–Zenger (*Psalms 3*, Hermeneia, 2011) and Mitchell (*The Message of the Psalter*, 1997) read the Hallel as the post-exilic community's liturgical reaffirmation of the Exodus paradigm: in singing Pss 113–118 at Passover, the Second Temple community rehearses the Yahwistic claim that the God who raised Israel from Egyptian slavery is the same God who continues to raise the *dal* from the dust.

The **Jewish trajectory** is among the richest of any psalm. Mishnah Pesahim 10 prescribes the singing of Pss 113–114 before the meal at the Seder, over the second cup; Pss 115–118 after the meal, over the fourth cup. The Babylonian Talmud (Pesahim 117–118) preserves debate between the Houses of Hillel and Shammai over the precise division. *Midrash Tehillim* on Ps 113 connects the "barren woman" of v. 9 to the matriarchs Sarah, Rebekah, Rachel, Hannah, and the unnamed Shunammite — all reversal narratives. Rashi reads the *avdei Yahweh* of v. 1 as Israel after the Exodus, freed from being *avdei Par'oh*. Ibn Ezra emphasizes the universal scope of v. 3 ("from sunrise to sunset") as a polemic against localized cult: Yahweh is to be praised everywhere. Radak (David Kimhi) treats the participles of vv. 5–9 as ongoing actions, not past events: Yahweh continues, in every generation, to raise the lowly. Modern Jewish readings (Buber, Heschel, Levenson, Sarna) emphasize the Hallel's role as the heart of Passover liturgy. Heschel especially loved the *enthroned-yet-stooping* image as the concentrated form of biblical theology — *gevurah* and *chesed* together.

The Magnificat link runs through Hannah's prayer in 1 Samuel 2:1–10, which contains the closest verbal parallel to Ps 113 in the entire Hebrew Bible: *meqim me-afar dal me-ashpot yarim evyon le-hoshiv im-nedivim* (1 Sam 2:8 — "he raises the poor from the dust, lifts the needy from the ash heap, to seat them with princes"). The verse is virtually a citation. Mary's Magnificat (Luke 1:46–55) then echoes both: "He has brought down the mighty from their thrones / and exalted those of humble estate; / he has filled the hungry with good things / and the rich he has sent away empty" (Luke 1:52–53). Whether or not the Lukan composer self-consciously knit together Hannah and Ps 113, the result is a triple-strand cord — Hannah, Ps 113, Magnificat — running through Israel's prayer book and Christian liturgy as the canonical theology of the *anavim*, the lowly whom God lifts.

The **Christian trajectory** unfolds along this Magnificat axis. Augustine (*Enarrationes in Psalmos*) reads Ps 113 as the church's Easter song, with the *enthroned-yet-stooping* God as the preincarnate Christ who would later stoop in the Incarnation. Cassiodorus develops this Christologically: the *ha-magbihi* / *ha-mashpili* pair is the Philippians 2 hymn in nuce. Aquinas (*Postilla super Psalmos*) reads the psalm as praise of divine condescension. Luther's reading concentrates on the *Magnificat* connection — Mary, in his treatise *Das Magnificat verdeutscht und ausgelegt* (1521), is Hannah's heir and Ps 113's exemplar. Calvin emphasizes the universal scope of v. 3 as warrant for the Reformed mission to all peoples. Liturgically, the Magnificat has been sung daily at Catholic Vespers since the early medieval period, at Anglican Evensong since Cranmer's first Book of Common Prayer (1549), and in Lutheran and Reformed offices ever since — meaning that the theology of Psalm 113, channeled through Mary, is the theology millions of Christians have prayed every evening for fifteen hundred years.

Among critical moderns, Hans-Joachim Kraus (*Psalmen*, BKAT) classifies Ps 113 as a hymnic "praise of Yahweh's name" psalm. John Goldingay (*Psalms 90–150*, BCOTWP, 2008) emphasizes the participial mood: Yahweh's lifting of the poor is not a single past act but a characteristic divine action, ongoing. Marvin Tate, James Mays, and Patrick Miller all stress the social-justice grain. Beth Tanner reads the psalm as the post-exilic community's affirmation that exile, ash heap, and barrenness are not the final word. Walter Brueggemann (*The Message of the Psalms*, 1984) treats the Hallel cluster as Israel's "doxology of orientation" reaffirmed *after* the disorientation of exile — a chastened, post-traumatic praise.

## Lens 4: The Theological Intent

The central theological tension of Psalm 113 lies in the relationship between **divine transcendence** and **divine condescension**: the God enthroned above the heavens (vv. 4–5) is the same God who stoops to the ash heap (vv. 6–7). How are these held together, and what does the holding mean?

**Position 1: The classical theist reading (Augustine, Aquinas, the Reformed tradition).** The transcendence of v. 4 and the condescension of v. 6 are coherent because Yahweh's height is precisely what enables his stooping. Only the One enthroned above the heavens has the moral altitude to attend to the ash heap without being himself reduced to it. Steelman: the theology of Ps 113 is the deep grammar of the Christian doctrine of God — *immanent because transcendent*. The Incarnation, in this reading, is the eschatological intensification of v. 6, not its contradiction. Aquinas's distinction between God's *per essentiam* presence (he is everywhere by his essence) and his *per gratiam* presence (he draws near to creatures by grace) maps onto the psalm's vertical movement.

**Position 2: The liberation-theology reading (Gutiérrez, Brueggemann, Tanner).** Psalm 113 is not abstract ontology but concrete politics. The God who raises the *dal* from the *afar* is the God whose characteristic activity is the reversal of imperial hierarchy. To praise this God is to commit oneself to that reversal; to refuse the reversal is to praise a different god. Steelman: the Magnificat-Hannah-Ps 113 axis is the canonical heart of biblical theology, and any reading that softens it into mere "spiritual" lifting evades the text. The barren-mother image (v. 9) is itself a social-economic claim in a culture where childlessness was destitution.

**Position 3: The cultic-liturgical reading (Mowinckel, Kraus, Hossfeld–Zenger).** Psalm 113 is first and foremost a hymn for the Passover Seder. Its theological function is not to argue but to *form* — to shape the worshipping community's imagination at the festival of liberation, so that the memory of Egypt and the present ash heap and the future redemption are held together in a single song. Steelman: theological readings that abstract Ps 113 from its Hallel context lose half its force. The psalm is sung in a specific place, at a specific table, by a specific people drinking the second cup of wine, and the theology only fully discloses itself in that practice.

**Position 4: The Christological-Magnificat reading (Augustine, Luther, the Catholic-Anglican Vespers tradition).** Psalm 113, mediated through Hannah, is taken up by Mary at the Annunciation, and the Magnificat becomes the church's daily echo of the same theology. The God of Ps 113 is the God whose ultimate stooping is the Incarnation — "though he was in the form of God ... he emptied himself" (Phil 2:6–7). Steelman: this is not a forced typology. The Lukan composer plainly knew Ps 113 and Hannah, and the daily liturgical use of the Magnificat for fifteen hundred years confirms the church's reception of Ps 113 as Christological. The psalm is sung *forward* into the Incarnation, not merely *backward* into Israel's ash heap.

**Where this leaves us:** Psalm 113 is the most concentrated form in the Psalter of the biblical theology of reversal. The same God enthroned above the heavens stoops to the ash heap; the same God who is high lifts the low. To pray this psalm — at Passover, at Vespers, at any moment of human need — is to commit one's imagination to a particular kind of transcendence: not a transcendence that withdraws, but a transcendence whose very height is condescension. Jesus and the disciples sang this psalm together at the Last Supper, just before the night that would test that theology to its breaking point. They sang of the God who raises the poor from the dust, hours before one of them would be raised from a tomb. The Hallel context is not background; it is foreground.

The closing question is what kind of God we have praised today, and whether that God stoops. The barren-mother-becomes-joyful-mother of v. 9 is a small image, but it is the psalm's last word, and it carries the whole theological weight: God's transcendence is most clearly seen not in distance but in the specific, embodied, social reversal of human destitution. A theology that does not bend down has not yet read v. 6.
