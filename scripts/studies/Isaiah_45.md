# Isaiah 45 — Going Deeper

## Lens 1: The Historical World Behind the Text

Isaiah 45 sits inside Second Isaiah (chapters 40–55), the second of the three blocks into which mainstream-critical scholarship has divided the book since Bernhard Duhm's 1892 commentary (*Das Buch Jesaia*). On Duhm's reading — the standard view in critical scholarship for over a century — chapters 40–55 are the work of an anonymous prophet active in Babylon during the closing years of the exile, around 545–540 BC, on the eve of Cyrus's conquest of Babylon in 539 BC. Chapter 45 is the chapter that more than any other anchors this dating: it names Cyrus the Persian by name (45:1), addresses him as a living political figure on the verge of his triumphs, and presupposes an audience in exile waiting for deliverance. Mainstream-critical scholars — Westermann, Blenkinsopp, Goldingay, Melugin, Tull, Whybray, Muilenburg — read the chapter as a real-time prophetic interpretation of a world about to change.

The unified-defense tradition (Motyer, Oswalt, Young, conservative evangelical scholarship) reads the Cyrus naming as predictive prophecy from the historical eighth-century Isaiah ben Amoz, given roughly 150 years before the events. The Childs canonical-criticism position sits between: whatever the date of composition, the canonical book stands as the unified prophetic word of "Isaiah," and chapters 40–55 must be read both within their exilic situation and within the larger book.

The historical anchor outside the text is the **Cyrus Cylinder** (British Museum BM 90920), the clay barrel-cylinder discovered at Babylon in 1879, on which Cyrus describes his 539 BC entry into Babylon as the work of *Marduk*, who chose him to set right the wrongs of the previous king Nabonidus. The cylinder presents Cyrus as the divinely chosen restorer of displaced peoples and the rebuilder of sanctuaries. Isaiah 45 makes the parallel theological claim — but with Yahweh, not Marduk, as the deity who has chosen Cyrus. This is the chapter's signature provocation: Cyrus is going to win, his propaganda will say Marduk did it, but the prophet says Yahweh did it, and Yahweh did it for Israel's sake.

The textual witness is unusually strong. The **Great Isaiah Scroll (1QIsa^a)** from Qumran Cave 1, dated paleographically to roughly 125 BC, preserves the full chapter, with only minor orthographic differences from the Masoretic Text. The Cyrus oracle was not a late Christian or rabbinic insertion — it was already a fixed part of the book by the late second century BC.

The Hebrew vocabulary carries the chapter's theological weight. The opening phrase **"to his anointed, to Cyrus"** is in Hebrew *li-meshicho le-Khoresh* (לִמְשִׁיחוֹ לְכוֹרֶשׁ) — and *meshiach* is the word elsewhere reserved for Israelite kings, priests, and (later) the eschatological Messiah. **Cyrus is the only non-Israelite ever called Yahweh's *meshiach* in the Hebrew Bible.** The shock-value of this is hard to overstate: a Persian polytheist who does not know Yahweh (45:4–5: *though you do not know me*) is given the title that was the most loaded political-religious term in the Israelite vocabulary.

The radical-monotheism formula appears repeatedly: *ani Yahweh ve-ein od* (אֲנִי יְהוָה וְאֵין עוֹד), *I am the LORD, and there is no other* (45:5, 6, 18, 21, 22). The most contested verse is 45:7, *yotzer or u-vore choshekh, oseh shalom u-vore ra* (יוֹצֵר אוֹר וּבוֹרֵא חֹשֶׁךְ עֹשֶׂה שָׁלוֹם וּבוֹרֵא רָע) — *I form light and create darkness, I make weal and create woe* (ESV) or, more literally, *I make peace and create evil*. The verbs *yotzer* (form) and *bara* (create — the same verb as Genesis 1:1) are deliberate Genesis-echoes. *Ra* can mean moral evil, calamity, disaster, or simply harm.

## Lens 2: The Literature

Chapter 45 is layered prophetic poetry working in three distinct genres simultaneously. **The Cyrus oracle (45:1–8)** is a royal-investiture speech — the sort of address an ancient Near Eastern god would deliver to a chosen king at his enthronement. The form is borrowed directly from Babylonian and Persian royal rhetoric (the Cyrus Cylinder uses the same form for Marduk's address to Cyrus), but with Yahweh in the speaker's seat. **The trial-speech and disputation (45:9–13, 20–25)** belong to the *rib* (court-case) genre that runs throughout Second Isaiah, in which Yahweh argues a case against the nations and their gods. **The hymn fragment (45:8)** is a brief liturgical interruption — the heavens commanded to rain down righteousness — that punctuates the longer argument with worship.

The poetic structure is built around the *ein od* refrain. The phrase *and there is no other* recurs five times (45:5, 6, 14, 18, 22) and functions as the chapter's formal spine. Between the refrains, the prophet builds the argument by stages: (1) Yahweh names Cyrus and grasps his hand (45:1–4); (2) the cosmic claim that Yahweh forms light and creates darkness (45:5–7); (3) the woe-oracles against those who challenge the potter (45:9–11); (4) the universal invitation, *turn to me and be saved, all the ends of the earth* (45:22); (5) the climactic oath, *to me every knee shall bow, every tongue shall swear allegiance* (45:23).

The potter-clay imagery in 45:9–11 — *Does the clay say to him who forms it, "What are you making?"* — is the source-text Paul reaches for in Romans 9:20. The *hidden God* statement of 45:15 (*Truly, you are a God who hides himself, O God of Israel, the Savior*) became the title-text for Pascal's *Deus absconditus* meditation and for Luther's theology of the cross.

## Lens 3: Inside the Larger Story

**Within the prophetic canon**, Isaiah 45 belongs to the chapters in which Israel's exile is reframed as the proving-ground for radical monotheism. In the pre-exilic prophets, Yahweh is one god among others — superior, sovereign, but in a crowded divine field. By Isaiah 40–55, the gods of the nations are not merely defeated but ontologically denied: there are no other gods. Whether this represents a development in Israel's theology (Mark S. Smith's *The Origins of Biblical Monotheism*) or a fresh articulation of an older insight (Richard Bauckham, John Goldingay), the language is unmistakable: *besides me there is no God*.

**Jewish trajectory**: The Cyrus oracle was foundational for the Second Temple period's positive memory of the Persian Empire. The Chronicler ends his history with Cyrus's decree (2 Chronicles 36:22–23); Ezra opens the same way (Ezra 1:1–4). Josephus (*Antiquities* 11.1.2) records the tradition that Cyrus was shown the book of Isaiah and recognized himself in it, which moved him to release the Jews. The rabbinic tradition is more ambivalent — Cyrus is praised but not idolized, and the title *meshiach* applied to a Gentile king is treated as a singular and bounded designation, not a precedent for messianic theology. The radical-monotheism formulas of Isaiah 45 became central to the Aleinu prayer's *ein od* refrain and to the daily liturgical confession of Jewish unity-of-God theology.

**Christian trajectory**: The chapter became one of the most heavily mined Christological texts in the Second Testament. Most importantly, **Philippians 2:10–11** — *that at the name of Jesus every knee should bow, in heaven and on earth and under the earth, and every tongue confess that Jesus Christ is Lord* — alludes directly to Isaiah 45:23, and **Romans 14:11** quotes the verse explicitly with reference to the judgment-seat of God. Mainstream NT scholarship (Bauckham's *God Crucified*, Hurtado's *Lord Jesus Christ*, N. T. Wright's *Paul and the Faithfulness of God*) reads the Philippians 2 hymn as one of the highest Christological texts in the New Testament: Paul takes the *every-knee-shall-bow-to-Yahweh* of Isaiah 45 — a passage whose entire point is that there is no other God — and applies it to Jesus. This is not a low-Christology accommodation; it is, on Bauckham's reading, the inclusion of Jesus within the unique divine identity of the God of Israel.

**Other NT echoes**: Romans 9:20–21 (*shall what is molded say to its molder, "Why have you made me like this?"*) directly takes up Isaiah 45:9. Acts 17:27–28 (Paul at the Areopagus on the *unknown God*) trades on the *God who hides himself* of Isaiah 45:15. The universal-invitation language of 45:22 (*turn to me and be saved, all the ends of the earth*) became one of the missional texts of the early church and was the verse that, famously, converted Charles Spurgeon as a teenager in a Primitive Methodist chapel in 1850.

## Lens 4: Theological Intent — Where the Tensions Sit

Three tensions deserve honest engagement.

**First, Cyrus as Yahweh's anointed.** The unified-defense tradition (Motyer, Oswalt) reads the Cyrus naming as predictive prophecy and uses it as an apologetic anchor for the supernatural inspiration of Scripture: only a God who knows the future could name a Persian king 150 years before his birth. The mainstream-critical tradition reads it as real-time interpretation: an anonymous exilic prophet recognized in Cyrus's rise the providential hand of Yahweh and preached it as such. The deeper question is the same on both readings: *can a polytheist who does not know Yahweh be Yahweh's instrument?* The text's answer is unambiguously yes. *I call you by your name, I name you, though you do not know me* (45:4). Catholic teaching since Vatican II's *Nostra Aetate* (1965) has drawn on this strand to articulate God's working outside the visible boundaries of the covenant people. Reformed common-grace theology (Bavinck, Berkhof, Kuyper) reads the Cyrus oracle as the paradigm of God's unwitting-instrument tradition: rulers and nations are bent to God's purposes whether they know it or not. The steelman of the secular reader is that this is theological retro-fitting — Cyrus's policy of repatriation was a standard Persian imperial strategy applied to many displaced peoples, not a special favor to Israel; the prophet baptizes a generic imperial pragmatism as Yahweh's specific intervention. That critique has force, but it does not dissolve the theological claim — it sharpens it. The prophet is not arguing that Cyrus was uniquely good to Israel. He is arguing that the same Cyrus whom Babylon will see as Marduk's chosen, and whom the historians will see as a successful Persian general, was in fact moved by the God of Israel for Israel's sake. The claim is metaphysical, not strategic.

**Second, the theodicy of 45:7 — *I form light and create darkness, I make weal and create woe.*** This is one of the most theologically loaded sentences in the Hebrew Bible. The interpretive options run a wide spectrum. (a) The **Augustinian privation-of-good** tradition reads *bara ra* not as God creating moral evil but as God ordaining the calamity that is the consequence of evil — evil itself is a privation of being, not a created thing. (b) The **Reformed sovereignty** tradition (Calvin, *Institutes* I.18; Edwards) reads 45:7 plainly as God's comprehensive sovereignty over all that comes to pass, including disaster, while distinguishing God's *ordaining* will from his *moral* will. (c) The **open-theism** tradition (Greg Boyd, *God at War*) reads the verse as God's sovereignty over judgment-disaster (the *ra* directed at Babylon, not over moral evil in general), insisting that God does not author moral evil. (d) The **mainstream-critical** reading (Westermann, Goldingay) emphasizes the polemical context: the verse rebuts Persian Zoroastrian dualism (Ahura Mazda as the god of light, Angra Mainyu as the god of darkness) by insisting that one God stands behind both light and darkness, weal and woe. Some scholars (Joel Burnett, Mark S. Smith) push further and ask whether the verse is, strictly speaking, monism — a one-source-of-everything claim — rather than monotheism in the later philosophical sense. The honest position is that the verse cannot be made comfortable. The God of Isaiah 45 is the God who claims responsibility for the calamity that fell on Babylon and, by extension, for the calamity that fell on Israel itself. The exilic context is the key: the prophet is preaching to people whose city was burned, whose temple was destroyed, whose king was blinded and dragged into captivity. To them he says — *I, Yahweh, did this, and I am the only one who could have, and I am about to undo it.* The theodicy is not theoretical; it is the only form of comfort the prophet has.

**Third, the Christological appropriation.** Did Paul mean to say that Jesus is Yahweh? The mainstream NT-scholarly answer, across confessional lines, is yes — though the categories of Nicene Trinitarianism are still three centuries downstream. Bauckham's argument in *God Crucified* is that Paul takes the most uncompromisingly monotheistic text in the Hebrew Bible and applies its climactic oath — *to me every knee shall bow* — to Jesus, and he does so without apology and without softening. Larry Hurtado's parallel argument is that the early Christian devotional pattern, including singing the Philippians 2 hymn, is a "binitarian shape" of monotheism in which Jesus is included within the unique identity of Israel's God. Jewish responses (Boyarin, Levenson, Daniel Boyarin's *The Jewish Gospels*) note that Second Temple Judaism had multiple resources for high-divine-figure speculation (the Son of Man tradition, the Logos of Philo, the heavenly Adam), and that the Christian application of Isaiah 45:23 to Jesus is intelligible within that landscape — not necessarily the betrayal of monotheism that later polemic made it out to be.

**Where this leaves us**: Isaiah 45 is the chapter that most fully discloses Second Isaiah's theological program. There is one God. He is sovereign over light and darkness, weal and woe. He works through pagan kings without their knowledge. He invites the ends of the earth to turn and be saved. And he swears, by himself, that every knee will bow. The chapter resists every attempt to make God smaller — smaller than the Persian Empire, smaller than the problem of evil, smaller than the nations, smaller than the cross. It does not solve the problems it raises. It refuses to.

**Closing question**: If the God who chose Cyrus the polytheist is the same God who claims sovereignty over calamity itself, how should we read the rise and fall of empires in our own time — and which calamities are we willing to attribute to Him?
