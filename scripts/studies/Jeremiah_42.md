# Jeremiah 42: The Asking That Was Not Listening

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

**Political situation**: The chapter occurs in the brief, terrified interval between Ishmael's assassination of Gedaliah (chapter 41, Tishri 587 BC) and the survivors' flight into Egypt (chapter 43). Johanan ben Kareah and the surviving captains have recovered the Mizpah captives at the great pool of Gibeon and now camp at Geruth Chimham near Bethlehem (41:17), poised on the road south. They have one functional asset: the prophet Jeremiah, who has lived through five reigns — Josiah, Jehoahaz, Jehoiakim, Jehoiachin, Zedekiah — and whose forty-year preaching has just been historically vindicated by Babylon's victory. Their political situation is acute. Nebuzaradan (cf. 39:9–14, 40:1–6) has already departed for Babylon with the bulk of the deportees; the murder of his hand-picked governor by a Davidide will, by any reasonable expectation, trigger a punitive return. The Babylonian Chronicle (BM 21946, in Donald Wiseman's edition) and Josephus (*Ant.* 10.181–82) both record Nebuchadnezzar's later Levantine campaigns; the survivors are not paranoid to fear reprisal.

**Material culture**: Geruth Chimham (גֵּרוּת כִּמְהָם, 41:17) is plausibly an estate near Bethlehem that preserved, for almost four hundred years, the name of Chimham son of Barzillai the Gileadite — the young man David rewarded with a Davidic land-grant after Absalom's revolt (cf. 2 Sam 19:37–40). The estate's survival as a toponym is a small but striking instance of the durability of Iron Age land-grants in the southern Judean hills, a phenomenon paralleled in the Khirbet Beit Lei tomb inscriptions and the Lachish ostraca's references to inherited holdings. The chapter's reference to "Egypt, where we shall not see war or hear the sound of the trumpet or be hungry for bread" (42:14) reflects the political fact that 26th-Dynasty Saite Egypt under Pharaoh Apries (Hophra, 589–570 BC) was actively recruiting Jewish refugees as garrison troops — a recruitment program the Elephantine papyri later document continuing into the Persian period at the Yeb military colony at Aswan. The "sound of the trumpet" (*qol shofar*) is the alarm-trumpet of the city watch, the same word that frames Jeremiah's call vision in Jer 4:19.

**Authorship & dating**: The chapter belongs to Mowinckel's biographical "B source," and its prose style and theological vocabulary place it inside the Deuteronomistic editorial frame the prose oracles of Jeremiah share with the books of Kings. The "build up and not pull down, plant and not pluck up" formula in 42:10 is the inversion of Jeremiah's call language in 1:10 — a deliberate editorial bracket. The MT and LXX agree closely here, with the LXX of the prose chapters being noticeably less divergent from the MT than the LXX of the early poetic oracles. Lundbom (Anchor) treats the chapter as substantially historical and as a coherent unit with chapters 40, 41, and 43. Holladay (Hermeneia) reads it as a Deuteronomistic theological set-piece on the obedient-asking-disobedient-answering pattern. Carroll (OTL) is more skeptical of historicity. McKane (ICC), Bright, Stulman, and Brueggemann all treat the chapter as historically rooted and theologically central. Kathleen O'Connor (*Jeremiah: Pain and Promise*) reads chapters 40–44 as trauma narrative.

**Archaeological & textual evidence**: Tell en-Nasbeh (Mizpah; Badè 1926–35; Zorn's restudy 2003) for the political center the survivors have just abandoned. The Pritchard excavations at el-Jib (1956–62) for the great pool of Gibeon mentioned in 41:12 as the survivors' rendezvous before reaching Geruth Chimham. The Saqqara papyri and the Elephantine archive (Cowley collection, 5th c. BC) for the Egyptian Jewish military-colony pattern the survivors are about to enter. The Babylonian Chronicle for the imperial frame. The Lachish ostraca for late-Judahite scribal Hebrew.

**Key terms**: *tehinnah* (תְּחִנָּה, "plea for mercy," 42:2) — the formal language of supplication, used in Solomon's Temple-dedication prayer (1 Kgs 8:28) and now turned toward the prophet as mediator after the Temple's destruction. *aḥarey eser yamim* (אַחֲרֵי עֲשֶׂרֶת יָמִים, "at the end of ten days," 42:7) — the prophetic delay. *ed emet ve-ne'eman* (עֵד אֱמֶת וְנֶאֱמָן, "true and faithful witness," 42:5) — covenant-oath formula, used here by the people in self-imprecation. *niḥamti al ha-ra'ah* (נִחַמְתִּי עַל-הָרָעָה, "I relent of the disaster," 42:10) — divine repentance language, the same root used of Yahweh in Jonah 3:10 and Joel 2:13.

## Lens 2 — The Literature

The chapter's literary architecture is one of the most carefully constructed in the prose tradition. It runs as a tight three-part oracle-frame: (1) the people's request and oath (42:1–6); (2) the prophetic delay and the oracle (42:7–18); (3) the prophet's prescient indictment (42:19–22). The architecture is mirrored: the people speak, the prophet promises, God delays, the prophet speaks, the prophet indicts. The oath in 42:5–6 is the linchpin. The people invoke YHWH as *ed emet ve-ne'eman* — true and faithful witness — *against themselves*, using the precise covenant-witness formula that the legal traditions reserve for treaty oaths (cf. Deut 4:26, 30:19; Josh 24:22). They then specify obedience in absolute terms: "whether it is good or bad, we will obey." This is the tightest possible self-binding, and the chapter's terrible irony depends on the reader noticing that the binding has already been broken in the *intent* of the asking.

The ten-day delay in 42:7 is a striking literary device. Hebrew prophetic literature contains very few comparable scenes of waiting. Habakkuk 2:1's watch is its closest parallel, but Habakkuk waits in poetic posture; here, Jeremiah simply waits, the narrator simply notes the delay, and we are left to guess at what fills the ten days. Lundbom and Holladay both note that the delay refuses the cliché of immediate prophetic access — Jeremiah does not have YHWH on speed-dial. The oracle itself (42:9–18) is a classic two-track conditional: stay-and-be-blessed or go-and-be-cursed. The "build up and not pull down, plant and not pluck up" of 42:10 deliberately reverses the four destructive verbs of Jeremiah's call (1:10), creating a bookend effect inside the prose tradition. The threat "you shall become an execration, a horror, a curse, and a taunt" (42:18) recycles classic Deuteronomistic curse-language (cf. Deut 28).

The closing verses (42:19–22) shift register sharply. The prophet now speaks not as oracle-deliverer but as forensic witness: "I have warned you this day, but you have not obeyed." The verbs are perfect-aspect: the disobedience is treated as already accomplished, before the action of departing for Egypt has occurred. This is the chapter's deepest literary move. The prophet *names the disobedience as fait accompli at the moment of the oracle's delivery*, because the people's hearts have already decided. Devices include the oath formula, the conditional oracle-form, the recycled curse-vocabulary, the prophetic delay, and the perfect-aspect indictment. MT and LXX of this chapter are very close.

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

In the canonical arc, Jeremiah 42 is the textbook chapter on *seeking the LORD without intent to obey*. Its position is structurally crucial: the survivors stand at a real fork — stay in Judah and inherit Jeremiah's restoration promises, or go to Egypt and undo the exodus. The chapter is the moment the door is still open and the people choose, by their inner disposition, to close it. The book of Jeremiah will end (chapter 52 excepted) with the prophet himself dragged into Egypt against his will (43:6), preaching one final oracle to Egyptian Jewish settlements (chapter 44). Chapter 42 is the hinge that determines that ending.

**Jewish trajectory**: Sirach 49:6–7 names Jeremiah as the prophet of "uprooting and afflicting and destroying, and again building and planting" — directly echoing the language of 42:10 and Jer 1:10. Targum Jonathan paraphrases the chapter with characteristic theological clarification (rendering the divine *niḥamti* as "I have turned back from the evil"). Rashi on 42:6 stresses that the people's oath-formula is binding precisely because it covers both *good* and *bad* outcomes — Rashi reads them as already self-condemned by the breadth of the oath. Radak emphasizes the ten-day wait as YHWH's mercy — the delay is grace. Ibn Ezra notes the legal structure of the *ed emet* witness-formula. Abrabanel, again writing as a Sephardi exile, reads the chapter as a meditation on the diaspora's perennial temptation to flee further rather than stay-and-trust. The Bavli (Sanhedrin 89a) cites the chapter in its discussion of false prophecy and prophetic accountability. The medieval mussar tradition reads the chapter as a paradigmatic case of *bittul ratzon* failure — the failure to annul one's own will to YHWH's even when ostensibly seeking guidance.

**Christian trajectory**: Origen's *Homilies on Jeremiah* (Hom. 18 on this passage in the Greek series, where preserved) read the ten-day delay allegorically as the time of self-examination the soul must undergo before God answers. Jerome's *Commentary on Jeremiah* (ad loc.) treats the chapter as a moral allegory on petitionary prayer — Jerome insists, with characteristic sharpness, that to ask God's will while having already decided is to make oneself the witness against oneself. Aquinas (*Summa* II-II q.83 a.7 on petitionary prayer; q.171–74 on prophecy) draws on this passage's ten-day delay to argue that prophetic knowledge depends on God's free communication, not the prophet's possession. Calvin's *Lectures on Jeremiah* (ad loc.) is particularly extensive on this chapter — he reads it as the definitive biblical case study in the disconnect between *outward seeking* and *inward submission*, and the lectures spend several pages on the question of how a prayer can be sincere in its words and damning in its disposition. The Reformed *Westminster Confession* on prayer (Ch. 21) and the Puritan tradition (Owen, Goodwin) develop the chapter's logic into a full doctrine of *prepared* prayer. Critical scholarship since Duhm reads the oracle as Deuteronomistic shaping; O'Connor reads it as trauma — the people *want* a different answer because they cannot bear the answer they fear, and the chapter's harshness must be read inside the trauma context.

**Where they diverge**: Jewish tradition treats the chapter primarily as halakhic-aggadic material on oath-taking and on prophetic mediation. Christian tradition has historically used the chapter as the central proof-text for the moral psychology of disobedient prayer. Critical scholarship reads it as a Deuteronomistic theological set-piece. They diverge on the *prophetic delay*: rabbinic readings tend to treat the ten days as mercy, patristic readings as preparation, Reformed readings as sovereignty, critical readings as narrative device.

## Lens 4 — The Theological Intent

**Tension 1: the ethics of seeking divine guidance.** The chapter sets up the deepest possible problem about asking. The people frame their petition in textbook form: they ask the prophet to pray, they invoke YHWH as witness, they oath obedience to whatever answer comes — "whether it is good or bad." Yet the prophet, in 42:19–22, treats the disobedience as already accomplished. This raises a question that has divided every theological tradition: *can* one seek God's will without already, in some sense, deciding what one wants to hear? The Catholic discernment tradition, especially Ignatius's *Spiritual Exercises* (the rules for discernment of spirits, weeks 1 and 2), treats this as the central problem of guidance — the *agere contra* discipline (acting against one's own attachment) is precisely an exercise in surfacing pre-decided answers. The Reformed tradition through Calvin's distinction between God's *secret* and *revealed* will (Institutes 1.17–18; 3.20 on prayer) handles this through the doctrine of preparation: one must first *will to obey* before one can rightly ask. Wesley's "open heart" preaching tradition addresses the same problem through a different vocabulary, emphasizing the affective transformation that must precede genuine petition. The Jewish tradition develops *bittul ratzon* (annulling one's will) and the *Mussar* literature on prayer-preparation. Steelman the most uncomfortable position: that human beings cannot, *in principle*, seek God's will except as already partly formed, and that the chapter's indictment is not of these particular people but of the universal human condition under what Augustine would call concupiscence and what the rabbis call *yetzer ha-ra*. On that reading, the chapter is not a story about a uniquely faithless remnant but about every petitioner. **Where this leaves us:** the chapter refuses the comfortable distinction between *sincere* and *insincere* asking and forces the question whether genuine seeking requires a prior formation of the will toward obedience.

**Tension 2: the ten-day prophetic delay.** Verse 7 is one of the most theologically unusual verses in the prophetic corpus: "At the end of ten days the word of the LORD came to Jeremiah." The detail is small, but it is doing a great deal of work. It tells us, against any caricature of prophecy as immediate hotline, that even Jeremiah waits. This raises a tension across traditions about prophetic *not-immediately-knowing*. The Catholic tradition, especially the contemplative-tradition strand that runs from the Desert Fathers through John Cassian to John of the Cross's *dark night*, reads divine silence as a positive feature of the spiritual life — the *waiting* itself shapes the soul to receive. The Orthodox tradition develops *hesychia* (stillness) as a contemplative discipline. The Reformed tradition through the Westminster *Confession* reads the chapter through the ordinary-means framework: the delay is sovereignty, not invitation to mystical waiting. The Jewish tradition has the *responsa* tradition of deliberation — answering hard questions slowly, with time. Critical readers see the verse as narrative device — building suspense before the oracle. Steelman the position one might dismiss: the contemplative reading. Even within a non-mystical theology of revelation, the chapter insists that the prophet *waits*, and the waiting is part of the answer's character. Had the answer come instantly, it would not have carried the weight that ten days of waiting impart. **Where this leaves us:** the verse refuses both the caricature of immediate prophetic access and the dismissal of contemplative waiting; it simply reports the delay and lets the theological traditions wrestle with what the delay means.

**Unresolved question:** If, as 42:19–22 strongly implies, the prophet *already knew* the people would not obey, what was the function of the oracle and the ten-day wait? The chapter seems to insist that the answer must be given even when the disobedience is foreknown — the people must hear the word that condemns them. What does this say about the purpose of prophetic speech when its addressees are not its intended hearers? Is the oracle for the doomed, or for the canonical reader watching them refuse it?

## The Thread

The pattern of *seeking the LORD with no intent to obey* threads from Saul's necromantic seeking at Endor (1 Sam 28:6–25) through Jeremiah 42 into the New Testament rich young ruler (Mt 19:16–22), where the asker frames a sincere question and walks away when the answer is "good or bad." James 1:6–8 ("the doubting one is like a wave of the sea") generalizes the logic. The "build up and plant, pull down and pluck up" formula links 1:10 with 42:10 as a deliberate canonical inclusio inside the prose tradition. The forbidden return to Egypt (Deut 17:16, Hos 8:13, Jer 42:14–18) drives forward into Jer 43–44 and resurfaces typologically in Matthew 2:13–15, where the holy family's flight to Egypt with the infant Jesus deliberately reverses the pattern. The ten-day prophetic delay echoes Habakkuk 2:1's watch and forward to Pentecost's ten-day wait between ascension and the Spirit's coming (Acts 1:3, 9; 2:1).
