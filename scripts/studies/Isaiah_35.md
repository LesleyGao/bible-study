# Isaiah 35 — Going Deeper

## Lens 1: The Historical World Behind the Text

Isaiah 35 is one of the most beloved and most disputed chapters in the entire book. With its companion chapter 34, it forms a diptych — chapter 34's cosmic-judgment-and-Edom-destruction paired with chapter 35's wilderness-blossoming-and-ransomed-return — and the question of when, by whom, and why this diptych was composed has occupied Isaiah scholarship for over a century.

The mainstream critical position, defended by a remarkable consensus of major scholars (Bernhard Duhm in 1892, Hans Wildberger, Brevard Childs, Joseph Blenkinsopp, Hugh Williamson, Claus Westermann, and many others), holds that chapters 34-35 are *late additions* drawn extensively from Second Isaiah's vocabulary and themes. The argument has multiple layers, and it is one of the strongest source-critical cases in Isaiah studies.

First, the *vocabulary parallels* with Second Isaiah (chapters 40-55) are exceptionally dense. The wilderness-blossoming motif (Isa 41:18-19, 43:19-20, 51:3), the highway-in-the-desert (Isa 40:3-4, 49:11), the ransomed and redeemed of the LORD (Isa 43:1, 44:22-23, 48:20, 51:11, 52:3, 62:12), the *peduyim* and *geulim* terminology, the wilderness-becoming-fruitful imagery, the call to strengthen weak hands, and the explicit return-to-Zion-with-singing motif — every one of these clusters in Second Isaiah and recurs in Isaiah 35. The verbal density of these connections is too high to be coincidence.

Second, Isaiah 51:11 contains an *almost verbatim* parallel to Isaiah 35:10. The two verses are nearly identical: "And the ransomed of the LORD shall return and come to Zion with singing; everlasting joy shall be upon their heads; they shall obtain gladness and joy, and sorrow and sighing shall flee away." Critical scholars debate which is the source and which is the borrowing, but the parallel is striking enough that one almost certainly depends on the other.

Third, the *thematic coherence* with Second Isaiah's exilic-restoration context — the scattered people returning to Zion, the divine-warrior-as-redeemer motif, the radical reversal of desert-into-paradise — fits the post-587 BCE context of exiles longing for return, not the eighth-century geopolitical context of Isaiah ben Amoz.

A second position, defended by some more conservative scholars, holds that chapter 35 contains genuine Isaianic core material that has been redactionally framed and updated. A third increasingly influential position (Hugh Williamson's *Book Called Isaiah* and others) reads chapters 34-35 as deliberate editorial bridge — composed by the editor who unified First and Second Isaiah into a single book, designed to function as a literary-theological hinge between the two halves.

The Great Isaiah Scroll (1QIsaᵃ) preserves chapter 35 with the standard pattern: orthographic variants, fuller spellings, no substantive textual deviation. The diptych structure is firmly in place by the second century BCE.

The Hebrew vocabulary of Isaiah 35 is among the most lyrical in the Hebrew Bible. The opening line — *yesusum midbar ve-tziyyah ve-tagel arava ve-tifrach ka-chavatzelet* (יְשֻׂשׂוּם מִדְבָּר וְצִיָּה וְתָגֵל עֲרָבָה וְתִפְרַח כַּחֲבַצָּלֶת, "the wilderness and the dry land shall be glad; the desert shall rejoice and blossom like the crocus") — uses three different terms for arid land (*midbar*, *tziyyah*, *aravah*) and pairs them with two verbs of joy (*sus*, *gil*) and one of flowering (*parach*). The piling of synonyms is intentional poetic abundance — the verse's form mimics its content, the wilderness producing more language than ordinary speech requires.

The word *chavatzelet* (חֲבַצֶּלֶת) is uncertainly translated. ESV renders "crocus"; KJV "rose"; some scholars suggest meadow saffron, lily, or asphodel. The exact botanical referent is unrecoverable. What is recoverable is the rhetorical function: the desert blooms with a flower so distinctive it has its own name.

Verses 5-6 contain what is sometimes called the "messianic checklist": *az tipaqachnah einei ivrim, ve-oznei chershim tipatachnah; az yedaleg ka-ayyal piseach, ve-taron leshon illem* (אָז תִּפָּקַחְנָה עֵינֵי עִוְרִים וְאָזְנֵי חֵרְשִׁים תִּפָּתַחְנָה אָז יְדַלֵּג כָּאַיָּל פִּסֵּחַ וְתָרֹן לְשׁוֹן אִלֵּם, "then the eyes of the blind shall be opened, and the ears of the deaf unstopped; then shall the lame man leap like a deer, and the tongue of the mute sing for joy"). The four conditions — blindness, deafness, lameness, muteness — and their reversals form a structured chiasm that lists comprehensively the major sensory-motor disabilities of ancient experience. The verb forms are passive (Niphal) for the senses (eyes opened, ears unstopped) and active for the motor functions (the lame leap, the tongue sings) — a subtle distinction the English translations smooth over but the Hebrew preserves.

The "highway of holiness" (*derekh ha-qodesh*, דֶּרֶךְ הַקֹּדֶשׁ) of v. 8 is a dedicated processional road on which the unclean cannot travel and on which even fools cannot go astray. The image is technical: a *derekh* in ancient prophetic literature is not merely a path but a graded and engineered roadway, and *qodesh* (holiness) here functions adjectivally — the road *itself* is holy, set apart, consecrated.

The closing line — *u-fduyei Yahweh yeshuvun u-vau Tziyyon be-rinnah, ve-simchat olam al rosham, sason ve-simchah yassigu, ve-nasu yagon va-anachah* (וּפְדוּיֵי יְהוָה יְשֻׁבוּן וּבָאוּ צִיּוֹן בְּרִנָּה וְשִׂמְחַת עוֹלָם עַל־רֹאשָׁם שָׂשׂוֹן וְשִׂמְחָה יַשִּׂיגוּ וְנָסוּ יָגוֹן וַאֲנָחָה) — is among the most beautiful Hebrew sentences in the prophetic corpus. *Peduyim* (the ransomed) is technical redemption language, used of those bought back from slavery or destruction; *yashuv* (return) is the verbal root for both physical return (return from exile) and moral return (repentance — *teshuvah* in later Jewish theology). The chapter's final word, *anachah* (sighing), is what flees away — not what comes.

## Lens 2: How the Text Works as Literature

Chapter 35 is structured in three movements that build toward a final climactic image. Verses 1-2 open with the wilderness rejoicing and blossoming — three terms for arid land, two verbs of joy, one of flowering, all converging on the vision of seeing Yahweh's glory. Verses 3-7 issue a pastoral-prophetic exhortation ("strengthen the weak hands, make firm the feeble knees") and then deliver the blind-deaf-lame-mute reversal alongside the wilderness-becoming-springs imagery. Verses 8-10 introduce the highway of holiness and conclude with the ransomed of the LORD returning to Zion with everlasting joy.

The literary architecture is one of *radical reversal*. Every category in the chapter is staged as inversion of an earlier desolation. The wilderness rejoices (against the wilderness desolation of 32:14, 34:11-15). The blind see, deaf hear, lame leap, mute sing (against the prophetic-deafness motif of Isa 6:9-10). The waters break out in the wilderness (against the agricultural collapse of 32:9-14). The unclean cannot pass on the highway (against the chapter 34 vision of Edom becoming the haunt of unclean creatures). The ransomed return to Zion (against the exile that scattered them).

This reversal-architecture is not merely thematic; it is structurally tied to chapter 34. The diptych works as a single literary unit. Chapter 34's heavens-rolling-up is followed by chapter 35's wilderness-blossoming. Chapter 34's *charam*-judgment is followed by chapter 35's *peduyim*-return. Chapter 34's wild creatures inheriting Edom is followed by chapter 35's redeemed walking the highway of holiness. The two chapters are mirror images, and each requires the other to function.

The "messianic checklist" of vv. 5-6 deserves particular attention. Its four-fold structure (blind, deaf, lame, mute) is poetically tight, and its placement within the larger chapter — between the call to courage and the highway-of-holiness vision — gives it functional weight. The healing of the four conditions is not the chapter's point but its enabling condition: the people who must walk the highway of holiness, who must process to Zion in singing, must first be made physically capable of the journey. The healings are *for* the procession.

The "highway of holiness" image carries enormous theological weight in the Isaianic and broader prophetic tradition. The motif of a graded road through the wilderness, on which the redeemed travel back to Zion, becomes one of the most enduring images in Jewish and Christian eschatological imagination. The highway is *engineered* — Yahweh has graded it, and it is set apart. It is *exclusive* — the unclean cannot pass. It is *fool-proof* — even those without wisdom cannot go astray on it. And it is *safe* — no lion or ravenous beast can come up on it. Each of these features is a deliberate literary choice with theological cargo.

The closing image of v. 10 — *peduyei Yahweh* returning to Zion with singing, *simchat olam* (everlasting joy) on their heads, *yagon va-anachah* (sorrow and sighing) fleeing away — is the chapter's final word and the diptych's final word. After the heavens rolling up like a scroll, after the *charam* of Edom, after the wild creatures inheriting the ruins, after all the violence of chapter 34 — the diptych ends with sighing fleeing away. The pairing matters: the same canonical text that contains the most violent judgment-imagery contains the most tender restoration-imagery, and the editor who placed them together meant for both to be read as theologically necessary.

## Lens 3: The Larger Story

In the prophetic canon, Isaiah 35 sits at a structurally critical location. The chapter forms (with chapter 34) the *redemption-frame* before chapters 36-39's historical narrative about Hezekiah and the Sennacherib crisis, which itself prepares for the great pivot to Second Isaiah at chapter 40 — the prophetic voice of consolation announcing the end of Babylonian exile. Chapters 34-35 anticipate the consolation; chapters 36-39 supply the historical narrative; chapter 40 announces the consolation arrived.

The Jewish trajectory has read Isaiah 35 along several major lines. In rabbinic and medieval Jewish thought, the chapter has been read as both a vision of return from Babylonian exile (its plain post-exilic referent on critical readings) and as a vision of the messianic age (its eschatological referent on traditional Jewish readings). The Targum Jonathan paraphrases verse 1 with messianic-age expectation. Rabbinic literature pairs the wilderness-blossoming with messianic restoration (e.g., Pesiqta de-Rab Kahana). The medieval commentators (Rashi, Ibn Ezra, Radak) read the chapter as encompassing both the post-exilic return and the future messianic ingathering. The "highway of holiness" became a recurring image in Jewish liturgical and poetic tradition for the path of *teshuvah* (return/repentance) and the eschatological ingathering of exiles. The ransomed-return of v. 10 is recited in the *Yom Kippur* liturgy and is woven into Jewish hopes for messianic redemption across two millennia.

The Christian trajectory has read Isaiah 35 even more intensely Christologically, primarily because of its New Testament use. The most consequential New Testament citation occurs at Matthew 11:4-5 and its parallel Luke 7:22 — the famous "are you the one who is to come?" exchange between John the Baptist's disciples and Jesus. John, imprisoned and apparently uncertain, sends his disciples to ask Jesus directly whether he is the messiah. Jesus's reply is striking. He does not give a yes or no. Instead, he tells the disciples: "Go and tell John what you hear and see: the blind receive their sight and the lame walk, lepers are cleansed and the deaf hear, and the dead are raised up, and the poor have good news preached to them" (Matt 11:4-5).

This reply is a deliberate conflation of Isaiah 35:5-6 and Isaiah 61:1. The blind, deaf, lame elements come from Isaiah 35:5-6; the "good news preached to the poor" comes from Isaiah 61:1. Mainstream New Testament scholarship — including the work of N.T. Wright, Joel Marcus, Richard Hays, and others — has identified this exchange as one of Jesus's clearest *self-identifications as messiah*. By pointing to the messianic-checklist of Isaiah 35, Jesus is implicitly saying: the conditions Isaiah named as the markers of the messianic age are arriving in my ministry; draw your own conclusion. Richard Hays in *Echoes of Scripture in the Gospels* (and elsewhere) treats this as a paradigmatic case of Jesus's "metaleptic" use of Scripture — citing fragments that activate the larger Isaianic context to make implicit Christological claims.

The chapter is also embedded throughout the broader New Testament Christological imagination. The wilderness-as-place-of-preparation motif of v. 8 (the highway of holiness) is taken up in all four gospels' presentation of John the Baptist as the wilderness voice (Matt 3:3, Mark 1:3, Luke 3:4, John 1:23) — though the explicit prophetic citation in those passages is to Isaiah 40:3, not 35:8. Still, the two passages share the highway-in-the-wilderness motif and form a single textual stream in early Christian use. Hebrews 12:12 cites Isaiah 35:3 ("strengthen the drooping hands and the weak knees") to exhort Christian endurance.

The chapter's healing imagery — eyes opened, ears unstopped, lame leaping, mute singing — recurs throughout the gospel healing-narratives in ways the gospel writers seem deliberately to choreograph against Isaianic expectation. When Jesus heals a deaf-mute man at Mark 7:31-37 (the *Ephphatha* miracle), the crowd's exclamation that "he has done all things well; he even makes the deaf hear and the mute speak" reads as direct echo of Isaiah 35:5-6. The pattern is consistent across the synoptic gospels and into John.

## Lens 4: What the Text Means

The chapter raises several major theological tensions that have shaped Jewish and Christian reading communities for two millennia.

The first is the **Matthew 11 / Luke 7 Christological appropriation**. Mainstream New Testament scholarship — N.T. Wright, Joel Marcus, Richard Hays, Joel Green, R.T. France — generally treats Jesus's reply to John's disciples as one of his clearest self-identifications as messiah, mediated through Isaiah 35:5-6 and 61:1. The argument has two layers. First, the items Jesus cites (blind, lame, lepers, deaf, dead raised, poor evangelized) match too precisely the messianic-age expectations of Isaiah 35 (and Isaiah 26 and 61) for the citation to be coincidental. Second, the rhetorical strategy — citing the Isaianic conditions and trusting John to draw the conclusion — is a recognizable Jewish exegetical move (called metalepsis by Hays) where a fragment activates the larger context. The Christological conclusion, on this reading, is invited rather than asserted — but the invitation is unmistakable.

The strongest steelman of the Christological reading is that it is textually disciplined: it does not require importing later doctrinal claims into the gospel; it simply takes Jesus's citation seriously and follows the Isaianic links the text itself provides. The strongest steelman of a *non*-Christological reading (more common in some Jewish scholarly engagements) is that Jesus's reply could be read as claiming participation in the messianic-age conditions without claiming to *be* the messianic figure — that is, that the prophetic reversals are arriving, but that the question of who Jesus is remains theologically open. Both readings have textual warrant, though the Christological reading has the weight of the gospel narratives' overall framing behind it.

The second is the **late-composition** position on chapters 34-35. The mainstream-critical view (Childs, Wildberger, Blenkinsopp, Westermann, Williamson) is that chapter 35 is drawn from Second Isaiah's vocabulary and was composed (or finally redacted) in the post-exilic period. This raises a substantive question: does this affect the chapter's theological authority or canonical function? Two positions emerge.

The *historical-critical* position takes the late composition seriously and reads the chapter as the deliberate work of an exilic or post-exilic editor who used Second Isaiah's themes to frame First Isaiah's woe-oracles with a vision of return. The strongest steelman is that this reading honors what the textual evidence actually shows — the dense vocabulary parallels, the verbatim correspondence with Isaiah 51:11, the bridging function — and that it produces a more historically grounded reading of the book. The chapter's authority is not diminished but located in the redactor's theological achievement.

The *canonical-final-form* position (Brevard Childs's signature methodology) accepts the late composition but argues that the theological meaning of the chapter is determined by its final canonical placement, not its compositional pre-history. The strongest steelman is that the chapter, regardless of when it was composed, *now* functions canonically as the redemption-frame between First and Second Isaiah, and that is its theological location for any reading community.

The *traditional-conservative* position (defended by some evangelical scholars) holds that the chapter is genuinely Isaianic, perhaps with redactional updates, and that the vocabulary parallels with Second Isaiah may point to a unified Isaianic authorship across the book. This position is increasingly minoritized in academic biblical studies but remains in some ecclesial readings. Its strongest steelman is that vocabulary alone is not decisive evidence of multiple authorship and that the prophetic books may be more unified than late nineteenth-century critical scholarship assumed.

The third is the **closing-of-First-Isaiah** structural function. Chapters 34-35 form a redemption-frame before the historical narrative of chapters 36-39, and that frame *anticipates* the great consolation of Second Isaiah at chapter 40. This is theologically loaded. The frame implies that the woe-oracles of chapters 28-33 are not the last word — that judgment, however cosmic and however violent, opens onto restoration. The chapter's vision of *peduyei Yahweh* returning to Zion is not just a closing image; it is a theological claim about how the book of Isaiah understands the moral structure of history. Judgment comes; restoration follows; sighing flees away.

There is also the question of **honest engagement with the violence** of the diptych. Chapter 34's *herem*-language and chapter 35's tender restoration are paired in the canon. Some readers want chapter 35 without chapter 34 (the restoration without the judgment); some readings emphasize chapter 34 to the exclusion of chapter 35 (the judgment without the restoration). Both moves are interpretively dishonest. The diptych is a unit. The wilderness blossoms because the heavens rolled up. The ransomed return because the destroyer was destroyed. Whether one finds this pairing morally satisfying is a separate question; but the pairing is the canonical fact.

The chapter's vision of the highway of holiness raises a final tension. Verse 8 says "the unclean shall not pass over it." This exclusionary logic has been used historically — sometimes piously, sometimes catastrophically — to police religious community boundaries. The strongest steelman of the exclusionary reading is that the chapter is describing a *processional path* for those returning from exile, and that the unclean-cannot-pass language is technical pilgrimage-purity language, not a generic exclusion of moral inferiors. The strongest steelman of a more inclusive reading is that the chapter also says even fools cannot go astray on the highway — implying that the way's distinctive feature is its *graciousness*, its capacity to carry even the morally and intellectually weak to Zion. Both readings are possible. The chapter's tone tilts, perhaps, slightly toward the second.

**Where this leaves us:** Isaiah 35 is the chapter to which Jesus pointed when his cousin and forerunner asked, in prison, *are you the one?* The chapter closes the first half of the book of Isaiah with sighing fleeing away. It anticipates the consolation of Second Isaiah, the restoration of Zion, and — for Christian readers — the messianic ministry the gospels narrate as fulfillment. It carries inside it the tension between desert and bloom, between violence and tenderness, between the terror of chapter 34 and the joy of v. 10. It refuses to let any reader settle for half the canon. The wilderness blossoms because the diptych demands both halves; the ransomed return because exile and judgment have been real; the everlasting joy on their heads is real because the sighing was real first.

The chapter's final image — the redeemed walking the highway, joy on their heads, sorrow and sighing fleeing away — is one of the most striking pictures in the Hebrew Bible. It does not promise that sorrow will not have happened. It promises that sorrow will not have the last word. The Hebrew word for joy in this verse — *simchat olam* — is *forever-joy*, joy that does not depend on the absence of memory. The ransomed remember where they have been. They are still singing.

**Closing question:** When you imagine your own return — from whatever wilderness you are currently in — do you imagine sorrow being erased, or sorrow being outpaced? The chapter pictures the second. What would it mean to walk a highway where joy is forever and sighing flees, but the wilderness behind you is real and remembered?

## The Thread

The wilderness blossoms. The blind see, the lame leap, the mute sing. A highway is built through the desert. The ransomed return to Zion. Sorrow and sighing flee away. This is the answer to the question John asked from prison.
