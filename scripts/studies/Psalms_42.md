# Psalm 42 — Go Deeper

## Lens 1: The Historical World

Psalm 42 opens Book II of the Psalter (Pss 42–72) and simultaneously inaugurates the Korahite collection (Pss 42–49, with a second cluster at 84–85, 87–88). The superscription reads *lamnatzeach maskil livnei-Qorach* (לַמְנַצֵּחַ מַשְׂכִּיל לִבְנֵי־קֹרַח), "To the choirmaster. A *Maskil* of the Sons of Korah." The Korahites were a Levitical guild of temple singers and gatekeepers. Numbers 26:11 famously notes that "the sons of Korah did not die" when their ancestor was swallowed by the earth in Numbers 16, and 1 Chronicles 6:31–38 and 9:17–19 place their descendants among the cult musicians appointed by David. The label *maskil* (משכיל) — perhaps "instructive poem" or "skillful composition" (the root *s-k-l*, "to be wise, to instruct") — designates roughly thirteen psalms; Hossfeld and Zenger treat it as a guild-marker for songs intended for liturgical pedagogy.

Form-critically, Psalm 42 is a paradigmatic **individual lament** in Gunkel's *Klagelied des Einzelnen* category — though one of the unusual ones, since it lacks an explicit petition and its center is interior dialogue rather than accusation against enemies. Mowinckel's cult-functional reading places its *Sitz im Leben* in a pilgrim's longing for the Jerusalem sanctuary, possibly a temple-functionary in temporary exile from his post; the geography of 42:6 ("from the land of Jordan and of Hermon, from Mount Mizar") points to Israel's far north, and the verb *e'evor* in 42:4 ("how I would *go* with the throng") evokes pilgrimage processions to Zion.

A crucial editorial observation: Pss 42–83 form what Hermann Gunkel and after him Frank-Lothar Hossfeld and Erich Zenger call the **Elohistic Psalter**, in which the divine name *YHWH* (יהוה) has been systematically replaced by *Elohim* (אלהים). Psalm 42 itself uses *Elohim* roughly twenty times against only one occurrence of YHWH (42:8). Kraus and Goldingay both read this as deliberate redaction — possibly a northern recension brought south, or a postexilic editorial choice to render the divine name more transferable across diaspora settings. The Korahite voice in Ps 42, with its northern geography and longing-from-distance posture, fits this hypothesis.

Hebrew terms worth marking:

- *ke-ayyal ta'arog al-afikei mayim* (כְּאַיָּל תַּעֲרֹג עַל־אֲפִיקֵי מָיִם), "as a deer pants for streams of water" (42:1). The verb *arag* is rare (only here and Joel 1:20) and onomatopoeic — a guttural longing-cry. *Ayyal* is masculine; some manuscripts and the LXX read the feminine *ayyalah*, which produced the famous medieval painting iconography of the doe at the spring.
- *tzame'ah nafshi le-Elohim le-El chai* (צָמְאָה נַפְשִׁי לֵאלֹהִים לְאֵל חָי), "my soul thirsts for God, for the living God" (42:2). The juxtaposition of *Elohim* and *El chai* is the only YHWH-substitute that retains a personal-living quality.
- *mah-tishtochachi nafshi* (מַה־תִּשְׁתּוֹחֲחִי נַפְשִׁי), "why are you cast down, O my soul" (42:5, 11; 43:5). The Hithpael of *shachach*, "to bow down/be bowed down," with the soul as a self-addressed interlocutor — the dialectical structure that Brueggemann calls disorientation moving toward reorientation.
- *tehom el-tehom qore* (תְּהוֹם אֶל־תְּהוֹם קוֹרֵא), "deep calls to deep" (42:7). *Tehom* is the primordial cosmic deep of Genesis 1:2; the line evokes chaos-water imagery.

Textual evidence: 4QPs(a) and 11QPs fragments preserve portions of Ps 42 consistent with the Masoretic text. The Septuagint numbers it Ps 41 and — significantly — joins it with Ps 43 in some witnesses; the Vulgate continues this practice. This is the most important textual fact about the psalm: in many Hebrew manuscripts (Kennicott catalogs at least thirty-seven) and in much of the Greek tradition, **Pss 42 and 43 are a single composition**. The shared refrain at 42:5, 42:11, and 43:5; the absence of a superscription on Ps 43; and the parallel three-strophe structure all support what Goldingay, Hossfeld–Zenger, and Kraus regard as the mainstream-critical reading: one psalm split, probably for liturgical reasons, and never fully rejoined.

## Lens 2: Literature

The poem's architecture is built on the threefold refrain. Read as a unity with Ps 43, the structure is three strophes (42:1–5; 42:6–11; 43:1–5), each closing with the same self-address: *Why are you cast down, O my soul, and why are you in turmoil within me? Hope in God; for I shall again praise him, my salvation and my God.* This is **inclusio across strophes** — a refrain that does both rhetorical work (binding the units) and theological work (re-asserting hope after each fresh wave of grief).

Lowth's parallelism categories, as refined by Berlin, Kugel, and Alter, run densely through the psalm. The opening couplet is paradigmatic synonymous parallelism with intensification: "as a deer pants for flowing streams // so pants my soul for you, O God." Alter would note the heightening from animal to human, from generic water to "you, O God." The dominant device, however, is what Berlin calls **grammatical parallelism**: the verbs of motion (pants, thirsts, comes, appears, remembers, pours out, leads in procession) accumulate against verbs of stasis and downness (cast down, in turmoil, mourning, oppressed, taunted). The poem moves spatially — the water imagery oscillates between the dry-tongued thirst of vv. 1–2 and the over-flooding waters of v. 7 ("all your breakers and your waves have gone over me"). The same element nourishes and drowns. Wenham reads this as the psalm's central poetic move: the absent-good and the present-evil are imagined under the same trope.

Strophic structure (treating 42–43 as one psalm):

- **Strophe I (42:1–5)**: Thirst-longing and memory of pilgrimage; refrain.
- **Strophe II (42:6–11)**: Geographic-cosmic dislocation, the deep-calls-to-deep flood; the taunt "Where is your God?"; refrain.
- **Strophe III (43:1–5)**: Petition for vindication, light-and-truth as guides, the altar of God; refrain.

There are no *selah* markings in Ps 42 in the MT, which is unusual for a Korahite psalm and may reflect its splitting from Ps 43. Westermann classifies the poem within his lament-form template — invocation, complaint, confession of trust, petition, vow of praise — but notes that here the "vow of praise" is folded into the refrain itself, embedded as anticipated future ("I shall *again* praise him"). The grammar of *od odennu* (עוֹד אוֹדֶנּוּ) — "still / yet I will praise him" — is hope-grammar: a future tense pried open inside present grief.

The taunt "Where is your God?" (*ayyeh Eloheikha*, אַיֵּה אֱלֹהֶיךָ) appears twice (42:3, 42:10), itself a refrain-within-refrain. The enemy's voice and the self-addressed soul's voice form a dialogical structure: the psalmist is being interrogated from outside and inside simultaneously, and the only counter-voice is hope's *od* — yet, again.

## Lens 3: The Larger Story

**Placement.** Psalm 42 opens Book II and the Korahite collection. Book I (Pss 1–41) is overwhelmingly Davidic and YHWH-named; the move into Book II is a move into Korahite, Asaphite, and Elohistic territory. Hossfeld–Zenger argue that the editors of the final Psalter deliberately placed the Korahite lament cycle (Pss 42–49) at the threshold of Book II to introduce a more communal, more diasporic, more interrogative voice. After the Davidic individual of Book I, we hear the Levitical singer's longing-from-afar.

**Jewish trajectory.** *Midrash Tehillim* (Shocher Tov) on Ps 42 dwells on the deer image: the deer is said to be the most pious of animals, lifting its head heavenward when drinking; some midrashim associate the panting deer with Israel longing for redemption. Rashi reads "from the land of Jordan and Hermon" as the lament of the exiled community. Ibn Ezra and Radak both wrestle with the unity question and tend to treat 42–43 as related but distinct. Nachmanides reads the *tehom el-tehom* line as the saint's experience of one wave of trial after another. In modern Jewish reception, Levenson treats Ps 42 within his Sinai/Zion typology — the psalmist's longing is not for an idea but for a place where YHWH is enthroned. Sarna and Zornberg both emphasize the dialectical self-address as a Hebrew distinctive: the soul is not silenced by its own grief but addressed, argued with. Heschel cites the "deep calls to deep" line as paradigmatic of the prophetic experience of pathos. Liturgically, Ps 42 is recited on the Days of Awe in some Sephardic rites and figures in Ashkenazi pre-Tashlich devotions.

**Christian trajectory.** Augustine's *Enarrationes in Psalmos* on Ps 41 (his numbering of our 42) is one of the most influential pieces of psalm exegesis in the Christian tradition. Augustine reads the deer as the catechumen approaching baptism — and the line "as the deer pants for streams of water" was inscribed on baptismal fonts across late-antique North Africa and Italy. The very first words of his *Confessions* — *fecisti nos ad te, et inquietum est cor nostrum donec requiescat in te* ("you have made us for yourself, and our heart is restless until it rests in you") — are a direct theological echo of *tzame'ah nafshi le-Elohim*. Athanasius cites Ps 42 in his *Letter to Marcellinus* as a paradigm for how the soul should learn to address itself. Cassiodorus treats the threefold refrain as a Trinitarian rhythm. Aquinas in his *Postilla super Psalmos* reads the deer-spring image sacramentally. Luther preached on Ps 42 in the *Operationes* and again during his Wartburg seclusion — his own dislocation from Wittenberg colored the sermon. Calvin (1557) emphasizes the psalm's pedagogy of the believer learning to speak to oneself with the words of faith against the roar of one's own dejection. In Eastern Orthodox tradition, the deer-pants verse is the central troparion of the **paschal-baptismal liturgy** — the catechumens process toward the font as the choir sings *On*\ekemu *I psyche mou*. The image of the panting deer was carved into baptisteries from Ravenna to Aquileia.

Modern critical commentary: Gunkel established the form-critical reading; Mowinckel placed the psalm in pilgrim-cult context; Westermann mapped the lament-form; Kraus, Goldingay, Tate, Wenham, Anderson, Brueggemann, Miller, Brown, Tanner, and Gillingham have each contributed. Brueggemann's *Message of the Psalms* makes Ps 42 a flagship of his "psalms of disorientation" — the poetry of the in-between, after the world has cracked but before it has been remade.

**NT use.** Ps 42 is not directly cited in the New Testament, but its imagery suffuses Johannine water-discourse — Jesus's promise of "rivers of living water" (John 7:37–38), the woman at the well (John 4), the river of life flowing from the throne (Rev 22:1) all run through Ps 42's tributary.

## Lens 4: Theological Intent

The psalm presses two tensions worth taking seriously.

**Tension one: Is longing for God a defect to be cured or a feature to be cultivated?** The deer's panting is not satisfaction; it is the marker of a desire-not-yet-met. If Ps 42 is the soul's school, what is it teaching? Five mapped positions:

- *Augustinian / Catholic*: longing is the soul's natural orientation toward God as final end; it is to be cultivated, not extinguished, because it is the very form of pilgrim love (*caritas viatoris*) on this side of the beatific vision. The restlessness of *Confessions* I.1 is the divinely-given motor of the spiritual life.
- *Protestant Reformed (Calvin, Edwards)*: longing is the Spirit's witness in the believer that the present is not yet home; it is the affective shape of faith stretched between the already and the not-yet. Edwards's *Religious Affections* makes Ps 42 a touchstone for distinguishing genuine spiritual hunger from sentimental piety.
- *Eastern Orthodox*: longing is *eros* directed toward *theosis*; the deer panting is the soul's instinctive movement toward union with the divine energies. Gregory of Nyssa's *epektasis* — eternal stretching forward — finds its psalm here.
- *Jewish (Levenson, Heschel)*: longing is the appropriate posture of a covenant-people in galut, in exile from the place where God's presence is liturgically encountered. The thirst is not for God-as-idea but for God-at-the-altar (cf. 43:4); it is land-and-temple-shaped, not mystical.
- *Critical / phenomenological (Brueggemann, Ricoeur)*: the psalm performs the work of grief — the dialectical self-address keeps the sufferer from being silenced or absorbed by the trauma. Longing here is a mode of *speaking* in the ruins, a practice of refusing both denial and despair.

The least intuitive position is the Jewish one — that the longing is *place*-specific, not metaphysical-universal. Steelman: the deer is not a Platonist seeking the Form of the Good. The deer is thirsty for *that stream*. The psalmist is not pining for a generic divine presence; he is pining for the morning sacrifice at a specific altar in a specific city, in a community with specific singing voices ("how I would go with the throng…"). To universalize the longing into a soul-to-Beauty trajectory is to lose the political-liturgical specificity of the complaint. Levenson presses the point: cosmic-soul readings tend to dissolve the scandal of God's having tied himself to a place and a people.

**Tension two: What is the soul doing when it talks back to itself?** *Mah-tishtochachi nafshi* — "why are you cast down, O my soul" — is one of the strangest sentences in the Hebrew Bible. The speaker is at once subject and addressee. Brueggemann, drawing on Westermann, reads this as the central act of biblical lament: the sufferer refuses to be reduced to his suffering, splits the self into a complaining-self and a hoping-self, and lets the hoping-self preach to the complaining-self. The Christian devotional tradition (Lloyd-Jones's *Spiritual Depression* is the canonical modern statement) reads this as the soul talking *to* itself rather than *listening to* itself — preaching faith, in the second person, against the roar of unbelief in the first. A Jewish counter-reading (Sarna) emphasizes that this is dialogue, not preaching; the soul does not silence its sorrow, it converses with it. The contemplative tradition (Cassian, Climacus) reads it as the discernment of spirits in the cell.

**Where this leaves us:** Ps 42 is not a poem that resolves. The third refrain still ends with "I shall *again* praise him" — future tense, not present. The psalm canonizes longing-without-arrival as a legitimate and durable Christian and Jewish posture. To say "my soul thirsts" is not a confession of failure; it is the form the love of God takes for those who are not yet home.

**Closing question.** If the panting deer never reaches the stream within the poem itself — if the psalm ends in the future tense and not the indicative — what does the church gain by singing this song every week, and what does it lose if it sings only the songs of arrival?

## The Thread

- Numbers 26:11 — the sons of Korah did not die; the genealogical thread that makes the superscription possible.
- John 7:37–38 — "if anyone thirsts, let him come to me and drink"; the Johannine answer to the deer.
- Romans 8:24–25 — "in this hope we were saved … but if we hope for what we do not see, we wait for it with patience"; the Pauline grammar of *od odennu*.
- Revelation 22:1–2 — the river of the water of life flowing from the throne; the deer arrives at the stream at last.
