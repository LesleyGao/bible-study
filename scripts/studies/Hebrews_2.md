# Hebrews 2: So Great a Salvation — The Pioneer Made Perfect Through Suffering

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

Hebrews 2 lands its first warning in a community that knows what it costs to belong to it. The audience has already faced public reproach, property loss, and imprisonment of fellow believers (10:32-34); they have not yet "resisted to the point of shedding blood" (12:4); and they live in a Roman political world where the costs of dissent from civic religious norms were rising. Whether the homily was composed in the late 60s, the 70s, or the 80s, the Mediterranean world of its audience was one where the Jewish-Roman war (66-70 AD) had either just unfolded or had cast a long shadow, where the imperial cult was tightening under the Flavians, and where Jewish-Christian and Gentile-Christian communities in Rome (the leading destination candidate, supported by William Lane and Barnabas Lindars) lived in an in-between social location — too Christian for the synagogue, too Jewish for the imperial cult, too philosophically peculiar for the Greco-Roman associations.

Material culture matters here. The "message declared by angels" of 2:2 presupposes a Second Temple Jewish tradition, attested in Acts 7:53, Galatians 3:19, *Jubilees* 1:27-29, and Josephus *Ant.* 15.136, that the Sinai Torah was mediated through angelic intermediaries — a tradition that elevated the dignity of the Mosaic legislation by routing it through heavenly couriers. Hebrews knows this tradition and uses it: if a word that came through angels carried just retribution for its violation, how much more a word spoken directly by the Son? The community of the audience is one that takes for granted the Jewish angelology of its time, but they are being asked to relativize it.

Authorship remains anonymous. Origen's verdict — "Who wrote the epistle, in truth God knows" (Eusebius, *HE* 6.25.14) — still governs. The Eastern church inherited a Pauline ascription; the West hesitated for centuries until Hippo (393) and Carthage (397). Modern proposals include Apollos (William Manson), Barnabas (Tertullian, *De Pudicitia* 20), Priscilla (Adolf von Harnack, "Probabilia," 1900), Luke, Silas, and Clement. The author is a Hellenistic-Jewish Christian of high rhetorical training, formed in the LXX and probably in Alexandrian-Philonic categories, writing to second-generation believers who heard the gospel "from those who heard" the Lord (2:3) — a key dating data point that places composition at least one generation removed from Jesus' ministry.

Dating is constrained on the late side by *1 Clement* (c. 96 AD), which appears to know Hebrews; *1 Clem* 36 quotes Heb 1:3-13 closely. On the early side, the present-tense priestly verbs throughout the homily push some toward pre-70 dating; Ellingworth (NIGTC) and Attridge (Hermeneia) defend a post-70 date with present tenses read as generic-pattern descriptions of the tabernacle.

Textually, P46 (c. 200) places Hebrews directly after Romans, suggesting how seriously its tradition treated the Pauline associations. Sinaiticus (א), Vaticanus (B), Alexandrinus (A), and Ephraemi (C) where extant preserve chapter 2 well. There are no major variants of theological consequence in this chapter, though 2:9 has a textual variant — "by the grace of God" *chariti theou* (most witnesses) versus "apart from God" *chōris theou* (a minority reading attested by Origen and a few witnesses) — which has occasioned theological discussion since the patristic period.

Greek key terms: παραρρέω *pararreō* "drift away" (2:1, hapax in NT — a nautical metaphor for a boat slipping past its mooring); ἀρχηγός *archēgos* "founder/pioneer/champion" (2:10); τελειόω *teleioō* "to bring to completion / to perfect" (2:10); καταργέω *katargeō* "render ineffective / nullify / destroy" (2:14); ἀρχιερεύς *archiereus* "high priest" (2:17 — first appearance of the central image of the homily); ἱλάσκομαι *hilaskomai* "make propitiation" (2:17, with its noun *hilastērion* echoing Lev 16's mercy seat).

## Lens 2 — The Literature

Hebrews 2 contains the first of the homily's five major warning passages (the others: 3:7-4:13; 5:11-6:12; 10:19-39; 12:14-29). The form Lawrence Wills (*The Form of the Sermon in Hellenistic Judaism and Early Christianity*, 1984) and Hartwig Thyen identified — alternation of expository and hortatory sections — is on full display. The catena of chapter 1 (exposition) yields to the warning of 2:1-4 (hortatory), which in turn yields to a fresh expository unit grounded in Psalm 8 (2:5-9), which in turn yields to a hortatory-doctrinal climax (2:10-18). The pattern will repeat throughout the homily.

The rhetorical hinge of 2:1-4 is the *a fortiori* (qal vahomer) argument: if the angelically-mediated message bore consequences, how much more the word spoken in the Son? This is the same argument-form rabbinic exegesis would later codify; it is the standard move of Hellenistic-Jewish reasoning. The author also uses *paraklēsis* in its forensic sense — exhortation that names the cost of negligence.

Then comes the Psalm 8 exposition (2:5-9). Psalm 8 in its original setting is a hymn about the dignity of humanity in creation: "what is *anthrōpos* that you are mindful of him?... You made him a little lower than the *elohim* / angels." Hebrews reads it Christologically — but with a careful argumentative move. The author exploits the LXX rendering: the Hebrew *me'at* ("a little") is rendered *brachy ti*, which can mean either "a little less" (spatially) or "for a little while" (temporally). Hebrews chooses the temporal reading: Jesus was made "for a little while" lower than the angels — that is, during his earthly life — and is now crowned with glory and honor. The text-form does the work; without the LXX, the argument would not run. Richard Hays's *Echoes of Scripture in the Letters of Paul* offers methodological guidance for hearing this kind of intertextual move; G.K. Beale's commentary on the NT use of the OT (*Commentary on the New Testament Use of the Old Testament*) maps it explicitly.

Albert Vanhoye's structural analysis (*La Structure littéraire de l'Épître aux Hébreux*, 1963/1976) treats 2:5-18 as the doctrinal exposition of "Jesus crowned with glory and honor" that completes the chapter-1 announcement. The sevenfold catena announces the Son's superiority; the Psalm-8 exposition explains the paradox of how that superior Son was for a moment lower than the angels he surpasses. The chapter is held together by an inclusio: 2:5 ("the world to come, of which we are speaking") looks back to 1:6 ("when he brings the firstborn into the world"); 2:17-18 (the merciful and faithful high priest) sets up chapters 5-7.

Locate the chapter in the book's argument: the warning pattern that begins here will be the homily's organizing rhythm. Each warning is positioned to land with maximum force after the high Christology of the preceding exposition. The pastoral logic is: precisely because the Son is who he is, neglecting the salvation he brings is unthinkable.

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

Within the canonical NT arc, Hebrews 2 places the suffering and the human solidarity of Jesus at the center of the salvation story. The Son who in chapter 1 was the radiance of God's glory is in chapter 2 the brother who shares blood and flesh, who was made like his brothers in every respect, who tastes death for everyone. The high Christology is not abandoned; it is filled out by the deep humanity. The Christology of Hebrews 1-2 read together is one of the strongest NT warrants for the dyophysite formulation that would crystallize at Chalcedon (451): truly God, truly human, without confusion or division.

**Christian Interpretive Trajectory.** Patristic theology found in Hebrews 2 a key text for the doctrine of the incarnation. **Athanasius's *On the Incarnation* (§20-25)** developed an account of Christ's death as defeat of the devil that draws on Heb 2:14-15 directly: by sharing in flesh and blood, the Son "renders inoperative" (*katargēsē*) the one who held the power of death. Chrysostom's *Homilies on Hebrews* treat 2:10-18 as a meditation on the brotherhood of Christ with believers. Theodoret of Cyrus emphasized 2:17 (made like his brothers in every respect) for its anti-docetic force. Cyril of Alexandria mined 2:14 for the unity of subject in Christ — it is the divine Son who suffers, in his humanity. The phrase "made perfect through suffering" (2:10) was a flashpoint in the Arian controversy: Arius read the verb developmentally (the Son is improved by suffering, hence not eternally perfect); Athanasius read it as "completion" of the Son's saving mission, not addition to his being.

Medieval theology developed two trajectories from chapter 2. **Anselm's *Cur Deus Homo* (1098)** drew on 2:14-17 for its satisfaction theory: the God-man is necessary because only one who is human can rightly satisfy the human debt and only one who is divine can offer infinite satisfaction. Aquinas's *Commentary on Hebrews* and his *Summa Theologiae* III.46-49 develop the satisfaction frame within a richer Christological synthesis. The other trajectory was the Christus Victor theme — Christ defeats death and the devil — which the Latin liturgy preserved and which the Eastern church kept central. The Greek Pascha hymn ("Christ is risen from the dead, trampling down death by death") is a direct liturgical descendant of Heb 2:14-15.

The Reformation inherited both. Luther fused Christus Victor and satisfaction in his cross-theology; Calvin (*Institutes* II.16) developed the penal-substitution frame more systematically while preserving the cosmic-victory theme. The Wesleyan tradition, with its emphasis on Christ's full humanity and his solidarity in temptation, leaned into 2:18 ("because he himself has suffered when tempted, he is able to help those who are being tempted") — a text central to Wesleyan pastoral theology.

Critical scholarship since the nineteenth century has produced major recoveries. **Gustaf Aulén's *Christus Victor* (1931)** argued that the Anselm-Calvin trajectory had marginalized the patristic Christus-Victor frame, and that texts like Heb 2:14-15 demanded its retrieval. Aulén's reading has been disputed in detail but has decisively reshaped Anglophone atonement theology. **René Girard's *I See Satan Fall Like Lightning*** offered a different reading of "the one who has the power of death": the satanic accuser is the scapegoat-mechanism itself, which the cross dismantles. Contemporary commentators — Ellingworth (NIGTC), Attridge (Hermeneia), Lane (WBC), Koester (Anchor Yale), Cockerill (NICNT), O'Brien (Pillar), deSilva — have produced a generation of careful exegesis. Kenneth Schenck's *Cosmology and Eschatology in Hebrews* attends to the cosmic frame within which Christ's victory is staged.

**Jewish Scholarly Critique and Its Evolution.** Heb 2's reading of Psalm 8 has been a flashpoint in Jewish-Christian textual conversation. Psalm 8 is, in its primary Jewish reading, a hymn about humanity's dignity within creation; the Heb 2 Christological reading exploits the LXX's *brachy ti* to land a frame the Hebrew text does not naturally support. Jewish polemic from the patristic era through the Middle Ages treated this as evidence of Christian misreading; modern scholarship is more nuanced. Daniel Boyarin's work on intra-Jewish interpretive traditions has shown that Christological readings of Psalm 8 are not aliens to Second Temple Judaism — the *Son of Man* tradition (Daniel 7, *1 Enoch*'s Similitudes) was already wrestling with how a human figure could share divine prerogatives. Pamela Eisenbaum and Mark Nanos have argued for reading Hebrews as an intra-Jewish document, written from within Judaism rather than against it. The 11QMelchizedek fragment (Qumran, 1st c. BC) shows how a Second Temple Jewish community could elevate a human-priestly figure into the heavenly *elohim* — a move structurally parallel to what Hebrews does with the Son.

Where contemporary Jewish scholarship still pushes back is on supersessionist readings. The phrase "he helps the offspring of Abraham" (2:16) has historically been read in Christian polemic as defining who the "true offspring" are — replacing Israel with the church. Recent Jewish-Christian dialogue (Krister Stendahl, Pamela Eisenbaum, the post-Vatican II *Nostra Aetate* tradition) has pushed back hard on this reading; Hebrews 2:16 most plausibly identifies the recipients of help as the Jewish-Christian audience itself, who are *physical* offspring of Abraham now incorporated into the Son's family.

**Where this leaves us.** Heb 2 is rich enough to sustain multiple atonement frames (Christus Victor, satisfaction, propitiation, moral influence) without privileging one as exhaustive. The contemporary trend is integrative — multiple models held together — and the Jewish-Christian dialogue has moved past polemic toward shared philological work.

## Lens 4 — The Theological Intent

**Tension 1: Christ "made perfect through suffering."** The phrase *teleiōsai dia pathēmatōn* (2:10) has caused theological vertigo since the second century. If the Son is eternally divine and already perfect, how can he be "made perfect" by suffering? Athanasius's reading is non-developmental: Christ retains divine perfection but, in the incarnation, assumes the human capacity for suffering and obedience — and is "completed" not in his being but in his saving mission. The Reformed tradition has followed Athanasius. Eastern Orthodoxy distinguishes between essence and energies and reads 2:10 in terms of the economy of salvation rather than ontological development.

The ancient adoptionist reading (Theodotus of Byzantium, late 2nd c.; Paul of Samosata, 3rd c.) — that Jesus was a man whom God progressively perfected and adopted — was condemned by orthodox councils, but the modern variants persist. Modern kenotic theology (Gottfried Thomasius, P.T. Forsyth, Charles Gore — the Anglican kenotic tradition) holds that the Son in becoming incarnate genuinely surrendered some divine attributes for the duration of the incarnation; "made perfect through suffering" can on this reading describe a real developmental process within the kenotic frame. Process theology and open theism (Charles Hartshorne, Clark Pinnock, Thomas Oord) take the developmental reading further — God's experience is genuinely temporal, and the Son's perfection is fulfilled in time, not merely manifested.

The least intuitive position to most contemporary Protestants — the position to steelman — is **modern kenotic theology.** Steelmanned: the high Christology of Heb 1:1-4 and the deep humanity of Heb 2:10-18 cannot both be honored without some account of how the divine Son took on the genuine limits of human existence. Kenosis (Phil 2:7 *ekenōsen heauton*) is biblical, not invented. If Christ "learned obedience through what he suffered" (5:8 — directly parallel to 2:10), then there is something genuinely new in the divine-human life that was not there before. The strength of kenotic theology is that it takes the texts on suffering, learning, and being-made-perfect at face value rather than spiritualizing them. The weakness, classical theologians reply, is that surrendering divine attributes (omniscience, omnipotence) at the incarnation seems to undo the Son's identity with the Father in a way that is hard to reconcile with the eternal generation. The classical tradition prefers the Athanasian-Cyrilline solution: one subject (the divine Son) acts through two natures, and the suffering is genuine but does not entail surrender of divine attributes.

**Where this leaves us:** Hebrews 2:10 cannot be read in a way that makes Christ's perfection developmental in the adoptionist sense without breaking the chapter's high Christology; but a careful reading of *teleioō* as the completion of the saving mission, combined with an honest reckoning with the kenotic insights, holds the tension.

**Tension 2: Christus Victor atonement.** Heb 2:14-15 — "that through death he might destroy *katargēsē* the one who has the power of death, that is, the devil, and deliver all those who through fear of death were subject to lifelong slavery" — is the strongest Christus-Victor text in the NT. Aulén's *Christus Victor* argued that this frame, dominant in patristic theology and in the Eastern liturgy, was eclipsed in the West by Anselm's satisfaction theory and Calvin's penal substitution. What does Heb 2:14-15 actually require?

The classical Catholic tradition (Anselm, Aquinas, Trent) reads satisfaction and Christus Victor together: Christ both pays the debt of human sin (satisfaction) and defeats the devil's tyranny (Christus Victor). Reformed theology (Calvin *Institutes* II.16, John Owen *The Death of Death*) reads penal substitution as the deeper ground of the cosmic victory: it is because the wrath of God is propitiated that the devil's accusation loses its force. Lutheran theology (Luther, Gerhard) holds the two together with characteristic paradoxical force: the cross is both judgment and victory. Eastern Orthodoxy (Athanasius, the liturgy of Pascha, contemporary theologians like John Behr) has kept Christus Victor central. Abelard's moral-influence theory (the cross transforms by moving the heart) is a fourth distinct model. Girard's scapegoat-dismantling reading is a fifth.

The least intuitive position to many Reformed Protestants — the position to steelman — is the **Aulén-Eastern Christus Victor reading as primary, with penal substitution as a secondary lens.** Steelmanned: Heb 2:14-15 says nothing about wrath, debt, or imputation; it speaks of *katargēsis* (rendering inoperative), of fear of death, of slavery. The grammar of the passage is liberation from a tyrant, not payment of a debt to a creditor. The Pauline-style penal-substitution texts (Rom 3:25, 2 Cor 5:21) are real, but Heb 2 is its own theological statement, and its frame is cosmic-liberation. The strength of this reading is its respect for the actual grammar of the text; the weakness is that Heb 2:17 immediately introduces *hilaskesthai tas hamartias* "to make propitiation for sins," which the Reformed reading takes as the ground for the liberation just described. A satisfying reading of chapter 2 must hold liberation and propitiation together.

**Where this leaves us:** Heb 2:14-15 demands that any atonement theology give serious place to the Christus-Victor frame; but Heb 2:17's propitiation language demands that any Christus-Victor reading not collapse into mere liberation. The integrative readings (Aquinas, Calvin held in tension, Tom Wright's recent work) come closest.

**One unresolved question:** If Christ took on flesh and blood to render the fear of death powerless, why does the fear of death still grip even those who have been told the news — why does the homily still need to be preached, decade after decade, century after century, to people who already say they believe?

## The Thread

- **Psalm 8** — the OT text Heb 2 reads Christologically. Read in its original setting, Ps 8 is about the dignity of human vocation in creation; in Heb's reading, it is about the dignity of the human Christ. The two readings are in productive tension and together illuminate the doctrine of theosis.
- **Philippians 2:5-11** — the Pauline kenosis hymn. The structural parallel to Heb 2:9-10 is striking: Christ humbled, suffering death, exalted. Comparing the two hymns clarifies the difference between Pauline and Hebraic Christological idiom while showing their common substance.
- **Genesis 3:15** — the *protoevangelium*: the offspring of the woman will crush the serpent's head. Heb 2:14's defeat of the one who has the power of death has long been read as the typological fulfillment. The connection is exegetically robust.
- **Hebrews 5:7-9** (later in the homily) — "in the days of his flesh, Jesus offered up prayers and supplications, with loud cries and tears... and being made perfect, he became the source of eternal salvation." 5:7-9 is the explicit echo and development of 2:10. Reading them together clarifies what *teleioō* means in Hebrews.
