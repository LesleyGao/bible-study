# 2 Kings 9: Jehu's Coup, Naboth's Field, and the Woman at the Window

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

The chapter narrates the central political event of ninth-century Israelite history: Jehu's coup of c. 842 BCE, by which the Omride dynasty (founded by Omri c. 880 BCE; Ahab; Ahaziah of Israel; Joram of Israel) was extinguished and the Jehuite dynasty installed. The political consequences were vast: the loss of the Phoenician alliance (Jezebel was Sidonian princess; her death in vv. 30-37 effectively ended the Tyrian-Israelite axis), the loss of the Aramaic-Israelite peace at Ramoth-gilead, the eventual loss of Transjordan to Hazael (cf. 10:32-33), and Israel's incorporation into the Neo-Assyrian tribute system within twenty years (the Black Obelisk of Shalmaneser III, c. 825 BCE, depicts Jehu prostrating with tribute).

The most consequential extrabiblical synchronism is the **Tel Dan Stele** (discovered 1993-1994 by Avraham Biran's team at Tel Dan; Aramaic; dated late ninth century; *editio princeps* in *IEJ* 43, 1993 and *IEJ* 45, 1995, by Biran and Joseph Naveh). Its boast — almost universally attributed to Hazael of Aram-Damascus — claims that the speaker killed "[*y*]hwrm son of [Aha]b king of Israel" and "[*A*]haz[yahu] son of [Jehoram ki]ng of the House of David." The stele's "House of David" (*bytdwd*) phrase is the earliest extrabiblical reference to the Davidic dynasty by name. The collision with 2 Kings 9 — where Jehu, not Hazael, kills both kings — has generated intense scholarly debate. **William Schniedewind** (*BASOR* 302, 1996) argues that Hazael claimed the political credit for kills that Jehu actually executed, possibly because the chapter implies Hazael's army wounded Joram first at Ramoth-gilead (8:28-29; cf. 9:15) — Jehu finished what Hazael had started, and Hazael claimed the entire victory in his own propaganda. Cogan and Tadmor (Anchor Bible *2 Kings*) consider the possibility that Hazael in some sense fomented or authorized the Jehu coup. Gary Knoppers extends the analysis: the stele is Hazael's own monument to the political reordering of the Levant in the 840s, and 2 Kings 9 preserves the same event from the Israelite-prophetic side. The two accounts are not contradictory if one accepts that Hazael's military pressure created the conditions in which Jehu's anointing could succeed; the divergence is one of credit-claiming, not of fact.

**Ramoth-gilead** (*Ramot-gile'ad*, רָמֹת גִּלְעָד, vv. 1, 4, 14) — the Israelite garrison town at the Aramean frontier (probable identification: Tell ar-Rumeith or Tell el-Husn in Jordan) — is the same site of Ahab's death (1 Kgs 22). **Jezreel** (*Yizre'el*, יִזְרְעֶאל) — the Omride summer palace — has been excavated by David Ussishkin and John Woodhead (1990-1996) and re-studied by **Norma Franklin** with the Mazar team. The Iron IIA monumental enclosure they identified (c. 290m × 150m, with corner towers and casemate wall) corresponds well to the palace where Joram is recovering from his Aramean wounds (8:29; 9:15-16). The chapter's geographical precision — the watchman on the tower (v. 17), the gate area where Jezebel's body is trampled (v. 33), Naboth's property within the Jezreel territory — is consistent with the excavated topography.

**Naboth's vineyard / property** (vv. 21, 25-26): the narrator emphasizes the location three times. 1 Kings 21 calls it a *kerem* (vineyard); 2 Kings 9 calls it a *ḥelqat ha-sadeh* (parcel of field) — Cogan-Tadmor accept this as natural agricultural variation. The point is the deliberate fulfillment of Elijah's prophecy in 1 Kings 21:19 — the dtr historian locates Joram's death exactly where his father's covenantal crime was committed.

The "woman at the window" (vv. 30-37) is one of the chapter's densest archaeological and literary intersections. The motif — a royal or noble woman appearing at a high window, framed in a balcony — is attested across the ancient Near East. Phoenician ivory plaques showing a woman framed in a window (the so-called "Woman at the Window" type) have been excavated at Nimrud, Khorsabad, Arslan Tash, Samaria, and elsewhere — in clear Phoenician artistic style and likely associated with the Astarte/Asherah cult. The image is also evoked at Judg 5:28 (Sisera's mother) and 2 Sam 6:16 (Michal watching David dance). Jezebel's deliberate self-presentation in v. 30 — kohl-eyes (*va-tasem ba-puk eyneha*, וַתָּשֶׂם בַּפּוּךְ עֵינֶיהָ; the *puk* is antimony-based black eye-paint, abundantly attested archaeologically as kohl pots), adorned head, looking out of the window — is the deliberate evocation of this iconographic type. **Janet Howe Gaines** (*Music in the Old Bones: Jezebel Through the Ages*) argues that Jezebel is performing royal-defiant identity at the window — not seductive primping, as later misogynist readings have it — and that the painted eyes are royal Phoenician self-presentation in death.

Three Hebrew terms shape the chapter. **Mashaḥ** (מָשַׁח, "to anoint," v. 3) is the standard royal-installation verb (cf. 1 Sam 10:1, 16:13); the substantive *mashiaḥ*, "anointed one," underlies the later "messiah" / *christos*. **Meshugga** (מְשֻׁגָּע, v. 11, "mad fellow") is a contemptuous term that the army officers apply to the prophetic messenger and that Jehu deploys with a knowing wink. The same root underlies 1 Sam 21:14 (David's feigned madness before Achish) and Jer 29:26 (the dismissive treatment of prophets); the term marks the royal-political class's standard contempt for the prophetic class. The narrator's own assessment of the prophetic word as authoritative is, of course, opposite. **Shalom** (שָׁלוֹם, "peace") repeats five times in the chapter (vv. 11, 17, 18, 19, 22, 31) — Joram's panicked questioning, Jezebel's defiant taunt, Jehu's harsh rejection. The repetition makes the absence of *shalom* the chapter's keyword.

## Lens 2 — The Literature

Genre-precisely, ch. 9 is a regicide-narrative integrated into the Elisha cycle and the dtr's larger Omride-judgment composition. Robert Alter (*The Hebrew Bible*, vol. 2) and Walsh both treat it as a single sustained narrative arc with three distinct movements: the anointing at Ramoth-gilead (vv. 1-13), the killing of the two kings at Naboth's field (vv. 14-29), the killing of Jezebel at Jezreel (vv. 30-37).

The chapter's literary architecture is built on the iteration of "Is it peace?" — a question asked five times by escalating speakers (two horsemen, Joram, Jezebel) and answered with escalating violence. *Shalom* encompasses both political peace and personal welfare; Jehu denies both. Sternberg (*The Poetics of Biblical Narrative*) and Polzin both treat the *shalom* repetition as the chapter's primary structuring device. The chapter's other organizing device is the chariot-driving signature — the watchman recognizes Jehu by his furious driving (v. 20), Joram and Ahaziah ride out in chariots (v. 21), Joram is shot in his chariot (v. 24), Ahaziah is shot in his chariot (v. 27). The chapter is, in Polzin's reading, choreographed around chariotry, the Iron Age's signature instrument of royal power.

Devices: dramatic irony in vv. 11-13 — the army officers initially mock the prophetic messenger as a *meshugga*, then receive the news of Jehu's anointing with immediate proclamation and improvised coronation. The cloaks-on-the-stairs detail (v. 13) has been read by **Brueggemann** and others as a deliberate anticipation of Mark 11:8 / Matt 21:8 / Luke 19:36 (the cloaks at Jesus's triumphal entry); whether Mark intentionally echoes 2 Kings 9 is debated, but the structural homology is there. The narrator's signature note about Jehu's furious driving (v. 20) is one of the rare etymological-character notes in the historical books — the watchman recognizes him at distance by the manic style of his horses. This is folkloric texture that Cogan-Tadmor read as evidence the chapter incorporates pre-dtr northern oral material.

The "woman at the window" scene (vv. 30-37) is one of the most carefully constructed in the historical books. Robert Alter, **Janet Howe Gaines**, **Tina Pippin**, **Cheryl Exum**, and **Phyllis Trible** have all given it sustained literary analysis. The pattern of preparation (kohl, hair, window), defiant address ("Is it peace, you Zimri, murderer of your master?" — a precisely calibrated reference to Zimri's coup of 1 Kgs 16:8-20, where Zimri killed Elah and reigned only seven days before Omri killed him; Jezebel is naming Jehu as the next regicide-traitor whose own end will be brief), the appeal to the eunuchs ("Who is on my side?" — Jehu's recruitment of the most marginal court figures against the queen), the throw-down, the trampling, the spattered blood, the eaten body, the prophetic word fulfilled — the scene is a self-contained literary unit of high finish. The note that "they found no more of her than the skull and the feet and the palms of her hands" (v. 35) is the most graphic disposal of a royal woman in the HB, and the chapter's narrator does not soften it.

The chapter's place in the book argument: ch. 9 is the climactic execution of the prophetic word against the house of Ahab announced in 1 Kgs 21:21-24 by Elijah (the dogs eating Jezebel; the dogs licking Ahab's blood at Naboth's vineyard) and confirmed by Elisha through the anonymous prophet-anointer of vv. 1-10. The dtr's apologetic construction is unmistakable: Jehu's coup is portrayed as the proper fulfillment of prophetic word, and the chapter ends with Jehu himself citing the Elijah prophecy verbatim (v. 36). **Robert Coote** (*In Defense of Revolution*) reads the entire chapter as Jehuite court propaganda — the dtr historian preserves a pre-existing Jehuite apologetic that justifies the coup as prophetic-divine warrant — but with a critical theological framing the dtr eventually overlays. The interpretive key is that the dtr will simultaneously celebrate the coup's fulfillment of prophecy and (in 10:29-31) condemn Jehu for incomplete reform; and the prophetic tradition (Hosea 1:4) will eventually indict the coup's violence outright. The chapter is preserving the dynastic apologetic that the canon will subsequently complicate.

Textual variants: 4QKgs preserves only minor fragments for ch. 9. The LXX (Vaticanus) has minor variants — particularly in the Hazael-Jehu chronological synchronisms — and Lucianic recensional material is more developed for this chapter than for adjacent ones, suggesting the chapter generated significant late-antique editorial activity. Cogan-Tadmor and Sweeney both identify multiple compositional layers in the chapter: a pre-dtr Jehuite apologetic core (vv. 14-26 and the Naboth-field reference), an Elisha-cycle anointing layer (vv. 1-13), and the Jezebel scene (vv. 30-37) as a possibly distinct prophetic-traditional unit.

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

The Jewish trajectory: **Josephus** (*Antiquities* 9.105-124) retells the chapter at length, with Hellenistic dramatic embellishment — Jezebel's defiant address is rendered as a public speech rather than a window-shouting; Jehu's response is a grand speech of religious zeal. **Rashi** reads Jezebel's "Is it peace, you Zimri" as a reference to 1 Kgs 16:9-15 — Zimri killed his master Elah and reigned only seven days before Omri's army killed him. Jezebel, on Rashi's reading, is naming Jehu's destiny as Zimri's: a brief usurper. (The narrative falsifies her prediction in the immediate term — Jehu reigns 28 years — but Hosea 1:4 will arguably vindicate it on a longer time scale.) **Radak (David Kimhi)** reads Jezebel's window-presentation as queenly composure in the face of death — not seduction or vanity, but the deliberate self-presentation of a Phoenician royal who refuses to die in private. **Ralbag** reads the chapter as a sustained exemplification of *middah keneged middah* (measure for measure) — Jezebel killed Naboth in his vineyard (1 Kgs 21); her blood spatters at the window of the same Jezreel where Naboth was killed. **Abarbanel** writes at length on the chapter's political theology — the dtr's emphasis on Naboth's field (vv. 21, 25-26) is the historian's signal that Joram's death is not random political violence but precise covenantal recompense. **Avivah Zornberg's** psychological readings (in *The Murmuring Deep* and adjacent essays) treat the chapter as a study in the moral weight of carrying out prophetic word — Jehu becomes the agent of divine judgment, and the narrative shows him both empowered and morally compromised by the role. **Yair Zakovitch** treats the chapter as a literary masterpiece of doubling — two horsemen sent and converted, two kings killed, two prophetic words fulfilled.

The Christian trajectory: **Origen** (catena fragments) reads the chapter typologically — Jezebel's painted eyes as the figure of the soul that adorns itself for judgment; the dogs eating her flesh as the standard patristic figure of moral self-consumption. **Augustine** (*City of God* 17.13, briefly) cites Jezebel's death as paradigmatic of the divine punishment of royal idolatry. **Theodoret of Cyrus** (*Quaestiones in Reges*) writes at length on the chapter — he addresses the moral status of Jehu's regicide, defending it as divinely-warranted prophetic judgment, but also noting the canonical complication of Hosea 1:4 (which Theodoret reads as condemning the *excess* of Jehu's violence rather than the killing of the kings as such). **Chrysostom** uses the chapter homiletically as the standard illustration of divine justice on idolatrous queens. **Gregory the Great** (*Moralia in Job*) cites the eunuchs of v. 32 ("Who is on my side?") as the type of the marginal saved by their proximity to divine judgment. **Aquinas** (*Summa* II-II q. 64, on killing; q. 42, on sedition) treats Jehu's coup as a paradigmatic case of divinely-warranted regime change — but with the Hosea 1:4 caveat that the executor of judgment can himself be morally culpable for the manner of execution. **Calvin's sermons on 2 Kings** are extensive on this chapter — Calvin defends Jehu's commission as proper prophetic warrant but is unsparing about Hosea 1:4's later indictment, reading the coup as showing that even rightly-executed divine judgment can carry moral debt for the executor. **Luther** in his marginalia treats the chapter as a sermon on the providential overthrow of idolatrous regimes. The critical-historical commentaries — **Cogan & Tadmor (AB 2 Kings)**, **Hobbs (WBC)**, **Sweeney (OTL)**, **John Gray (OTL)**, **Mordechai Cogan**'s separate essays on the Tel Dan Stele, **William Schniedewind** on Hazael's claim, **Gary Knoppers** on the dtr framework — give the dense scholarly base. **Frank Moore Cross** and **Richard Nelson** treat the chapter as a key node in the dtr's larger judgment-fulfillment scheme. **Brueggemann** (*1 & 2 Kings*) reads the chapter as a sustained meditation on the moral cost of revolutionary violence, even when divinely warranted. **Eugene Peterson** (Westminster Bible Companion) reads it pastorally as a study in the difficulty of distinguishing prophetic warrant from political opportunism. **Iain Provan** (NIBC) and **John Goldingay** (*1 & 2 Kings for Everyone*) emphasize the chapter's moral seriousness — Jehu does what he is told and ends up under prophetic indictment a generation later. **Ellen Davis** (*Opening Israel's Scriptures*) reads the chapter in tension with the Hosea text. **Robert Coote** (*In Defense of Revolution*) reads the entire chapter as Jehuite royal apologetic preserved by the dtr but eventually overruled by the prophetic canon. Feminist scholars have given Jezebel's death sustained attention. **Janet Howe Gaines** (*Music in the Old Bones*) traces the long reception history of Jezebel from biblical narrative to Renaissance painting to contemporary epithet — she argues that Jezebel's biblical death is misogynist in its method (the queen is thrown out a window by eunuchs, trampled by horses, eaten by dogs) but that her direct speech (v. 31) preserves royal dignity that later interpreters have systematically erased. **Tina Pippin** and **Cheryl Exum** read the chapter through the lens of biblical violence against women — the most prominent Phoenician royal woman in the HB ends as canine refuse, and the narrative's relish in her dismemberment is a problem the chapter does not solve. **Phyllis Trible** treats the chapter only briefly in passing but in line with her broader analysis of "texts of terror."

## Lens 4 — The Theological Intent

**Tension 1: The chapter's celebration of Jehu's violence versus Hosea 1:4's eventual indictment of it.**

Position A (dtr-apologetic, classical Reformed and the chapter's primary voice): the violence executes prophetic word; Joram dies in Naboth's field exactly where Elijah said he would; Jezebel is eaten by dogs exactly where Elijah said she would be; the chapter's first-order theological assessment is celebration of fulfillment. Position B (canonical-prophetic, Hosea 1:4 incorporated): Hosea, writing a generation or two later (probably c. 750 BCE), names "the blood of Jezreel" as something God will *avenge on the house of Jehu* — directly indicting the violence the dtr historian celebrates. The canon contains its own self-correction. Position C (synthetic-pastoral, Calvin and modern Reformed): the prophetic word can be rightly executed and yet the executor can be morally culpable for the manner of execution; Jehu's commission was proper but his execution was excessive; both the dtr's celebration and Hosea's indictment are correct.

Steelman of position B: Hosea 1:4 is unambiguous — God will avenge "the blood of Jezreel," and "Jezreel" is the geographic name of the very place Jehu kills Joram and Jezebel in this chapter. Hosea is naming the chapter's violence as itself bloodguilt. This is one of the canon's clearest cases of internal theological argument — the historical books celebrate; the prophets indict; both are preserved.

**Where this leaves us:** The chapter's first-order voice is dtr celebration of prophecy fulfilled; the canon's larger voice (read with Hosea) is moral indictment of the violence by which the prophecy was fulfilled. The two are not synthesizable into a single ethical position without violence to one or the other. The reader is asked to hold the chapter's theological satisfaction and Hosea's prophetic indictment in the same frame.

**Tension 2: Jezebel's death — divine justice or misogynist climax?**

Position A (covenantal-just, classical Christian and Jewish): Jezebel killed Naboth, persecuted the prophets, propagated Baalism; her death fulfills Elijah's specific prophecy and is in deliberate proportion to her crimes; the chapter is just. Position B (feminist-critical, contemporary): the *manner* of Jezebel's death — thrown by her own eunuchs from a window, trampled by horses, eaten by dogs, dismembered to skull and palms — exceeds any covenantal proportion and reflects narrative misogyny that the canon preserves uncritically. **Janet Howe Gaines** and **Tina Pippin** make this argument forcefully. Position C (defiant-dignity, recoverable reading): even if the *narrator's* treatment is harsh, Jezebel's *direct speech* (v. 31) preserves royal Phoenician dignity that the chapter cannot fully suppress; the woman at the window faces her killer with named historical reference (Zimri) and royal poise; the reader can recover her dignity even within the chapter's hostile frame.

Steelman of position B: the chapter does not need to dismember Jezebel's corpse to fulfill Elijah's prophecy. The prophecy required dogs to eat her flesh; the *narrator's* details — the spattered blood, the trampling, the bare skull and feet — are excess to the prophetic word and reflect the narrator's own affect. The reader who notices this affect is reading the chapter, not against it.

**Where this leaves us:** The chapter is ethically harder than the dtr's first-order frame admits. Jezebel did real evil; her death fulfills real prophetic word; and the manner of her death exceeds the prophetic warrant in ways the narrator cannot fully justify. The reader is asked to hold the covenantal logic, the prophetic word, and the narrative excess in honest tension.

**Unresolved question:** Is Jehu the chapter's hero or the chapter's antihero? The dtr presents him as the executor of prophetic word; the eunuchs throw a queen out a window at his command; the chapter ends with his casual "see now to this cursed woman and bury her, for she is a king's daughter" — a chilling line in which courteous burial is offered to the corpse he just had thrown and trampled. The chapter does not say.

## The Thread

Cross-references: 1 Kgs 21:19-24 (Elijah's prophecy at Naboth's vineyard) is the prophetic word the chapter is fulfilling, and the chapter cites it explicitly at vv. 25-26 and vv. 36-37. 1 Kgs 16:8-20 (Zimri's seven-day coup) is the historical reference Jezebel deploys at v. 31 — she is naming Jehu as the next regicide-traitor whose end will be brief. 2 Sam 6:16 (Michal at the window) and Judg 5:28 (Sisera's mother at the window) supply the literary-iconographic background for Jezebel's window scene at vv. 30-37. 1 Kgs 22 (Ahab's death at Ramoth-gilead) is the deliberate echo behind the chapter's geography: the same town where Ahab died is where Jehu is anointed.

Forward flag: **Hosea 1:4-5** ("I will avenge the blood of Jezreel on the house of Jehu") will indict the chapter's violence within seventy or eighty years — the prophetic canon's most direct rebuttal of a chapter the dtr celebrates. The **Tel Dan Stele**'s claim that Hazael killed Joram and Ahaziah complicates the chapter's historical reconstruction — Hazael may have wounded both kings at Ramoth-gilead, with Jehu finishing them; the stele claims political credit Hazael's army at most enabled. The **Black Obelisk of Shalmaneser III** (~825 BCE, British Museum) will visually attest "Jehu son of Omri" prostrating with tribute — the founder of a dynasty that wiped out Omri's house, still designated "son of Omri" by Assyrian convention because the polity itself remained "House of Omri" (*bit Humri*). The Athaliah of 8:18, 26 is now Ahaziah's mother and will dominate ch. 11 — the Davidic line will be reduced to a single infant in a temple cupboard within two chapters precisely because of the Omride-Davidic intermarriage that this chapter has just severed by killing Ahaziah at Ibleam.
