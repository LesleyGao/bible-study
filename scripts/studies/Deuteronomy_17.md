# Deuteronomy 17: A Copy of This Law

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

Deuteronomy 17 contains the single most consequential political text in the Hebrew Bible: the *tôrat hammelek*, the "Torah of the King" (vv. 14–20). Set in the Hebrew book titled *Dĕbārîm* ("words"), delivered in oration on the plains of Moab, this chapter finishes the judicial architecture begun in ch. 16 (local judges) by adding three more institutions: local court procedure for capital apostasy (vv. 2–7), an appellate court at the central sanctuary (vv. 8–13), and a constrained monarchy (vv. 14–20). Taken together with chs. 16 and 18 (courts, king, priest, prophet), the unit sketches a distributed polity with no single human institution holding ultimate authority. This is what Eric Nelson, in *The Hebrew Republic* (2010), and before him Josephus (who coined *theokratia*, *Against Apion* 2.165), identified as the Torah's constitutional contribution.

The opening verse on blemished sacrifices (v. 1) functions as a bridge from ch. 16's altar-polemic (vv. 21–22) into the courts and king. The apostasy law (vv. 2–7) handles the gravest local case: a covenant member who worships other gods or the astral host. The procedure is careful — "you shall inquire diligently" — the evidentiary threshold is two or three witnesses, and the accountability mechanism is that the witness's hand must strike first (v. 7). This is not a lynching; false witness kills the accused, so the witness's hand on the first stone is skin in the game. Roman Lex Cornelia de Sicariis and later Anglo-American perjury doctrine echo the principle without matching its surgical force.

The appellate-court law (vv. 8–13) resolves cases "too difficult" for local judges by sending them to the central sanctuary, where Levitical priests and "the judge who is in office in those days" decide. The phrase **לֹא תָסוּר** (*lō tāsūr*, "you shall not turn aside," v. 11) becomes, in rabbinic tradition (Bavli *Sanhedrin* 87a; Sifre on Deut 17:11), the foundational verse for the authority of the Sages: whatever the duly constituted court rules, one does not deviate right or left. This single clause grounds the entire rabbinic concept of *da'at torah* and the halakhic authority of the majority. Maimonides in *Hilchot Mamrim* 1:1–2 makes it explicit.

The King Law (vv. 14–20) is the chapter's center. Its structure is remarkable. First, the condition: "when you come to the land… and say, 'I will set a king over me'" (v. 14). The king is *permitted*, not *commanded* — and the phrasing ("like all the nations that are around me") already signals that the desire for monarchy is a concession to conformity, not a covenantal imperative. First Samuel 8:5–22, when Israel asks Samuel for a king "like all the nations," recapitulates this verse with devastating literary precision. Yahweh tells Samuel: "They have not rejected you, but they have rejected me from being king over them" (1 Sam 8:7).

Second, the constraints. "One from among your brothers" (v. 15) — native, not foreign. This is a nationality clause but also a fraternity clause; the king must belong to the covenant community he rules. Then four specific limits, each addressing a known ANE kingship vice: (1) no accumulating horses — the horse was the Iron Age military-imperial technology, associated particularly with Egypt's chariot force; "not make the people return to Egypt for horses" seals the link. (2) No returning the people to Egypt — the exodus cannot be reversed for military-economic gain. (3) No many wives — royal wives were political alliances, Solomon's wives being the paradigm case (1 Kgs 11); the king must not build international power through marriage. (4) No excessive silver and gold — fiscal restraint; the king may not accumulate private wealth beyond what his office requires.

Third, and this is the Torah's unique constitutional move: the king, upon sitting on the throne, shall write for himself a copy of this Torah (**מִשְׁנֵה הַתּוֹרָה**, *mišnēh hattôrâ*, v. 18 — the very name "Deuteronomy" comes from the LXX's *deuteronomion* rendering this phrase, "second law" or "copy of law"). The copy is "approved by the Levitical priests," a clause that makes the priests the authenticating body for the constitutional text. The king reads it daily, learns to fear YHWH, does not lift his heart above his brothers (v. 20). The Torah is the king's constraint, and it is physically in his hands.

Hebrew terms that organize the chapter. **לֹא תָסוּר** (*lō tāsūr*, vv. 11, 20) — "do not turn aside," applied to both the appellate court's decision (v. 11) and the king's relation to Torah (v. 20); the same verb binds both to the law. **מִשְׁנֵה הַתּוֹרָה** (*mišnēh hattôrâ*, v. 18) — the king's Torah copy. **אַחֶיךָ** (*ʾāḥeykā*, "your brothers") — the kinship framing both of the king (one from among) and of the king's subjects (not to lift his heart above). **יָמִים רַבִּים** (*yāmîm rabbîm*, "many days," v. 20) — the promise of longevity tied to constraint.

ANE parallels throw the King Law into sharp relief. Neo-Assyrian kingship ideology (Esarhaddon's Succession Treaty, 672 BCE; royal inscriptions of Ashurbanipal; the Assyrian royal coronation ritual) treats the king as the gods' representative with essentially unlimited prerogative over subjects. The Egyptian pharaoh was a god-king. The Hittite king, as attested in the Mursili prayers, was more constrained — the king prays and confesses; the treaty framework of Hittite kingship may be the closest formal parallel to Deuteronomy's king-under-covenant. The second-millennium Hittite vassal treaty structure (Kline, Kitchen, Mendenhall) provides a plausible formal template. But even the Hittite king is not required to copy out the suzerain's treaty text and read it daily. Deut 17's king, uniquely, is a subject of a higher treaty with a living God who is the real sovereign.

This is the provocation that has made Deut 17 a foundational text of Western political theology. The king is *under* Torah. His accumulation is limited. His wives are limited. His military is limited. His heart is limited ("not lifted up above his brothers"). In the mid-first millennium BCE, when Deuteronomy's redaction was at or near complete, this was politically astonishing. Against the background of Neo-Assyrian royal ideology — which Bernard Levinson (*Deuteronomy and the Hermeneutics of Legal Innovation*) has argued Deuteronomy deliberately subverts — Deut 17 is revolutionary constitutional thought.

Dating debate. De Wette and Wellhausen place the King Law in Josiah's or late-monarchic context; it is plausibly a retrospective critique of Solomon (1 Kgs 10–11 reads as a point-by-point violation of Deut 17:16–17). Bernard Levinson argues that Deut 17:14–20 directly subverts Neo-Assyrian vassal-treaty kingship ideology (late 7th c.). Kitchen and Kline read the treaty structure as second-millennium Mosaic. McConville: form ancient, redaction later. Manuscript evidence (4QDeut^b, 4QDeut^q, LXX, SP) is stable.

## Lens 2 — The Literature

The genre remains legal code in oration. Literary structure: a bridging verse (1), a capital-apostasy procedure (2–7), an appellate-court law (8–13), and the King Law (14–20). The three units share a common phrase — "purge the evil from your midst/Israel" appears in vv. 7 and 12 — binding local judgment and central judgment under a single covenantal purpose.

The Apostasy-procedure unit (vv. 2–7) uses forensic vocabulary with notable precision. "You shall inquire diligently" (*dārašta hêṭēb*, v. 4) is the hendiadys of careful investigation. "True and certain" (*ʾĕmet nākôn*, v. 4) names the evidentiary threshold. The "two or three witnesses" rule (v. 6) — repeated in Deut 19:15 and cited in the NT (Matt 18:16; 2 Cor 13:1; 1 Tim 5:19; Heb 10:28) — becomes a cornerstone of biblical procedural law. The witness-strikes-first rule (v. 7) is elegant literary-legal design: the one who accuses must stake life on it.

The Appellate unit (vv. 8–13) uses architectural vocabulary ("arise and go up," *qamtā wĕʿālîtā*) to describe moving cases to the central place. The dual authority — "Levitical priests and the judge" — sketches a mixed tribunal of religious and judicial expertise; Maimonides in *Hilchot Sanhedrin* 1 reads this as the Sanhedrin's constitutional warrant.

The King Law (vv. 14–20) has its own literary elegance. Five prohibitions ("he shall not…") followed by two positive commands ("he shall write… he shall read"). The four negative limits (horses, Egypt-return, wives, silver-gold) are organized by the corruptions they prevent: military, political-economic, marital-political, fiscal. The positive commands — writing and reading Torah — are the counterweight to the negatives. Take Torah out of the king's hand and the prohibitions collapse into aspiration; put it in his hand, and he is constituted by it.

The literary signature of Deuteronomy pulses through: the repetition of "brothers" (v. 15, 20) framing the king as one of his people; the "heart" language (v. 17 on wives "turning his heart," v. 20 on "not lifting his heart above his brothers") echoing ch. 6's *šĕmaʿ*; the longevity-through-obedience promise ("that he may continue long in his kingdom, he and his children") echoing the parental Fifth Commandment (Deut 5:16).

Parallelism with 1 Samuel 8 and 1 Kings 3–11 is structural. 1 Sam 8 is Deut 17:14 narrated: the people ask for a king "like all the nations," and Samuel warns of exactly the accumulations Deut 17 forbids. 1 Kings 10–11 is Deut 17:16–17 in narrative: Solomon accumulates horses from Egypt, wives, silver and gold. The narrative books diagnose the monarchy's failure by the constitutional standard the king was supposed to have copied.

Textual variants. The LXX generally follows MT. The Samaritan Pentateuch has minor variants but preserves the King Law intact. 4QDeut fragments confirm stability. One interesting detail: the rabbinic reception treats the king's "copy of this law" as the full Torah, but at the text's level it may mean only Deuteronomy or only this law code; the ambiguity is productive.

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

**Jewish tradition.** Second Temple reception. Philo in *Special Laws* IV.157 treats the King Law as a demonstration that Israel's polity is ruled by law not by kings. Josephus in *Against Apion* 2.165 coins *theokratia* to describe the Mosaic polity — not clerical rule but the constitutional sovereignty of God's law over all human officers, including kings. The Qumran Temple Scroll (11Q19) col. 56–59 expands Deut 17 into a royal code with a council of twelve priests, twelve Levites, twelve laypeople.

Rabbinic tradition. Mishnah *Sanhedrin* 2 gives the classical rabbinic King Law; 2:4 specifies the king must write two Torah scrolls, one in his treasury, one carried with him always. Mishnah *Sotah* 7:8 describes the seven-year royal Torah-reading ceremony (*hakhel*). Sifre *Shofetim* on Deut 17 is extensive.

Medieval commentary. Rashi on v. 14 reads the permission to appoint a king as concession to Israel's weakness. Ibn Ezra reads the permissive tone more neutrally. Nachmanides takes the positive view: appointing a king fulfills a commandment; 1 Sam 8's failure was in the manner, not the asking. Maimonides in *Hilchot Melachim* 1–5 gives the classical constitutional treatment — selection (prophet-ordained, Sanhedrin-confirmed), limits, duties, subjection to Torah. Abarbanel, with his Italian-republican horizon, reads Deut 17 as permitting but discouraging monarchy — a reading that would influence early modern European political thinkers.

Modern Jewish thought. Jonathan Sacks treats Deut 17 as the ancient charter of limited government. Michael Walzer in *In God's Shadow: Politics in the Hebrew Bible* (2012) reads Deut 17 as establishing a "constitutional anti-politics" in which Torah circumscribes every human office. Moshe Weinfeld's AB argues that the King Law is late-monarchic and deliberately critical of Solomon. Bernard Levinson reads it as the most innovative constitutional text of the ancient Near East. Jeremy Waldron in *God, Locke, and Equality* (2002) draws on Deut 17's "brothers" framing for a theological-egalitarian reading.

**Christian tradition.** Patristic readings. Origen allegorizes the King Law: the true king is Christ, whose Torah is written in his heart. Augustine in *City of God* 5.24 discusses the Christian emperor's virtue in terms shaped by Deut 17 — limits, humility, Scripture-reading. Chrysostom preaches the King Law as warning against royal avarice.

Medieval political theology. This is where Deut 17 becomes foundational. John of Salisbury's *Policraticus* (1159), one of the first full treatises of medieval political theory, quotes Deut 17:14–20 at length to argue that kings are under law and tyrants forfeit their claim to rule. Aquinas in *De Regno* and *ST* I-II q. 105 a. 1 discusses the King Law as establishing a mixed polity — monarchy permitted but best when tempered by aristocratic and popular elements. Later medieval conciliarists use Deut 17 on the side of constitutional limits.

Reformation transformation. Luther's two-kingdoms doctrine drew on Deut 17 selectively. Calvin's *Institutes* 4.20 and his commentary on Deuteronomy treat Deut 17 as establishing the magistrate's subjection to Scripture; his most famous use is in *Institutes* 4.20.31, defending resistance to tyranny by "inferior magistrates." This reading shaped Reformed political thought for three centuries.

Samuel Rutherford's *Lex Rex* (1644) is the most sustained use of Deut 17 in the early modern period. Rutherford argues against the divine right of kings point by point: the king is from among his brothers (chosen by covenant, not inheritance alone); under law (the *mišnēh hattôrâ*); constitutionally limited; and when he violates these, forfeit. John Milton's *Tenure of Kings and Magistrates* (1649) uses Deut 17 to justify the execution of Charles I: if the king is "one from among your brothers," his office is derivative from the people. Eric Nelson's *The Hebrew Republic* (2010) shows how Deut 17, read through Josephus, Maimonides, and Abarbanel, became a primary resource for 17th-century English and Dutch republicanism — James Harrington's *Oceana* (1656), Algernon Sidney's *Discourses Concerning Government* (1698), Cunaeus's *De Republica Hebraeorum* (1617).

American constitutional thought inherits this line. The founders read Harrington, Sidney, Locke, and Montesquieu, all of whom had read Deut 17 through the Hebrew republic tradition. Samuel Langdon's 1775 sermon before the Massachusetts Provincial Congress preaches Deut 17 as the template for American government. The constitutional restraints on executive power — presidential eligibility, term limits, impeachment, the requirement that the president uphold a written Constitution analogous to the *mišnēh hattôrâ* — carry Deut 17's logic even where direct citation is absent.

Contemporary. Oliver O'Donovan in *The Desire of the Nations* (1996) retrieves Deut 17 for Christian political theology. John Howard Yoder's *The Politics of Jesus* (1972) treats the King Law as one of the Torah's great political-critical achievements.

**Convergence and divergence.** The convergence is substantial. Jewish and Christian traditions both read Deut 17 as the cornerstone of limited government, both ascribe to it the origin of constitutional monarchy and, indirectly, of modern republicanism. Josephus's *theokratia* — God is the real sovereign; human rulers are stewards — is the position that Abarbanel, Rutherford, and the American founders share. The divergence is on the king's necessity. Nachmanides and much rabbinic tradition treat monarchy as commanded; Abarbanel, Milton, and the republican tradition treat it as permitted but undesirable. The King Law's ambiguity — *you may indeed set* a king — has been read both ways for two millennia, and both readings have political consequences still operative in the modern world.

## Lens 4 — The Theological Intent

**Tension 1: Is the king a concession or a blessing?**

Three positions. The **concessive** reading (1 Samuel 8 itself, Abarbanel, much Jewish and Protestant-republican tradition): the king is a failure of faith — Israel's desire to be "like all the nations" is the diagnosis, and the King Law works to contain damage after a concession already made. The **commanded** reading (Nachmanides, classical rabbinic, much Catholic political theology): "you shall set a king" is a positive commandment; the failure of individual kings does not negate the institution. The **mixed-polity** reading (Aquinas, Calvin, Walzer, Nelson): the King Law permits monarchy only when embedded in a constitutional architecture (prophet, priest, court, elder) that prevents any single office — king included — from holding ultimate power. The ideal polity is not monarchy but mixed. Steelmanned on the least intuitive (commanded): Deuteronomy issues the King Law in the affirmative. The text does not say "if you must set a king"; it says "you may indeed set." The grammar is permissive but positive. To read it as mere concession imports 1 Samuel 8's later prophetic judgment back into the Torah's own voice, which is quieter and more neutral. If monarchy is a genuine gift of God — not the best of all imaginable polities, but a real good — then the King Law's limits are a positive constitutional design, not damage control.

**Where this leaves us:** the chapter refuses both triumphalist monarchism and reflexive anti-monarchism. A king is permitted — even, in Nachmanides' reading, commanded — but only as a constrained office under Torah, embedded in a larger polity. The chapter's political theology is neither pro-monarchy nor anti-monarchy; it is anti-absolutism.

**Tension 2: Where does constitutional authority finally rest?**

Three positions. The **textual** reading (rabbinic *lō tāsūr* tradition; Reformed sola Scriptura): ultimate authority rests in Torah as a written document; both the king (v. 18–20) and the appellate court (v. 11) are bound to it; the text is primary. The **institutional** reading (Catholic magisterial tradition; some rabbinic): ultimate authority rests in the duly constituted interpretive institution — the priests and judge of v. 9, the later Sanhedrin, the Magisterium — whose reading of the text is itself authoritative. The **covenantal-people** reading (Walzer, Elazar, some Reformed traditions): ultimate authority rests in the covenant community itself, through whose consent king and court are constituted; the people are the sovereign under God. Steelmanned on the least intuitive (institutional): a text without a community that reads it is dead text. The Torah Calvin and Rutherford wield is already the Masoretic text, mediated by centuries of Jewish scribal and interpretive tradition. The "text" they appeal to *is* an institutionally delivered text. Pretending otherwise — pretending that the text itself, absent an authoritative reader, can adjudicate — collapses into whoever happens to be reading. The institutional reading accepts the text as primary but insists that text + community are inseparable.

**Where this leaves us:** the chapter distributes authority without settling its locus. King is under Torah; court interprets Torah; people constitute king and court; Torah is given by God. No one piece has ultimate authority. This is the chapter's political-theological genius — and its permanent source of constitutional argument.

**Unresolved question.** The King Law assumes the king will "write for himself" a copy of Torah (v. 18). But what happens when the king refuses — or, more subtly, when the king produces a textually correct copy but reads it only as ceremony? The chapter gives no enforcement mechanism against a king who formally complies and substantively ignores. 1 Kings 10–11 shows Solomon with a Torah scroll in the palace and accumulating exactly what Deut 17 forbids. The text anticipates failure (2 Sam 7's Davidic covenant anticipates that kings will sin and be chastised) but does not solve it. The gap between the king's formal compliance and the king's substantive obedience is the gap every subsequent constitutional tradition has had to address, and none has fully closed.

## The Thread

**Non-obvious cross-references.**

First, 1 Kings 10:26–11:8 is the King Law as negative narrative. Horses from Egypt (10:28–29 — violating v. 16 directly), wives (11:1–3 — "Solomon loved many foreign women… seven hundred wives, princesses, and three hundred concubines" — violating v. 17), silver and gold ("the king made silver as common in Jerusalem as stones," 10:27 — violating v. 17). The narrator does not cite Deut 17, but any reader with the Torah open will hear the King Law being shattered line by line. The editor assumes we are reading with the Torah open.

Second, Matthew 4:1–11 — Jesus's wilderness temptations — is Deut 17 (with ch. 6, ch. 8) answered by the messianic king. The three temptations (bread, temple, kingdoms) test the King's accumulations: sustenance, spectacle, dominion. Jesus answers each from Deuteronomy ("man shall not live by bread alone," "you shall not put the Lord your God to the test," "you shall worship the Lord your God and serve him only" — all Deut quotes). The messianic king is the King Law obeyed perfectly; the wilderness is his Torah-copy test.

Third, Philippians 2:6–11 ("though he was in the form of God, did not count equality with God a thing to be grasped") reframes Deut 17:20's "not lift his heart above his brothers" Christologically. The King who does not lift his heart — who instead takes the form of a servant, who is exalted by God rather than by self-accumulation — is the King Law's telos. Paul is not citing Deut 17 directly, but the constitutional logic (the king among brothers, not above brothers) runs through the hymn.

Fourth, Revelation 19:11–16 — the returning King whose robe carries the name "KING OF KINGS AND LORD OF LORDS" — collapses the whole Deut 17 apparatus. The appellate court ("judge and make war in righteousness"), the Torah (the sharp sword from his mouth, the word of God), the kingly office — all are gathered into one figure. What the chapter distributes across multiple offices is finally held in one person, and at that moment the constitutional architecture both completes itself and transcends itself. The King Law, which has governed Western political thought for two and a half millennia, looks forward to a King who is his own constitution.
