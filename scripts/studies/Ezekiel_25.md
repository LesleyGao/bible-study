# Ezekiel 25: The Four Neighbors and the Recognition Formula

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

**Political situation**: Ezekiel 25 inaugurates the **Oracles Against the Nations** (OAN, chapters 25–32), a structurally fixed feature of major Hebrew prophets (cf. Isa 13–23, Jer 46–51, Amos 1–2). The chapter is undated in its superscription but its content presupposes the **fall of Jerusalem in 586 BC**: each of the four oracles turns on the neighboring peoples having *rejoiced* over the city's destruction. The chapter is to be located in the immediate exilic-aftermath window between 586 and roughly 580 BC, with **Greenberg (Anchor)**, **Block (NICOT)**, and **Zimmerli (Hermeneia)** all reading the four oracles as a single redactional unit composed near each other. The four targets — **Ammon, Moab, Edom, and Philistia** — form the *eastern, southeastern, southern, and western* perimeter around Judah, geographically encircling the fallen kingdom. The omission of **Tyre and Egypt** from chapter 25 is structural: those two are reserved for the extended treatments of chapters 26–28 (Tyre) and 29–32 (Egypt). The four neighbors of chapter 25 are *minor* powers whose offense was specifically to *gloat* over Judah — politically opportunistic, locally vindictive, geographically adjacent.

**Material culture**: The four named peoples are each independently attested in epigraphic and archaeological record. **Ammon** is attested by the **Tell Siran bottle inscription** (c. 600 BC, naming Amminadab king of Ammon), by the **Tell el-Umayri seal** (an *Ammonite* inscription bearing the name *Milkom-or* — "Milkom is light," confirming the Ammonite divine name *Milkom* known from 1 Kings 11:5), and by the **Heshbon ostraca** with administrative records of late-monarchic Ammon. The Ammonite capital **Rabbah** (modern Amman) — *I will make Rabbah a pasture for camels* (25:5) — is well-located archaeologically. **Moab** is anchored by the **Mesha Stele** (c. 840 BC, the longest Northwest-Semitic royal inscription, in which King Mesha of Moab boasts of throwing off Israelite suzerainty under Omri's dynasty — corroborating 2 Kings 3 from the *Moabite* perspective). The cities named in 25:9 — **Beth-jeshimoth, Baal-meon, Kiriathaim** — are all attested in the Mesha Stele as Moabite holdings, and Baal-meon and Kiriathaim are explicitly mentioned. **Edom** is attested by the **Edomite ostraca from Horvat Uza** (late 7th c. BC, in *Edomite* dialect bearing the divine name *Qaus*), by **Tell el-Kheleifeh** (Ezion-geber, the Edomite Red Sea port), and by **Bozrah** and **Sela** — **Teman** and **Dedan** named in 25:13 are attested respectively by the Negev highland sites and by the **Tayma inscriptions** of northwest Arabia. **Philistia** is anchored by the **Tel Miqne-Ekron royal inscription** (c. 670 BC, in which Padi son of Ysd, king of Ekron, dedicates a temple to *Ptgyh his lady* — establishing late Philistine royal naming and divine-name iconography), by **Ashkelon** and **Ashdod** excavations, and by the **Caphtor / Crete** origin tradition (cf. Jer 47:4, Amos 9:7) which is *archaeologically* corroborated by Mycenaean and Aegean-style pottery in the early Iron-Age Philistine pentapolis (Stager, Dothan). The *Cherethites* of 25:16 (Hebrew *kerithim*, כְּרֵתִים) are widely identified with Cretans, preserving the Caphtor connection. The *people of the East* (Hebrew *bene-qedem*, בְּנֵי-קֶדֶם) of 25:4, 10 are the Arab tribes east of the Jordan — semi-nomadic groups whose pasturing of *camels* (25:5) gives an exact ethnographic detail: by the early 6th c. BC the **dromedary camel was domesticated and central to Arabian pastoralism** (Sapir-Hen and Ben-Yosef 2013 on dromedary domestication chronology).

**Authorship & dating**: Ezekiel ben Buzi, exilic priest. **Greenberg** treats chapter 25 as Ezekielian and the four oracles as composed near each other in the post-586 window. **Zimmerli** identifies a redactional pattern — the **recognition formula** (*they shall know that I am the LORD*, Hebrew *we-yād'û kî 'anî YHWH*, וְיָדְעוּ כִּי-אֲנִי יְהוָה) closes each oracle (25:5, 7, 11, 17) — and reads this as a Deuteronomistic-Ezekielian compositional signature. **Block (NICOT)** notes the formula closes *almost every paragraph* in the chapter (six times in seventeen verses) and treats this as a deliberate literary ostinato. The four oracles are stylistically uneven: the **Ammon oracle** (25:1–7) is the longest and most developed; the **Philistia oracle** (25:15–17) the shortest. The dating cluster is post-586, pre-Tyre's full siege (which begins 586 and ends 573).

**Archaeological & textual evidence**: **The Murashu and Al-Yahudu tablets** (Pearce & Wunsch 2014) anchor the contemporary exilic-Judean community in southern Mesopotamia; the four neighbor-peoples are simultaneously named in **Babylonian Chronicle** entries and **Nebuchadnezzar's prism inscriptions** as targets of his subsequent western campaigns (the **Wadi Brisa inscription**, the **Nahr el-Kalb inscription**). **4QEzek-a** is fragmentary at chapter 25; the **LXX** preserves the chapter with minor stylistic differences from the **MT** but no major variants. The **Targum Jonathan** of chapter 25 generally smooths the Hebrew imagery — for instance reading *people of the East* as *Arabs from the East* — but preserves the recognition-formula structure intact.

**Key terms**: *bene-qedem* (בְּנֵי-קֶדֶם, "people of the East" — the eastern Arab tribes, 25:4, 10); *Rabbah* (רַבָּה, the Ammonite capital, 25:5); *Beth-jeshimoth, Baal-meon, Kiriathaim* (Moabite cities, 25:9 — all three independently in the Mesha Stele); *Teman, Dedan* (25:13 — southern Edom and northwest Arabia); *kerithim* (כְּרֵתִים, "Cherethites," 25:16 — Cretans / Caphtorites); *we-yād'û kî 'anî YHWH* (the recognition formula, 25:5, 7, 11, 17 — six occurrences in the chapter); *naqam* (נָקָם, "vengeance," used six times in the Edom oracle alone — 25:12, 14, 15, 17, the chapter's leitwort for tit-for-tat reciprocity).

## Lens 2 — The Literature

Genre: **Oracles Against the Nations**, formally a sub-genre of prophetic speech with stable structural conventions: (a) addressee identification (*set your face toward X*), (b) accusation (*because you said/did*), (c) sentence (*therefore I will*), (d) recognition formula (*they shall know that I am the LORD*). Ezekiel 25's four oracles are textbook OAN form, executing the four-element structure four times in succession.

The literary architecture:

- A. Ammon (25:1–7): two oracles, both ending with the recognition formula
  - A1. Aha! over the sanctuary (25:3) → handed to people of the East (25:4–5)
  - A2. Clapped hands and stamped feet (25:6) → cut off from peoples (25:7)
- B. Moab and Seir (25:8–11): Judah is *like the nations* → Moab opened to people of the East
- C. Edom (25:12–14): revengeful action against Judah → vengeance by Israel
- D. Philistia (25:15–17): revengeful action with malice of soul → vengeance with rebukes

The **fourfold structural symmetry** is striking but uneven. Ammon gets *two* oracles (a doubling that emphasizes its prominence as Judah's most consistent foe in the late monarchy). Moab and Edom each get one. Philistia, the most distant and the least immediately implicated in 586, gets the briefest treatment (three verses). The geographical sweep — **east (Ammon), southeast (Moab), south (Edom), west (Philistia)** — is *counterclockwise from the perspective of someone standing in Jerusalem*, encompassing every neighbor except the Phoenician coast (Tyre/Sidon, ch. 26–28) and Egypt (ch. 29–32).

The **recognition formula** is the chapter's structural ostinato. It appears at 25:5 (after Ammon-1), 25:7 (after Ammon-2), 25:11 (after Moab), 25:14 (varied: *they shall know my vengeance*, after Edom), and 25:17 (after Philistia). The formula's persistence suggests the chapter's central theological weight is not punitive but **revelatory** — judgment is the *medium*, divine self-disclosure is the *message*. **Zimmerli (Hermeneia)** developed his entire theology of Ezekiel around the recognition formula (his classic essay "Erkenntnis Gottes nach dem Buche Ezechiel," 1954).

The **leitwort *naqam*** (vengeance) saturates the Edom and Philistia oracles (six occurrences in 25:12–17, three each in those two oracles). The vocabulary is judicial-retributive: Edom *acted revengefully* (Hebrew *naqom*, 25:12); YHWH *lays vengeance* on Edom (25:14); the Philistines *took vengeance* (25:15); YHWH *executes great vengeance* (25:17). The pattern is **measure-for-measure**: Edom's *revengeful action* is repaid with YHWH's *vengeance*, the same word turned back. This is the *lex talionis* logic of the prophetic OAN (cf. Obadiah 15: *as you have done, it shall be done to you*).

The **Mesha-Stele parallels** are textually striking. Mesha boasts: *I built Beth-bamoth, for it had been destroyed; I built Bezer, for it lay in ruins ... men of Gad had dwelt in the land of Ataroth ... and I took it and slew all the people of the city*. Ezekiel turns the same place-names — **Beth-jeshimoth, Baal-meon, Kiriathaim** of 25:9 — *back* against Moab. The literary device is exact reversal: the cities Mesha boasted of *taking* are now named as the cities YHWH will *expose*.

**MT/LXX variants**: 25:8 — MT has *Moab and Seir said*, but the LXX and one Hebrew manuscript read only *Moab said*, omitting *Seir*. **Greenberg (Anchor)** retains the MT and reads *Seir* as a gloss preparing for the Edom oracle in 25:12–14; **Cooke (ICC)** prefers the shorter LXX text. 25:16 — *kerithim* (Cherethites) is preserved in MT and LXX, both reading *Cretans*. The **Targum Jonathan** of 25:16 substitutes the more general *Pelishti'ei* (Philistines) for *Cherethites*, smoothing the geographic specificity.

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

**Place in the canonical arc**: Chapter 25 functions as the *threshold* of the OAN block. Where chapters 1–24 narrate Judah's judgment (the sins of Israel against YHWH), chapter 25 turns outward: the same divine sovereignty that judged Israel now judges *Israel's neighbors* — and the criterion of judgment is precisely their *response to Israel's fall*. The OAN's theological architecture is **universal sovereignty articulated through particular judgment**: YHWH is not merely Israel's national god, judging only Israel; YHWH is the God of all nations, and *all* are accountable.

**Jewish trajectory**: **Sirach 49:8** singles out Ezekiel for *the vision of glory*; the OAN material is treated more cursorily but is preserved as part of the prophetic canon. **Rashi** reads chapter 25 philologically, identifying the geographic referents and refusing speculative midrash. **Radak** elaborates with characteristic philological care, especially on the Ammonite-Moabite-Edomite genealogies (these three peoples share a Lot-and-Esau genealogy with Israel, making the *brother-betrayal* theme acute). **Targum Jonathan** smooths the imagery and reads each *recognition formula* as eschatological — *they shall know* in the messianic age. **Mekhilta de-Rabbi Ishmael** (on Ex 15:14–16) connects the four neighbors of Ezekiel 25 with the *trembling of the peoples* at the Red Sea exodus, reading the OAN as fulfillment of the Song of the Sea's announcement that *the inhabitants of Philistia, the chiefs of Edom, the leaders of Moab, the inhabitants of Canaan* would be dismayed. **Pirkei deRabbi Eliezer 38** preserves an aggadic tradition that **Edom's offense** specifically was that Edom *guided the Babylonian armies through the wilderness passes* during the 586 invasion — a tradition rooted in **Obadiah 11** (*on the day that you stood aloof, on the day that strangers carried off his wealth and foreigners entered his gates*) and **Psalm 137:7** (*Remember, O LORD, against the Edomites the day of Jerusalem, how they said, "Lay it bare, lay it bare, down to its foundations!"*).

**Christian trajectory**: Patristic — **Origen's *Homilies on Ezekiel*** (homily 5 specifically treats the OAN) reads the four neighbor-peoples allegorically as *the four cardinal vices*: Ammon as pride, Moab as lust, Edom as wrath, Philistia as avarice. **Jerome's Commentary on Ezekiel** (414) treats the OAN historically and identifies the *people of the East* as Nebuchadnezzar's later Arab campaigns. **Gregory the Great's *Homilies on Ezekiel*** (twenty-two homilies) treat the OAN cursorily — Gregory's main interest was chapters 1, 10, and 40–48. **Calvin's *Lectures on Ezekiel*** (1565) treats chapter 25 exhaustively as a doctrine of *common grace* and *general revelation*: the recognition formula's universal extension — the *pagan nations* shall know YHWH — is for Calvin a foundation for the doctrine that **God's self-revelation reaches beyond the covenant community**. Critical — **Cooke (ICC 1936)**, **Eichrodt (OTL)**, **Zimmerli (Hermeneia)**. Contemporary — **Greenberg (Anchor)**, **Block (NICOT)**, **Odell (Smyth & Helwys)**, **Kelle**, **Lapsley**, **Wilson**.

**Where they diverge**: the **Christian patristic-allegorical** reading (Origen) treats the four neighbors as moral types abstracted from history. The **Reformed common-grace** reading (Calvin) treats the recognition formula as foundation for **general revelation** doctrine: *Romans 1:19–20*'s *what can be known about God is plain to them, because God has shown it to them* is for Calvin pre-figured here. The **Catholic *Lumen Gentium* 16** tradition extends this: **non-Christian peoples participate in salvation history through their (often imperfect) knowledge of God** — a doctrine the Vatican II council grounded in part in the OAN's recognition-formula universalism. The **Jewish particularist** reading (Rashi, Targum Jonathan) keeps the recognition formula tied specifically to YHWH's covenant action — the nations *come to know* YHWH only through Israel's vindication. The **critical-historical** reading treats the OAN as Ezekiel's diaspora-rhetoric to the exilic community: the message is not really *to* Ammon and Moab but *to the exiles*, reassuring them that their oppressors will fall.

## Lens 4 — The Theological Intent

**Tension 1: Schadenfreude as judgment-trigger — what is the moral and theological status of *rejoicing over an enemy's fall*?** The chapter's striking feature is that it treats *Aha!* (Hebrew *he'aḥ*, הֶאָח, 25:3 — an exclamation of triumphant glee) as itself the indictable offense. The four neighbors are not condemned for *attacking* Judah (they did not, in 586) but for *rejoicing* at Judah's fall. The clapping of hands, stamping of feet, malice of soul (25:6, 15) — these gestures of celebration are what brings the divine sentence.

**Catholic fraternal-charity tradition** (Aquinas *ST* II-II.31–34 on charity, II-II.36 on envy) treats *gaudium de malis aliorum* — joy in another's misfortune — as a vice opposed to charity, falling under the broader genus of *malevolentia*. The **Catholic Catechism** (CCC 2538–2540) treats envy and its companion-vice schadenfreude as gravely incompatible with neighbor-love. **Reformed common-humanity doctrine** (Calvin Institutes 2.8.39, on the sixth commandment) extends this: every human bears the divine image, and rejoicing at any human's destruction violates the imago. **Anabaptist enemy-love** (Schleitheim Confession 6, the radical-reformation Sermon-on-the-Mount tradition) reads Matt 5:44 (*love your enemies*) as the explicit Christian commandment that *forbids* schadenfreude over even one's actual enemies — and Ezekiel 25's condemnation of Ammon-Moab-Edom-Philistia reads as the *prophetic foundation* for the Sermon on the Mount's enemy-love teaching. **Proverbs 24:17** (*do not rejoice when your enemy falls, and let not your heart be glad when he stumbles*) and **Obadiah 12** (*do not gloat over the day of your brother's misfortune*) supply the inner-canonical sapiential grounding.

The least intuitive position to a modern reader who treats schadenfreude as a minor moral irritant is **that it is a covenant-breaking offense severe enough to bring national destruction**. The steelman: the four neighbor-peoples did nothing *active* against Judah in 586; their offense was *purely affective* — clapping, stamping, saying *Aha!*. If this is sufficient grounds for divine judgment, then the *interior life*, the *configuration of the heart*, is not a private matter but is itself under judgment. The chapter's strongest moral claim is that *what we feel about our neighbor's suffering* is the test of our standing before God — a teaching the Catholic confessional tradition, the Reformed conscience-tradition, and the Anabaptist enemy-love tradition all converge on, however different their applications.

Where this leaves us: Ezekiel 25 establishes the principle that *rejoicing over an enemy's fall* is itself the sin — not because the enemy is innocent, not because the suffering is undeserved, but because the *configuration of the rejoicer's heart* fails the test of charity. The unresolved question: where is the line between *rejoicing in justice done* (cf. Rev 18:20, *rejoice over her, O heaven*) and *rejoicing in another's destruction*? The chapter does not draw the line; it puts the question on the table.

**Tension 2: The recognition formula's universal extension — does YHWH's *self-revelation through judgment* extend to *all* peoples?** The chapter applies the recognition formula six times, once for each of four pagan-neighbor peoples plus internal repetitions. The formula is not *Israel will know* but *they* (Ammon, Moab, Edom, Philistia) *will know*. The implication is that the four pagan peoples *come to know YHWH* — through judgment, in the moment of their destruction.

**Catholic *Lumen Gentium* 16** reads this as foundation for the doctrine that **non-Christian peoples participate in the divine self-revelation through their own histories and traditions**, even as that participation is imperfect and oriented (in Catholic teaching) toward fulfillment in Christ. **Pauline Romans 1:19–20** (*what can be known about God is plain to them, because God has shown it to them*) and **Acts 17:22–31** (Paul at the Areopagus: *what therefore you worship as unknown, this I proclaim to you*) supply the New Testament foundation; Ezekiel 25 supplies the Old Testament prefiguration. **Christian universalist tradition** (Origen *De Principiis*; **Gregory of Nyssa's *On the Soul and the Resurrection***; modern: Bulgakov, Balthasar's *Dare We Hope*) reads the recognition formula's universal extension as anticipating eschatological *apokatastasis* — the eventual restoration of *all things*, all peoples coming to know YHWH not only through judgment but through ultimate reconciliation. **Reformed double-predestination** (Calvin Institutes 3.21–24, the Westminster Confession 3) reads the same recognition formula very differently: the pagan peoples come to know YHWH *as judge*, not as savior; their recognition is *eschatologically belated*, not redemptive. **Jewish particularist** reading (Targum Jonathan, Rashi) keeps the recognition tied to YHWH's vindication of Israel — the nations come to know YHWH only insofar as Israel is restored.

The least intuitive position to a Reformed reader is **the Catholic Lumen Gentium 16 / Christian universalist** reading that the pagan peoples' recognition is *salvific-participatory* and not merely judicial. The steelman: the canonical text repeats the recognition formula six times in seventeen verses — its central theological weight is *self-revelation*, not *destruction*. If destruction were the message, the formula would not need to be repeated; that the formula is *the* repeated structural ostinato suggests the *coming-to-know* is the chapter's theological telos. Origen and Gregory of Nyssa took this seriously; the Catholic conciliar tradition has incorporated it; the Reformed tradition has resisted it. Whether the pagan peoples' *coming-to-know* is salvific or merely cognitive is the unresolved exegetical question — but that the *coming-to-know* is the chapter's central theological claim is textually unavoidable.

Where this leaves us: Ezekiel 25 inaugurates a theology in which divine self-revelation is *not* confined to the covenant community. The unresolved question: is the pagan recognition merely cognitive (Reformed reading), or is it the inauguration of a wider salvific economy (Catholic / Origenist reading)? The chapter does not answer; it puts the question on the table.

## The Thread

The schadenfreude theme of Ezekiel 25 reappears most directly in **Obadiah 10–14** (the *day of Jerusalem* against Edom — *do not gloat over the day of your brother's misfortune*), in **Psalm 137:7** (*remember, O LORD, against the Edomites the day of Jerusalem*), and is given Christological reframing in **Luke 23:34** (*Father, forgive them, for they know not what they do*) — the dying Christ refusing the schadenfreude that would have been his if he had cursed his executioners. The *recognition formula* of Ezekiel 25 is universalized in **Philippians 2:10–11** (*every knee should bow ... and every tongue confess that Jesus Christ is Lord*) — the eschatological recognition by *every* nation. The geographical sweep of the four neighbors — east, southeast, south, west — finds its mirror-image in **Acts 1:8** (*you will be my witnesses in Jerusalem and in all Judea and Samaria, and to the end of the earth*), where the same compass-rose is reversed: judgment-against-the-nations becomes mission-to-the-nations. The *people of the East* (*bene-qedem*) of 25:4, 10 is the same phrase used in **Matthew 2:1** (*magoi apo anatolōn* — magi from the east) — the eastern Arab peoples who were once the agents of judgment against Ammon and Moab now appear as the first Gentile worshippers of the Christ-child, an inversion the patristic tradition (Origen, Jerome) noted and built on.
