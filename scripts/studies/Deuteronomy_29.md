# Deuteronomy 29: A Covenant in Addition to Horeb

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

Chapter 29 opens with a formal introduction: "These are the words of the covenant that the LORD commanded Moses to make with the people of Israel in the land of Moab, besides the covenant that he had made with them at Horeb." The phrasing is striking. Moses is not narrating a covenant already made; he is ratifying a new one, *milbad* (מִלְּבַד, "besides" or "in addition to") the Sinai covenant. The Hebrew construction allows both "distinct from" and "alongside" — a grammatical ambiguity that has exercised Jewish and Christian interpreters for two millennia. Is the Moab covenant a renewal of the Horeb covenant, a supplement to it, or a new covenant in its own right? The book itself seems to want both: continuity with Sinai and genuine newness on the plains of Moab.

The setting is still Moab, east of the Jordan, in the last days of Moses's life. He is about to die (chapter 34); he has been speaking since chapter 5, and the speeches are approaching their climax. Chapters 27-28 staged the covenant ceremony and declared its blessings and curses; chapter 29 gathers the people into the covenant and extends its reach forward in time. Chapter 30 will add the promise of return after exile. Chapter 31 will transfer leadership to Joshua. The book is winding toward its end.

The covenant ceremony described in 29:10-15 has remarkable social scope. "You are standing today, all of you, before the LORD your God: the heads of your tribes, your elders, and your officers, all the men of Israel, your little ones, your wives, and the sojourner who is in your camp, from the one who chops your wood to the one who draws your water." Seven categories of Israelite society plus the *gēr* (גֵּר, "sojourner" or resident alien) and the lowest socioeconomic class (wood-choppers and water-drawers, likely conquered Canaanites in the camp, per Joshua 9:21-27). No one in the covenant community is outside the covenant. The listing is deliberate and has structural echoes in ancient Near Eastern treaty rituals, where the whole population of a vassal state is bound by the suzerain's oath.

On authorship and dating, chapter 29 sits close to the structural heart of the Deuteronomic History's theological program. De Wette's Josianic hypothesis remains the dominant critical framework: Deuteronomy is understood as a seventh-century composition or redaction, with chapter 29 contributing to the program of covenantal theology that frames Israel's history in Joshua-Kings. Weinfeld argues the covenant-making formulas align with Neo-Assyrian treaty practice. The conservative line, from Kitchen and McConville, notes that the covenant-assembly form has second-millennium antecedents and need not be late. The "next generation" perspective of verses 22-28 (looking back at the ruined land from the vantage of children and foreigners) has sometimes been read as a sure sign of exilic or post-exilic composition, but the same form appears in second-millennium treaty curses.

Archaeologically, the chapter has no direct physical correlate, but its frame of reference — the reduction of a land to "brimstone and salt... an overthrow like that of Sodom and Gomorrah, Admah and Zeboiim" (vv. 23) — draws on Israel's memory of the Dead Sea catastrophe and the Bronze Age destruction horizon in the southern Levant. The Cities of the Plain tradition (Genesis 19) and its geological substrate has been connected, with varying confidence, to Middle Bronze destruction levels at sites like Tall el-Hammam and Bab edh-Dhra.

Three or four Hebrew terms ground the chapter: *bĕrît* (בְּרִית, "covenant"), the word that defines the chapter's business; *ʾālâ* (אָלָה, "sworn oath" or "imprecation," v. 12, 14), the specific type of covenant involving self-curse if broken; *šōrēš pōreh rōʾš wĕlaʿănâ* (שֹׁרֶשׁ פֹּרֶה רֹאשׁ וְלַעֲנָה, "root bearing poisonous and bitter fruit," v. 18), the striking horticultural metaphor for covenant apostasy; and *hanniglōt* / *hanistārōt* (הַנִּגְלֹת / הַנִּסְתָּרֹת, "the revealed / the hidden things," v. 29), the final verse's contrast between disclosure and divine mystery.

## Lens 2 — The Literature

Chapter 29 is a covenant renewal speech with three main literary movements: a recital of past divine acts leading into present covenant obligation (vv. 1-9); the gathering of the people for covenant ratification (vv. 10-15); a warning against secret apostasy and a prospective prophecy of exile (vv. 16-28); and the famous closing verse on hidden and revealed (v. 29).

The structure echoes the ANE treaty form. Treaty preambles typically opened with the suzerain's prior acts on behalf of the vassal; the Hittite, Assyrian, and Aramean treaties all have comparable structures. But Deuteronomy 29's preamble is distinctive: "You have seen all that the LORD did before your eyes... the great trials that your eyes saw, the signs, and those great wonders. But to this day the LORD has not given you a heart to understand or eyes to see or ears to hear" (vv. 2-4). The praise of divine action is immediately undercut by acknowledgment of the people's incomprehension. This is a striking theological move. The same speech that rehearses God's mighty deeds confesses that the people have not been given the capacity to understand them. Sight without understanding, hearing without comprehension — the same formulation Isaiah 6:9-10 will use, and which Paul will cite in Romans 11:8, drawing on Deuteronomy 29:4 itself.

The covenant ratification itself (vv. 10-15) uses the formula *lĕʿobrĕkā bibrît* (לְעָבְרְךָ בִּבְרִית, "to cross over into the covenant," v. 12), a striking metaphor that may echo the ancient practice of passing between divided animals (Genesis 15:10, 17; Jeremiah 34:18). The covenant is entered through a ritual threshold, and all Israel — and all Israel not yet present — passes through it.

The "not with you alone but with those not here today" clause (vv. 14-15) is one of the most theologically consequential in the chapter. The covenant extends to absent members. In Jewish rabbinic tradition, this grounds the claim that every Jewish soul — even the souls of future converts — was present at the covenant-making (*Shevuot* 39a). In Christian reading, it opens the covenant to temporal extension: the people of the future are bound by the covenant of the present.

The apostasy warning (vv. 16-21) uses a striking botanical metaphor: "lest there be among you a root bearing poisonous and bitter fruit" (v. 18). The image is of secret corruption: a hidden root that bears visible fruit. The Hebrew *rōʾš* (רֹאשׁ) here is homonymic with "head" but means "poison" (likely gall or hemlock); *laʿănâ* (לַעֲנָה) is wormwood. The botanical pair appears together in Jeremiah 9:14, 23:15, and Lamentations 3:19, always as a metaphor for covenantal breakdown. Hebrews 12:15 cites the LXX of this verse directly: "lest any root of bitterness springing up cause trouble."

The prospective view of exile (vv. 22-28) is a literary tour de force. Moses puts words in the mouths of the *next generation* and of *foreign travelers* who will look at the devastated land and ask "Why has the LORD done thus to this land?" The narrative perspective shifts from the covenant-making present to a distant future in which the curses of chapter 28 have already fallen. The people will answer: "It is because they abandoned the covenant of the LORD... and went and served other gods." The chapter thus builds the interpretation of its own possible future into the present covenant-making.

The closing verse (v. 29) is among the most frequently quoted in Jewish and Christian theology: "The secret things belong to the LORD our God, but the things that are revealed belong to us and to our children forever, that we may do all the words of this law." The Hebrew contrasts *hanistārōt* (הַנִּסְתָּרֹת, "the hidden things") with *hanniglōt* (הַנִּגְלֹת, "the revealed things") — a contrast that has become foundational to both Jewish and Christian epistemology of divine mystery.

Textual variants are minor. The Masoretic chapter division differs between traditions: in the Hebrew Bible, verse 1 of chapter 29 in English translations is actually verse 29 of chapter 28, and English chapter 29 = Hebrew chapter 28:69-29:28. This minor numbering difference does not affect meaning but can generate confusion when consulting Jewish sources. Qumran fragments (including 4QDeut^k and related) support the Masoretic text for chapter 29.

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

### Jewish Reception

**Second Temple.** The covenant-extension formula ("with those not here today," v. 15) becomes central to Second Temple Jewish identity. Josephus (*Antiquities* 4.311-314) retells the covenant ceremony. The Qumran *yaḥad* read themselves as the "those not here" of Moab now standing before God. The *Damascus Document* and *Community Rule* (1QS) use covenant-entry language directly reminiscent of the chapter. *4QMMT* ends with echoes of Deuteronomy 29-30.

**Rabbinic.** *Shevuot 39a* expounds the "not with you alone" clause: "this teaches that he made them stand up, all the souls who were to be created in the future" — every Jewish soul, present or future-born, stood at Moab. *Sifre Deuteronomy* works the chapter line by line; *Midrash Tanhuma* and *Devarim Rabbah* elaborate the horticultural metaphor.

The closing verse (29:29) becomes a rabbinic classic. *Talmud Bavli Hagigah 13a* and *Berakhot 10a* cite it repeatedly for the principle that some things are not given to humans to know. Rashi explains the verse: the hidden things (the hidden sins of individuals) belong to God to judge; the revealed things (public transgressions) belong to the community to address. This reading turns the verse into a jurisdictional principle about communal responsibility: the community is responsible for what it can see, not for what is concealed. Ibn Ezra takes the verse more epistemologically: the hidden things are God's deep purposes; the revealed things are the Torah as given. Ramban combines both readings.

Maimonides in *Guide* 1.1 and 3.23 uses the hidden/revealed contrast as foundational to his negative theology: God's essence belongs to the hidden; commandments to the revealed. Judah Halevi in the *Kuzari* uses the same distinction; Mendelssohn in *Jerusalem* reads the verse as the foundation of Jewish epistemic humility.

**Modern.** Tigay's *JPS Torah Commentary* and Weinfeld's *AB Deuteronomy* give detailed treatments. Leibowitz's *Studies in Devarim* collects classical readings of verse 29. Heschel's *God in Search of Man* reads it as charter for divine pathos — God both hidden and revealed. Sacks returns to the "next generation" perspective as paradigm of temporal covenant extension. Yeshayahu Leibowitz uses the verse for his refusal of metaphysical speculation. Levenson reads verse 15 as the theological core of Jewish peoplehood.

### Christian Reception

**Patristic.** Origen's *Homilies on Deuteronomy* treats the closing verse as charter for apophatic theology. Chrysostom preaches it as check on speculation. Augustine in *Enchiridion* cites 29:29 as foundation of Christian epistemic humility. Cyril of Alexandria reads the covenant renewal typologically as figure of the New Covenant.

**Medieval.** Aquinas in *ST* I q. 1 art. 6 and I q. 12 art. 4 uses Deuteronomy 29:29 to distinguish between what can be known of God by natural reason and what can only be known by revelation. Bernard of Clairvaux preaches on the verse in his *Sermons on the Song of Songs* as a warning against speculative presumption.

**Reformation.** Calvin makes Deuteronomy 29:29 structurally central to his theology of divine mystery. In the *Institutes* 1.14 and elsewhere, he repeatedly invokes "the secret things belong to the LORD" as a check on speculation about divine decrees, the origin of evil, and the mechanisms of providence. The verse underwrites his famous refusal to speculate beyond Scripture's disclosure: "Let us rather be content to remain ignorant of what our God wills to keep secret." Calvin's treatment becomes canonical for Reformed theology. Luther, in his *Bondage of the Will*, uses the hidden/revealed distinction differently — distinguishing the *Deus absconditus* (God as hidden) from the *Deus revelatus* (God as revealed in Christ) — but draws on the same verse. Luther's hidden-God theology is one of the most controversial aspects of his thought and has generated a long tradition of interpretation (Oswald Bayer, Gerhard Forde, Eberhard Jüngel).

**Pauline.** Paul quotes Deuteronomy 29:4 ("a spirit of stupor, eyes that would not see and ears that would not hear") in Romans 11:8, citing it alongside Isaiah 29:10 in his argument that Israel's partial blindness is a mystery within God's saving economy. The citation is theologically loaded: Paul reads Deuteronomy 29 as prophetic of Israel's ongoing hardening within the covenant, which makes room for Gentile inclusion. Hebrews 12:15, as noted, cites 29:18 on the "root of bitterness."

**Critical.** De Wette, Wellhausen, and the source-critical tradition locate chapter 29 in the late Deuteronomic layer. Weinfeld and Nelson (*OTL*) give detailed comparative analyses of the covenant form. Römer and Schmid read chapter 29 as pivoting between Deuteronomic and Priestly traditions. Levinson treats the chapter as programmatic statement of covenantal theology.

**Contemporary.** Daniel Block (*NIVAC*), J. Gordon McConville (Apollos), Christopher Wright, Peter Craigie (*NICOT*), Duane Christensen (*WBC*), Walter Brueggemann (*AOTC*), Dennis Olson (*Death of Moses*), and Patrick Miller all treat the chapter extensively. Olson reads chapter 29 as the transitional moment in his generational structure, with the "not here today" clause anchoring the book's pedagogy of transmission. N. T. Wright's *Climax of the Covenant* reads Deuteronomy 29-30 as Paul's sustained storyline for Romans and Galatians. Karl Barth's *Epistle to the Romans* and *Church Dogmatics* invoke 29:29 in discussing divine hiddenness.

### Where They Converge and Diverge

Both traditions read chapter 29 as covenant extension in time and epistemic humility about divine mystery. Both treat verse 29 as a charter for theological restraint. Both see the "not here today" clause as foundational to ongoing covenantal identity. The divergence is structural. For Judaism, the chapter establishes the continuing covenant: every Jewish soul stands at Moab; Torah is the revealed thing given to the community forever; the task is the doing of all the words of the law. For Christianity, chapter 29's extension of covenant prepares for its further extension — Paul's reading of Romans 11 as Israel temporarily hardened so that Gentiles may be included. Barth's hiddenness theology secularizes the contrast between secret and revealed; Calvin's Reformed theology turns the verse into a canon against speculation. Jewish reception tends to keep the covenant embodied in community and practice; Christian reception tends to extend it temporally and ethnically into the Christ-event. Both readings draw on the same text.

## Lens 4 — The Theological Intent

### Tension 1: The Heart Not Given (v. 4)

"To this day the LORD has not given you a heart to understand or eyes to see or ears to hear." This is a theologically jarring verse. God has delivered Israel with mighty acts and signs, has sustained them in the wilderness, has brought them to the brink of the land — and yet, at the moment of covenant-making, declares that they still do not understand. The verse presupposes both that understanding is necessary and that it is not in the people's power to generate.

**Position A: The verse is descriptive, not prescriptive — Israel's failure to understand is their own.** Rashi, some medieval Jewish commentators, and a strand of classical rabbinic reading: the verse narrates Israel's ongoing obtuseness, which is their responsibility. The miracles should have elicited understanding; that they have not is a moral failure. This reading preserves human responsibility and reads the verse as indictment.

**Position B: Understanding is a divine gift that has not yet been given.** Paul in Romans 11:8, Augustine, Calvin, much of the Reformed tradition: the capacity for covenantal understanding is itself a divine bestowal. The verse confesses that the people lack what only God can give. This reading underwrites Augustinian-Reformed soteriology: human beings cannot produce the conditions of their own covenantal fidelity; grace must be given. The verse becomes proof-text for the doctrine of prevenient grace.

**Position C (steelman):** The verse articulates a temporal theology of gradual formation. Dennis Olson, Walter Brueggemann, Jon Levenson in his later work: Israel has not *yet* been given a heart to understand because covenant-making is itself the process by which that heart is being formed. The generation Moses addresses is in the midst of being formed; forty years in the wilderness is catechesis, not completion. The heart-to-understand is the goal of the pedagogy that the book itself performs. On this reading, Deuteronomy is not telling a story about grace withheld but about grace being given through the very process of hearing, standing, confessing, entering. The verse is confession rather than indictment.

**Where this leaves us:** Each position captures something genuine. The indictment reading preserves moral seriousness; the grace reading preserves divine initiative; the formation reading preserves the book's pedagogical character. What the verse resists is any neat account in which either human agency or divine initiative exclusively owns the cause of understanding. The covenant is entered with a heart still being given.

### Tension 2: Hidden and Revealed (v. 29)

"The secret things belong to the LORD our God, but the things that are revealed belong to us and to our children forever." What is the relationship between the two?

**Position A: The distinction is jurisdictional — what is revealed is ours to obey, what is hidden is God's to judge.** Rashi, much rabbinic tradition: the verse is a practical division of labor. The community responds to public transgressions; God handles hidden sins. This reading keeps the verse operational: it is about the community's responsibility, not about epistemology.

**Position B: The distinction is epistemological — there are truths about God that cannot be known by creatures.** Maimonides, Aquinas, Calvin: the verse is a canon for theology. Scripture discloses what God wills us to know; beyond that, speculation is forbidden. This reading underwrites the apophatic traditions in both Judaism and Christianity.

**Position C (steelman):** The distinction is existential — some things are only known by being done. Barth, Heschel, Jonathan Sacks: "the things that are revealed belong to us and to our children forever, *that we may do* all the words of this law." The revealed is not primarily information but instruction; understanding comes in doing. The hidden is not primarily information withheld but a mystery that cannot be captured in propositions. On this reading, the verse is not about what to think about God but about how to live the covenant. Knowledge is praxis. This is closer to Levinas than to Maimonides, but it has rabbinic antecedents.

**Where this leaves us:** The three readings are not mutually exclusive; they describe three dimensions of the same verse. The verse is jurisdictional (the community is responsible for the revealed), epistemological (there are limits to human knowledge of God), and existential (the revealed is for doing, the hidden is for trusting). What it does not permit is theological arrogance — the claim to know what has not been disclosed — or theological paralysis — the claim that nothing has been disclosed. The doing of the law is how the hidden and revealed are held together.

### Unresolved Question

If Deuteronomy 29:4 confesses that the people have not yet been given the heart to understand, and if Paul reads this verse as describing Israel's continuing condition until the fullness of the Gentiles (Romans 11:8, 25-26), then what is the covenantal status of a people addressed by a covenant they cannot fully understand? The book's own answer comes in chapter 30 — "the LORD your God will circumcise your heart and the heart of your offspring, so that you will love the LORD your God with all your heart" (30:6) — but the intervening space, between 29:4 and 30:6, is the space in which Israel actually lives most of its history. What is the theological character of the covenantal middle, when the heart has not yet been given and yet the covenant binds?

## The Thread

- **Jeremiah 31:31-34** — "Behold, the days are coming, declares the LORD, when I will make a new covenant with the house of Israel... I will put my law within them, and I will write it on their hearts." The prophet's "new covenant" language directly addresses Deuteronomy 29:4's confession: the heart not given will be given. Jeremiah reads the Deuteronomic covenant as preparatory to the covenant that writes itself on the heart. The Christian New Testament takes up this language at Hebrews 8, fusing Deuteronomy 29's diagnosis with Jeremiah 31's cure.
- **Ezekiel 36:26-27** — "I will give you a new heart, and a new spirit I will put within you. I will remove the heart of stone from your flesh and give you a heart of flesh." Again, the prophetic answer to Deuteronomy 29:4. The Holiness prophet frames the heart-not-yet-given as the explicit problem for which the new spirit is the solution. Reformed theology has read Ezekiel 36 and Deuteronomy 30 together with Deuteronomy 29 as a sustained theological arc.
- **Romans 11:8, 32** — "God gave them a spirit of stupor, eyes that would not see and ears that would not hear, down to this very day." Paul cites Deuteronomy 29:4 verbatim in his argument that Israel's partial hardening is within God's saving purpose. Romans 11 is Paul's most sustained engagement with Deuteronomy 29's theological puzzle, and his conclusion — "God has consigned all to disobedience, that he may have mercy on all" — is a restatement of Deuteronomy 29:4 as mystery rather than indictment.
- **Hebrews 12:15** — "See to it that no one fails to obtain the grace of God; that no 'root of bitterness' springs up and causes trouble, and by it many become defiled." The epistle cites Deuteronomy 29:18 verbatim, using the Moab covenant's warning against secret apostasy as instruction for the Christian community. The "root of bitterness" image moves from Moab to the early church as a continuing danger of covenant life: the small hidden corruption that spreads to the whole.
