# Matthew 25: The Last Three Parables and the Last Judgment

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

Matthew 25 continues the Olivet Discourse on the western slope of the Mount of Olives, looking back across the Kidron toward the Temple. The chapter delivers three eschatological tableaux — the Ten Virgins, the Talents, and the Sheep and the Goats — that bring Matthew's fifth and final discourse to its climax. Each scene draws on familiar institutions of first-century Galilean and Judean life.

The **wedding processional** of the first parable maps directly onto attested Jewish wedding practice in the late Second Temple period. Sources including m. Ketubot 1-2, m. Sotah 9:14, and the wedding scenes preserved in Tobit and Joseph and Aseneth describe the multi-day ceremony: the *erusin* (betrothal) was a binding legal contract, often months before the *nissuin* (the consummation feast). The bridegroom would come with his attendants from his father's house to fetch the bride, ideally at night, accompanied by torch-bearers; the bridal party would process to the bridegroom's home for the seven-day banquet. The "lamps" (***lampades***, λαμπάδες, v. 1) are most plausibly **torches** — wooden staves wrapped with oil-soaked rags, requiring periodic re-soaking from a flask of olive oil to keep burning. Excavated **Herodian-era pottery oil flasks** from sites like Magdala, Capernaum, and Bethsaida show the small ceramic *aryballoi* the foolish virgins forgot. The "midnight cry" (v. 6) reflects the customary late-arrival of the bridegroom — wedding processions intentionally delayed until after dark for festal effect.

The **economic world** of the second parable is concretely Greco-Roman commercial. The ***talanton*** (τάλαντον) was the largest weight-unit of currency in the Roman world — by Roman imperial standards roughly **6,000 denarii**, or approximately **fifteen to twenty years of agricultural laborer's wages** at one denarius per day (cf. Matt 20:2). The five-talents servant is therefore entrusted with what would today represent millions of dollars in purchasing power. Banking infrastructure existed: the verse "you ought to have invested my money with the bankers" (v. 27) reflects the *trapezitai* (τραπεζῖται, "table-keepers") — Greek and Roman bankers who accepted deposits at interest and lent at higher rates. The Hellenistic Egyptian papyri preserve actual deposit and loan contracts. **Burying coins as a wealth-preservation strategy** is well-attested archaeologically: the Galilee region has yielded multiple **coin hoards** from this period (the Tiberias hoard, Khirbet Qana finds, the Yodfat siege hoard) representing wealth hidden during the Jewish War. Rabbinic law (b. Bava Metzia 42a) explicitly endorsed burying entrusted money as the most secure form of stewardship; the third servant's defense had legal warrant.

The **shepherding scene** of the third parable reflects Palestinian mixed-flock husbandry. Sheep (white-fleeced, docile) and goats (often dark, more independent) commonly grazed together by day in the same flock — and were separated at night because **goats required shelter** while sheep could remain in open folds. The shepherd's daily separation of the mixed flock was a familiar sight on every Judean and Galilean hillside. The judgment image is also rabbinically resonant: m. Beitzah 5:7 and other rabbinic texts use shepherd-separation imagery for divine judgment, and the broader tradition of pastoral judgment is rooted in Ezekiel 34:17 — "I will judge between sheep and sheep, between rams and male goats."

Five Greek terms anchor the chapter. ***Lampades*** (λαμπάδες, v. 1) — torches/lamps; the same word at John 18:3 for the soldiers' torches in Gethsemane. ***Talanton*** (τάλαντον, v. 15) — the unit of weight/currency, originally a Babylonian-Persian measure adopted into Greek. The English word "talent" (gift/ability) derives from this parable's reception history. ***Adikōs*** vs. ***Pistos*** (πιστός, v. 21) — "faithful," the central virtue commended in both Talents and Ten Virgins. ***Ethnē*** (ἔθνη, v. 32) — "nations/peoples"; the question of whether this means "Gentile nations" (excluding Jews) or "all peoples" is decisive for the Sheep-and-Goats interpretation. ***Hoi adelphoi mou hoi elachistoi*** (οἱ ἀδελφοί μου οἱ ἐλάχιστοι, v. 40) — "my brothers, the least ones"; the most contested phrase in the chapter.

## Lens 2 — The Literature

Matthew 25 closes the **fifth and final discourse** of the gospel. The discourse-closing formula appears at 26:1: "When Jesus had finished all these sayings, he said to his disciples..." — completing Bacon's five-discourse structure. The structural parallel with the Pentateuch is now complete: as Deuteronomy 31-33 closes Moses's prophetic teaching with eschatological hope and ethical exhortation before his death, Matthew 24-25 closes Jesus's prophetic teaching with eschatological hope and ethical exhortation before his death.

The three parables form a triadic structure with a common theme — **eschatological readiness** — but distinct emphases. **Ten Virgins** (vv. 1-13) emphasizes *vigilance and personal preparation*; **Talents** (vv. 14-30) emphasizes *active stewardship of entrusted gifts*; **Sheep and Goats** (vv. 31-46) emphasizes *concrete service to the suffering*. Each closes with an outer-darkness/eternal-punishment image that sharpens the eschatological stakes. The triad escalates from the implicit (vigilance) through the active (stewardship) to the explicit ethical content (mercy to the suffering).

**Ten Virgins** is uniquely Matthean — no synoptic parallel. The parable's allegorical readability has driven debate about its origin. Did Jesus tell it as preserved? Or is it Matthean redaction, expanding earlier shorter sayings (the "watch" sayings of 24:42-44; Mark 13:35-37)? Davies/Allison (ICC vol. 3) argue for substantial Matthean shaping while preserving traditional core. **Klyne Snodgrass** (*Stories with Intent*, 2008) provides the fullest contemporary engagement with the parable's textual history.

**Talents** has a Lukan parallel — the **Parable of the Pounds** (Luke 19:11-27) — that diverges in striking ways. Luke's version is set in the political context of a nobleman going to receive royal authority over his subjects' opposition (an obvious reference to Archelaus's trip to Rome in 4 BCE to be confirmed as king of Judea, with a delegation of Jews lobbying against him); Luke's nobleman returns and slaughters his enemies. Matthew strips out the political frame and focuses on stewardship between master and servants. The parallels are close enough that source-critical scholars debate whether the parables share a common Q origin or whether Matthew has redacted Luke's source toward more domestic application.

**Sheep and Goats** is also uniquely Matthean. **Davies/Allison** classify it not strictly as a parable but as an *apocalyptic-judgment scene with parabolic elements* — the imagery of shepherd and flock is parabolic, but the framing scene of Son of Man enthroned over all nations is eschatological description. The structural parallelism is striking: the same six acts of mercy (feeding, watering, welcoming, clothing, visiting sick, visiting imprisoned) are repeated four times — twice in the positive (the King's commendation, the righteous's surprised question) and twice in the negative (the King's condemnation, the cursed's surprised question). Both groups are equally surprised. Neither knows what they did or failed to do. This is the parable's hinge: the ethical content of judgment is *invisible to its agents*.

The phrase "**the least of these my brothers**" (***hoi elachistoi tōn adelphōn mou***, v. 40) is grammatically and theologically the most contested phrase in Matthew. Earlier in the gospel, *adelphoi* ("brothers") consistently refers to disciples (12:48-50, 23:8, 28:10); *elachistoi* ("least ones") connects to the *mikroi* ("little ones") of Matt 10:42, 18:6, 18:10, 18:14 — language Matthew uses for vulnerable Christian disciples. This linguistic pattern grounds the major modern revisionist reading: that "the least of these my brothers" refers specifically to Christian disciples (especially traveling missionaries), not to all the suffering. The chapter's interpretive history hangs on this phrase.

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

**Sherman Gray**'s *The Least of My Brothers: Matthew 25:31-46, A History of Interpretation* (1989) is the foundational study of how Matthew 25:31-46 has been read across Christian history. Gray traces the trajectory in three eras.

**Patristic and medieval reading** (universal-humanitarian). For most of the patristic period, "the least of these" was read as the suffering generally — the poor, hungry, naked, imprisoned of any nation or faith. **Origen**'s *Commentary on Matthew*, **Chrysostom**'s *Homily 79 on Matthew* (one of his most powerful sermons, preached in Antioch and Constantinople), **Augustine**'s sermons (*Sermon* 38, 86, 113), **Gregory the Great**'s *Pastoral Rule* III.21, and **Aquinas**'s *Summa* II-II q. 32 all read the text universally. Christ identifies with the suffering as such, regardless of whether they are believers. The text grounds the **medieval Catholic seven works of mercy** — feeding, watering, clothing, sheltering, visiting the sick, visiting prisoners, burying the dead (the seventh added later from Tobit 1:17). The **monastic and mendicant traditions** — Benedict's *Rule* 53 ("all guests should be received as Christ"), Francis of Assisi's *Earlier Rule*, the Dominican commitment to preaching and works of mercy — explicitly drew on this universal reading.

**Reformation and modern reading** (universalist, increasingly contested). **Calvin**'s *Harmony*, **Luther**'s sermons, and the broader Reformed and Lutheran preaching traditions retained the universal reading: Christ identifies with the suffering as such. **John Wesley**'s sermons on the parable were among his most powerful and grounded the Methodist commitment to social action and abolition. The **Catholic Worker Movement** (Dorothy Day, Peter Maurin, founded 1933), **Mother Teresa's Missionaries of Charity** (founded 1950 — Mother Teresa cited Matthew 25 as her vocational text), and **liberation theology** (**Gustavo Gutiérrez** *A Theology of Liberation*, 1971; **Leonardo Boff**; **Jon Sobrino**) read the text as the locus of Christ's identification with the poor. Christ is *encountered* in the suffering. **Daniel Berrigan** and the Jesuit social-justice tradition continue this reading. Among twentieth-century theological readings, the parable became the most-cited NT text in Christian social ethics.

**Modern revisionist reading** (mission-restrictive). Beginning in the mid-twentieth century, a different reading emerged — pressed initially by **Joachim Jeremias** (*The Parables of Jesus*, 1972) and developed extensively by evangelical scholars. **Sherman Gray**'s study itself observes that the "universal humanitarian" reading was so dominant for so long that the alternative was barely visible until linguistic analysis of *adelphoi* in Matthew began to surface it. The revisionist reading: "the least of these my brothers" refers specifically to **Christian missionary-disciples** (cf. 10:40-42's "whoever receives a prophet... a righteous person... one of these little ones — a disciple"). The "judgment of the nations" is therefore the judgment of *Gentile peoples* based on their treatment of Christian gospel-bearers. **D. A. Carson** (commentary, 1984) and **Craig Blomberg** (*Interpreting the Parables*, 1990) defend this reading. Among major modern commentaries, this reading is supported with varying force by **R. T. France** (NICNT — cautiously) and **Hagner** (WBC); it is opposed by **Davies/Allison** (ICC), **Luz** (*Hermeneia*), **Keener** (NICNT), and **Harrington** (Sacra Pagina), all of whom return to the universal reading.

**Amy-Jill Levine**'s *Short Stories by Jesus* (2014) provides important counter-readings to the standard interpretations of all three parables. On the **Talents**, Levine offers a striking liberation-theological re-reading: *the third servant might be the hero*. The master describes himself (vv. 24-26) as "a hard man, reaping where you did not sow" — language that aligns the master with the exploitative landowners of first-century Galilee, not with God. The third servant's refusal to participate in usurious banking (which Torah-observant Jews would have viewed with suspicion under Lev 25:36-37, Deut 23:19-20) is, on Levine's reading, righteous resistance. The "outer darkness" punishment is then the cost of refusing complicity with an unjust system. Levine's reading is contested but valuable; it forces honest engagement with the master's self-description and the parable's economic context.

On the **Ten Virgins**, Levine notes the troubling refusal of the wise virgins to share oil — a refusal that fits the parable's allegorical logic (you cannot share salvation/perseverance/works) but sits awkwardly with the Sermon on the Mount's ethics of generosity. The patristic readings of the oil are diverse: **Origen** (Holy Spirit), **Augustine** (charity/love), **Chrysostom** (almsgiving), **Hilary of Poitiers** (faith and works combined), **Cyril of Alexandria** (perseverance). The diversity itself reveals the parable's resistance to one-to-one allegory.

**N. T. Wright** (*Matthew for Everyone*, 2004) reads the three parables within his broader narrative framework: as judgment-warning to Israel facing 70 CE, the parables proclaim that Israel's leaders have been unprepared (Ten Virgins), have buried what was entrusted (Talents), and have failed to recognize Christ in the suffering (Sheep and Goats). Wright resists pure futurist readings; the parables operate first within the chapter's first-century crisis, and only secondarily as far-eschatological description.

**Robert Capon** (*The Parables of Judgment*, 1989) reads the chapter as a sustained meditation on grace and works that resists both legalism and antinomianism — Capon's idiosyncratic but theologically rich engagement worth noting.

## Lens 4 — The Theological Intent

**Tension 1: Who are "the least of these my brothers" (v. 40)?** Three positions deserve full steelmanning. **(1) Universal reading** (patristic mainstream, medieval Catholic, Wesleyan, liberation theology, much modern Catholic and mainline Protestant scholarship including Davies/Allison, Luz, Keener, Harrington) — "the least of these" is all the suffering: hungry, thirsty, stranger, naked, sick, imprisoned, *whoever they are*. Christ identifies with all who suffer; the nations are judged by their concrete response. **(2) Mission-restrictive reading** (Carson, Blomberg, much modern evangelical scholarship; with caution, France) — the linguistic pattern of *adelphoi* and *mikroi* in Matthew points to Christian disciples specifically, especially traveling missionaries; "the nations" are Gentile peoples judged on their reception of gospel-bearers. **(3) Mid-position** (Hagner, some patristic) — Christ identifies primarily with his disciples but the principle extends; the immediate referent is the church, but the church's witness extends Christ's identification to all the suffering through its missions. **Where this leaves us:** linguistically, position (2) has the stronger Matthean intratextual case (*adelphoi* in Matthew normally means disciples, *mikroi* connects to specific disciple-references). Theologically, position (1) has the stronger reception-historical and Christocentric case (Christ's universal identification with the suffering is too central to the gospel to be reducible to mission strategy). Position (3) is the most defensible compromise. The pastoral and ecclesial cost of each reading differs sharply: position (2) reduces the parable to a mission-support sermon; position (1) and (3) preserve its expansive ethical force. The honest reader names the linguistic case for (2) while preserving the theological case for (1) and (3) — and acknowledges that the parable has functioned in Christian history primarily as universal humanitarian summons, with profound consequences for Christian social action that should not be lightly relativized.

**Tension 2: What does "eternal punishment" (***kolasin aiōnion***, v. 46) entail?** Three positions. **(1) Eternal conscious torment** (Augustine, Aquinas, mainstream Reformed and Catholic tradition, much evangelical theology) — *aiōnion* means "everlasting" in duration; *kolasis* is conscious suffering; the punishment is unending conscious torment. The verse's parallel structure ("eternal punishment / eternal life") binds the two durations identically — if life is everlasting, so is punishment. **(2) Annihilationism / conditional immortality** (Edward Fudge *The Fire That Consumes*, John Stott in his later writings, Clark Pinnock, Greg Boyd) — *aiōnion* describes the irreversible quality of the divine action (the punishment is final, not the suffering eternal); the wicked finally cease to exist; the verse describes destruction not duration. **(3) Universalism / hopeful universalism** (Origen tentatively, George MacDonald, Karl Barth's hopeful universalism, David Bentley Hart *That All Shall Be Saved* explicitly) — *aiōnion* in Greek can mean "of the age to come" rather than strictly "everlasting"; God's love and Christ's victory will ultimately reconcile all things; *kolasis* originally meant *corrective* punishment (pruning) before evolving toward retributive sense. **Where this leaves us:** position (1) has dominated Christian theology for most of its history but faces serious philosophical-theological strain (the proportionality problem: how can finite sin merit infinite punishment?); position (2) preserves divine justice while accommodating divine love but strains the parallel-structure of v. 46; position (3) preserves divine love and Christ's universal salvation but strains the warning-force of the parable and the broader weight of NT eschatology. None of the three is exegetically untenable; each costs something the other two preserve. The parable does not adjudicate the systematic question; it warns the church that judgment is real, that its content concerns concrete mercy to the suffering, and that its outcome is not under our control.

**Unresolved question:** If the chapter's three parables form a triad — vigilance, stewardship, mercy — does the third parable (Sheep and Goats) describe the *content* of what vigilance and stewardship require (i.e., genuine readiness *is* concrete mercy), or does it describe a *separate criterion* of judgment (i.e., judgment turns on mercy independent of explicit faith)? This question is theologically explosive. If the first, the chapter integrates faith and works in eschatological judgment. If the second, the chapter teaches a form of works-based judgment of the nations that resists confessional reduction.

## The Thread

Matthew 25 weaves Daniel 7:13-14 (Son of Man enthroned over all nations), Ezekiel 34:17 (the shepherd-judge separating sheep from sheep), Joel 3:2 (the gathering of nations for judgment), Isaiah 58:7 (the fast that includes feeding the hungry, sheltering the homeless, clothing the naked — a near-perfect catalogue parallel to Matt 25:35-36), Proverbs 19:17 ("whoever is generous to the poor lends to the LORD"), and Tobit 1:17 (burying the dead — added later as the seventh work of mercy). The Talents-pounds parallel between Matthew and Luke 19 reveals different shaping of common tradition. Forward in the NT, the bridegroom imagery returns at Revelation 19:7-9, 22:17; the judgment of the nations returns at Revelation 20:11-15; the inheritance of the kingdom prepared from the foundation of the world echoes at Ephesians 1:4 and 1 Peter 1:20. Forward in Matthew, the Olivet Discourse closes at 26:1 with the formal closing formula, transitioning directly into the Passion narrative — Jesus's last public teaching is followed immediately by his arrest, trial, and crucifixion. Flag this: the "King" who will judge the nations (25:34, 40) is the same figure who will be crowned with thorns (27:29) within hours of finishing this discourse; Matthew's irony is that the eschatological King speaks his royal judgment-vision the night before being stripped, mocked, and crucified by the very nations he describes himself one day judging.
