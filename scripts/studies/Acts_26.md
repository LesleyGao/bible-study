# Acts 26: The Third Damascus Account and "Almost a Christian"

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

The audience hall (*akroatērion*) of the Caesarean praetorium in roughly 60 CE—the staging that Joseph Patrich's excavations have helped recover—is the most cosmopolitan venue in the entire book of Acts. Present are: a Roman procurator (Festus); a Herodian client-king (Agrippa II); his royal sister (Bernice); the *chiliarchoi*, the senior military tribunes commanding the Caesarean cohorts; the *exochoi tēs poleōs*, the principal men of the polis—Caesarean civic leaders from a Greek-speaking, partially Jewish, deeply Romanized port city; a Roman-citizen Pharisee in chains. Lee Levine's *Caesarea Under Roman Rule* and the Patrich excavation reports both make clear that this is the kind of mixed Greco-Roman-Jewish elite gathering for which Caesarea was uniquely engineered. Paul's opening gesture—the rhetorical *cheirotonia*, "stretching out his hand" (26:1)—is a recognized convention of forensic oratory; Quintilian's *Institutio Oratoria* discusses the gesture as a marker of formal speech. **Witherington III** notes that Luke is portraying Paul as deploying full classical rhetorical convention, knowing his audience.

The historical density of the speech is unusual. Paul's opening (26:4-5) anchors his claim to Pharisaic identity in language that **Josephus** would have recognized: *kata tēn akribestatēn hairesin tēs hēmeteras thrēskeias*—"according to the strictest *hairesis* of our religion"—uses the same superlative *akribestatēn* that Josephus (*War* 2.162; *Antiquities* 17.41) uses for the Pharisees. The ancient marker of Pharisaic identity was *akribeia*—precision in the interpretation and observance of Torah—and Paul claims it without qualification. **Daniel Schwartz**'s work on the Herodian dynasty notes that Agrippa II would have understood this claim immediately; his court was familiar with the Pharisaic-Sadducean factional politics of late Second Temple Judaism, and Agrippa himself, despite his Roman tutelage, retained the right to appoint the Jerusalem high priest and was therefore embedded in Jerusalem's religious power structure.

The third Damascus-road account (26:12-18) is the most theologically explicit of the three Lukan retellings, and it contains a famous addition not present in the other two: "*sklēron soi pros kentra laktizein*"—"it is hard for you to kick against the goads" (26:14). This is a Greek proverb attested in **Euripides**, *Bacchae* 794-795 ("*pros kentra laktizoimi*—I would kick against the goads"), and in Pindar (*Pythian* 2.94-95), Aeschylus (*Agamemnon* 1624), and elsewhere. The image is from agricultural practice: the *kentron* is the sharpened ox-goad, and an animal that resists the goad only injures itself. Cicero (*Tusc. Disp.* 2.5) preserves the same proverb in Latin: "*aliquantum laxiores habenas si est noster animus, sin contra, durius freni*"—a related agricultural-restraint metaphor. Paul, addressing a Roman procurator and a Hellenized Jewish king in a Greco-Roman audience, embeds a distinctly Greek proverb in a heavenly voice that speaks "*tē Hebraïdi dialektō*"—"in the Hebrew language" (26:14). The literary effect is bilingual: Aramaic theophany delivering Greek wisdom.

Three Greek terms in the chapter carry weight beyond their translations. **(1)** ***akribestatēn hairesin*** (ἀκριβεστάτην αἵρεσιν, 26:5) — as discussed, the technical Pharisaic self-designation; here *hairesis* is again neutral, "school of thought." **(2)** ***Christianon*** (Χριστιανόν, 26:28) — "Christian," appearing for the third time in the New Testament after 11:26 (the Antioch coinage) and 1 Peter 4:16. The term is still rare and probably mildly derisive in Agrippa's mouth—a Greco-Roman label for the followers of *Christos*, formed on the Latin pattern of partisan-naming (*Caesariani*, *Pompeiani*). The translation history is complicated: the King James "almost thou persuadest me to be a Christian" follows the Textus Receptus reading *en oligō me peitheis Christianon genesthai*, while modern critical editions (Nestle-Aland 28) prefer *poiēsai* ("to make"), yielding "in a short time you are persuading me to make a Christian." **F. F. Bruce** in NICNT and **Barrett** in ICC both translate the construction as a question, and most modern English versions render it temporally—"in a short time"—rather than as the Wesleyan-revival "almost." **(3)** ***ouk en gōnia pepragmenon*** (οὐκ ἐν γωνίᾳ πεπραγμένον, 26:26) — "not done in a corner." The phrase has no exact LXX parallel; it is recognized Greek idiom for a public, attestable event. **Pervo** notes that the phrase functions as a Lukan apologetic claim: the Christ-event is verifiable Roman-imperial history, not a private mystery cult.

The historical figure of **Agrippa II** comes into sharp focus in his single recorded utterance to Paul: "*en oligō me peitheis Christianon poiēsai*"—an idiom that resists translation but probably means something close to "in a short time you would make me a Christian!" (26:28). The tone is contested—Bruce reads it as ironic (Agrippa deflecting a serious religious challenge with humor), Witherington reads it as more sincere (Agrippa was raised in Rome but knew Jewish religious thought intimately, and the question is genuine), Marguerat reads it as Lukan dramatic counterpoint to Paul's earnestness. What is historically clear is that Agrippa II would within a few years be working alongside Roman authority to suppress the Jewish revolt of 66-70, that he eventually retired to Rome with Bernice and lived out his last decades there, and that his name appears on coins minted as late as the early 90s CE. The last Herodian dynast asks Paul whether he can be made a Christian.

## Lens 2 — The Literature

Acts 26 is the climax of the **defense-trilogy** and the third of three Pauline Damascus-road accounts in Luke-Acts. The narrative architecture is intricate. **Account One** (Acts 9:1-19) is the Lukan-narrator third-person recounting; **Account Two** (Acts 22:1-21) is Paul's first-person speech to the Aramaic-speaking Jerusalem crowd; **Account Three** (Acts 26:12-18) is Paul's first-person speech to the Greco-Roman royal-imperial audience at Caesarea. The accounts contain well-known variants:

- **Companions hearing the voice.** Account 9: companions "stood speechless, hearing the voice but seeing no one" (*akouontes men tēs phōnēs*). Account 22: companions "saw the light but did not hear the voice" (*tēn de phōnēn ouk ēkousan*). Account 26: silent on the companions' auditory experience. The apparent contradiction (hearing vs. not-hearing) is famously addressed by traditional harmonization (Augustine, Bruce NICNT): the Greek genitive (*phōnēs*) in chapter 9 indicates hearing-as-sound, while the accusative (*phōnēn*) in chapter 22 indicates hearing-as-comprehended-content; companions heard the noise but not the meaning. **Pervo** (Hermeneia) and **Marguerat** are skeptical of this Greek-grammatical distinction and treat the variants as Lukan literary variation, freely produced for rhetorical purpose.

- **Companions standing or falling.** Account 9: companions "stood speechless" (*heistēkeisan*). Account 26: "we all fell to the ground" (*pantōn katapesontōn hēmōn*). The rhetorical purpose seems clear: in the speech to Agrippa, Paul universalizes the prostration to dramatize the divine encounter to a royal audience.

- **Length of the divine commission.** Accounts 9 and 22 spread the commission across multiple speakers (Ananias, the Jerusalem temple vision); Account 26 collapses everything into a single Christophanic word. **Tannehill** in *The Narrative Unity of Luke-Acts* sees this as deliberate literary compression for the climactic third telling—Luke is consolidating in the longest Pauline speech the entire commissioning theology that the earlier accounts distributed.

- **The "kick against the goads."** Unique to Account 26. The variant has prompted long debate: is it a Lukan literary embellishment for the Greco-Roman audience, or did Paul actually hear these words and report them only here? Bruce treats the variant cautiously—it's possible the proverb belongs to Paul's actual experience and was preserved for the Greco-Roman setting—while Pervo treats it as Lukan-rhetorical localization to the audience.

The chapter's literary structure is a complete Greco-Roman *apologia*: *exordium* (26:2-3), *narratio* of background (26:4-11), *narratio* of the conversion (26:12-18), *propositio* (26:19-23), *peroratio* with appeal to the king (26:25-27). **Witherington III** treats the speech as one of the most rhetorically polished compositions in Luke-Acts, drawing on the conventions of forensic *peroratio* (the appeal that turns the audience to the speaker's side). The structure is also a recognizable conversion-narrative typology: the prophetic-call pattern of Ezekiel 2:1-3 ("son of man, stand on your feet, and I will speak with you") and Isaiah 49:1-6 (the servant called from the womb to be "a light to the nations") are both audibly behind 26:16-18. Paul is presenting his commission as a prophetic call—not a conversion from Judaism, but a vocation within Israel's prophetic tradition.

The chapter ends with a literary signature Luke uses elsewhere (cf. the Lukan trial of Jesus before Pilate at Luke 23:13-15): the authorities, in withdrawing, declare the prisoner innocent. "*Ouden thanatou ē desmōn axion ti prassei ho anthropos houtos*"—"this man is doing nothing deserving death or imprisonment" (26:31). And the king's verdict: "*apolelusthai edynato ho anthropos houtos ei mē epekeklēto Kaisara*"—"this man could have been set free if he had not appealed to Caesar" (26:32). **Marguerat** in *The First Christian Historian* reads this as the climactic Lukan-apologetic conclusion: at every level of Roman jurisprudence and Jewish royal review, Paul is innocent; the only thing keeping him prisoner is his own appeal. Christianity is procedurally vindicated. The transit to Rome that follows in chapter 27 is therefore not a forced exile—it is a self-elected witness.

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

Read against Paul's own letters, Acts 26 contains one of the most thematic resonances in the Pauline corpus. Paul's autobiographical statement at Galatians 1:13-16 ("you have heard of my former life in Judaism, how I persecuted the church of God violently and tried to destroy it... but when he who had set me apart before I was born... was pleased to reveal his Son to me, in order that I might preach him among the Gentiles, I did not consult flesh and blood") is structurally identical to the Acts 26 speech: pre-conversion Pharisaic zeal, persecution of the church, Christophanic encounter, commission to the Gentiles. **N. T. Wright** in *Paul: A Biography* uses the Acts 26 / Galatians 1 parallel as the structural anchor for his entire reconstruction of Paul's self-understanding. **F. F. Bruce** treats the parallel as decisive evidence that the Lukan speech material is rooted in genuine Pauline tradition.

Pamela Eisenbaum (*Paul Was Not a Christian*), Matthew Thiessen (*A Jewish Paul*), and Paula Fredriksen (*Paul: The Pagan's Apostle*) have made this chapter a centerpiece of the "Paul within Judaism" interpretive movement. They note that 26:6-7 frames Paul's hope as *the* Jewish hope: "I stand here on trial because of my hope in the promise made by God to our fathers, to which our twelve tribes hope to attain, as they earnestly worship night and day. And for this hope I am accused by Jews, O king!" Paul does not present himself as a former Jew; he presents himself as a Pharisee continuing in the Pharisaic hope of resurrection (*anastasis nekrōn*, 26:8) which the Pharisaic party itself confessed (Josephus *War* 2.163; *Antiquities* 18.14). Fredriksen reads the chapter as evidence that the historical Paul never thought of himself as having left Judaism; his mission to the Gentiles was the eschatological inclusion of the nations into the people of Israel, exactly as Isaiah 49:6 (cited implicitly at 26:18, 23) had foretold. Adele Reinhartz cautions that Lukan rhetoric here can be read in two directions: the speech can be heard as inner-Jewish discourse, or it can be heard (in the framework of Lukan Christianity) as supersessionist appropriation of Jewish hope. The chapter sustains both readings.

Patristic interpretation focused on **Agrippa's "in a short time"** (26:28). **Chrysostom** in his *Homilies on Acts* (Hom. 52) treated the verse as the most poignant near-conversion in Scripture, and his exegesis—shaped by the early-Byzantine Greek text—does not yield "almost." Chrysostom reads Agrippa's response as ironic deflection: the king laughs off Paul's earnestness with a witticism. Bede follows Chrysostom. The famous "almost thou persuadest me to be a Christian" of the King James Version is a translation choice that **Calvin** in his *Commentary on Acts* anticipates ("*propemodum* ut me Christianum facias"—"nearly to make me a Christian") and that became central to the Wesleyan revival tradition: **Charles Wesley**'s hymn "*Almost Persuaded*" (technically Philip Bliss, 1871, drawing on Wesleyan Methodism) made the verse a fixture of nineteenth-century revival preaching, with Agrippa as the type of the gospel-resistant near-believer. The exegetical-history footprint of this single verse is extensive; **Pervo** notes that the homiletic afterlife of Agrippa's question may be larger than that of the entire chapter combined.

Modern commentary has converged on several key points. **Bruce NICNT** treats the chapter as the literary climax of Acts; the Damascus accounts have been building to this performance, and the inclusion of "kick against the goads" reflects Lukan adaptation to audience without falsifying tradition. **Barrett ICC** focuses on the speech's structural fidelity to Greek forensic convention. **Fitzmyer AB** emphasizes the prophetic-call typology in 26:16-18 (Ezekiel, Isaiah) and reads Paul's mission as cast deliberately as Servant-of-Yahweh fulfillment. **Gaventa Abingdon** highlights the chapter's dramatic-ironic structure: the prisoner is the speaker, the rulers are the audience, and the verdict at the end goes the wrong way (he should be free; he is appealing to a higher court). **Johnson Sacra Pagina** reads the chapter as the apex of Luke's case for Christianity-as-Jewish-fulfillment. **Keener** (Acts vol. 4) has perhaps the most extensive treatment, with detailed comparison of forensic rhetorical conventions and Pauline-letter parallels. **Marguerat** in *The First Christian Historian* and **Tannehill** in *Narrative Unity* both read the chapter as the climactic apologia of Acts.

**E. P. Sanders**'s *Paul and Palestinian Judaism* and *Paul, the Law, and the Jewish People* both engage the Acts 26 speech as evidence for Paul's continuing Pharisaic identity and for the "covenantal nomism" framework that has dominated Pauline studies since 1977. **Krister Stendahl**'s *Paul Among Jews and Gentiles* uses 26:16-18 as the proof-text for his foundational argument that Paul did not undergo a Western-introspective "conversion" from a guilty conscience but a prophetic-call commission to the Gentile mission.

## Lens 4 — The Theological Intent

**Tension 1: Was Paul converted, called, or commissioned?** Three positions. (1) The *conversion* reading (much patristic and Reformation tradition, classical Western piety): Paul on the Damascus road moved from one religion to another; he was a Jew who became a Christian; the moment is paradigmatic for Christian conversion narrative. (2) The *call* reading (Stendahl, Sanders, Eisenbaum, Thiessen, Fredriksen): Paul on the Damascus road did not change religions; he received a prophetic commission from the God of Israel to bring the gospel of Israel's Messiah to the Gentiles; the encounter is structurally a prophetic call (Isa 49, Jer 1, Ezek 2) and not a conversion in the post-Augustinian sense. (3) The *eschatological-realignment* reading (Wright, Bruce, much contemporary balanced commentary): Paul's encounter is both more and less than a conversion—it is the recognition that Israel's hopes have been inaugurated in Jesus the Messiah and that Paul is now commissioned to proclaim this inauguration; the language of "conversion" is not wrong but is too thin; what Paul experiences is a world-historical reorientation in which he discovers that the resurrection has begun.

Steelman: the call reading does the most justice to 26:6-8, 16-18 (the prophetic-call typology), but the eschatological-realignment reading better captures the totality of Pauline self-understanding across the letters; Paul does describe himself as "set apart from my mother's womb" (Gal 1:15, echoing Jer 1:5 and Isa 49:1), but he also describes himself as having "suffered the loss of all things" (Phil 3:8), language closer to conversion.

**Where this leaves us:** the theological vocabulary we use to describe Paul's encounter shapes how we hear the gospel. If "conversion" suggests leaving a false religion for a true one, the word distorts Paul's self-understanding. If "call" suggests merely a vocational reassignment within stable religious identity, the word minimizes the rupture. The most faithful reading is that something new has begun in the world—the resurrection of Israel's Messiah—and Paul's life has been reoriented around announcing that newness to the nations.

**Tension 2: What does Agrippa's "in a short time" (26:28) tell us about gospel-resistance?** Three positions. (1) The *almost-persuaded* reading (Wesleyan-revival tradition, much evangelical homiletics): Agrippa is on the edge of conversion, restrained only by political loyalty and royal pride; the verse is paradigmatic of those who hear the gospel clearly, are moved by it, and finally turn away. (2) The *ironic-deflection* reading (Chrysostom, Bruce, Witherington): Agrippa's response is sophisticated court humor, using the rare term "Christian" to deflect Paul's earnest evangelism without committing to either belief or hostility; the verse is not about near-conversion but about the urbane refusal of the gospel by those whose status is too entangled with the world. (3) The *Lukan-narrative-counterpoint* reading (Pervo, Marguerat): the king's response is set up by Luke as ironic counterpoint to Paul's "boldly" (26:26); Agrippa's flippancy throws Paul's earnestness into sharper relief; the verse is a literary device, not a window into Agrippa's interior.

Steelman: the ironic-deflection reading best fits the tone of the entire scene—Agrippa is presented throughout as polished, cosmopolitan, and politically careful; he later finds it convenient to declare Paul innocent (26:32) without committing personally to anything Paul has said. The "almost" of Wesleyan tradition is exegetically strained but homiletically powerful, and its long afterlife in Christian preaching is itself a piece of theological data.

**Where this leaves us:** the chapter shows how the gospel can be heard with full comprehension and politely declined. Paul has spoken "*alētheias kai sōphrosynēs rhēmata*"—"words of truth and rationality" (26:25)—and the king's response is literary deflection. This is itself a sober theological observation: the gospel is not finally a matter of insufficient evidence or unclear presentation. It is a matter of allegiance.

**One unresolved question:** Did Agrippa II ever revisit the encounter? He lived another forty years after this hearing, eventually withdrawing to Rome. There is no evidence in any extant source—Josephus, Tacitus, the early Christian apocrypha—that he ever encountered the Christian movement again or reconsidered his response. The text leaves him at the threshold.

## The Thread

The Damascus-road material here links back to Acts 9:1-19 and 22:1-21 (the two earlier accounts) and forward to Galatians 1:13-16 and Philippians 3:4-11 (Paul's own retrospective accounts). The prophetic-call typology of 26:16-18 picks up Isaiah 49:1-6 ("I will give you as a light for the nations, that my salvation may reach to the end of the earth") and Ezekiel 2:1-3 ("son of man, stand on your feet"); flag also Jeremiah 1:4-10 as background. The Agrippa verdict at 26:32 is the climactic Roman-Jewish-royal declaration of innocence that closes the trilogy begun at 24:1; cross-reference Luke 23:13-15 (Pilate-Herod parallel innocence verdict on Jesus). The "not done in a corner" claim at 26:26 will resonate forward to 1 Corinthians 15:3-8, where Paul lists the public witnesses to the resurrection.
