# Joshua 15: Judah's Long List and the Jebusite Exception

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

Joshua 15 is the longest single tribal allotment in the book, and this length is not incidental. Judah receives the most detailed territorial description because Judah is, by the time the text reaches its final form, the surviving southern kingdom whose scribes and priestly bureaucracy shaped the Deuteronomistic History. The chapter's geography extends from the Wilderness of Zin in the south — the arid Negev basin near Kadesh-barnea — to the Mediterranean coast in the west, to the northern shoulder of Jerusalem, to the Dead Sea in the east. This is roughly 9,000 square kilometers of highland, lowland, and desert, the largest tribal territory by area.

The political situation behind the text is layered. The narrative purports to describe the immediate post-conquest allotment (c. 1406 BCE high chronology, c. 1200 low chronology). But the city lists in 15:20–62 almost certainly reflect a later administrative reality. Albrecht Alt's 1925 essay "Judas Gaue unter Josia" ("Judah's Districts under Josiah") and Frank Moore Cross and G. Ernest Wright's 1956 JBL article "The Boundary and Province Lists of the Kingdom of Judah" argued persuasively that the eleven-district structure (southern Negev, four Shephelah districts, Philistine coast, three hill-country districts, Benjaminite enclave, wilderness district) reflects Josianic administration in the late seventh century BCE. Zecharia Kallai's *Historical Geography of the Bible* (1986) refined the thesis: the lists may contain older Judahite material from Hezekiah's administration or earlier, with Josianic editing. Yohanan Aharoni (*The Land of the Bible*, 1979) and Anson Rainey (*The Sacred Bridge*, 2006) have mapped the sites with varying degrees of confidence — some (Lachish, Beth-shemesh, Debir, Engedi) are well-identified; others remain uncertain.

Site identifications. Hebron is Tell er-Rumeideh (the tell underneath modern Hebron's old city). Debir / Kiriath-sepher has a contested identification: William Foxwell Albright excavated Tell Beit Mirsim (1926–1932) and proposed it as Debir, finding destruction layers at the Late Bronze–Iron transition; Moshe Kochavi's 1970s survey argued for Tell Rabud, ten kilometers south of Hebron, as a better match for the biblical location. Most current scholarship favors Kochavi's Tell Rabud. Lachish is Tell ed-Duweir, excavated by James Starkey in the 1930s and later by David Ussishkin; its Late Bronze destruction (level VII) is famously debated as evidence for Joshua 10 or for Egyptian Nineteenth Dynasty campaigns. Engedi is Tell el-Jurn at the oasis on the Dead Sea's western shore. Kiriath-jearim is Deir el-ʿAzar / Abu Ghosh.

Key Hebrew terms. First, *gôrāl* (גּוֹרָל), "allotment" (15:1), the mechanism that formally randomizes distribution and thereby sacralizes it. Second, *gəbûl* (גְּבוּל), "boundary" (15:1–12), used dozens of times in this chapter — the chapter is essentially a *gəbûl* document, a boundary-description text with close analogues in Neo-Babylonian *kudurrû* boundary stones and Hittite land grants. Third, *ʾaḥuzzâ* (אֲחֻזָּה), "holding/possession" — though the chapter prefers *naḥălâ* and *ḥelqâ* (portion). Fourth, *šəpēlâ* (שְׁפֵלָה), "lowland" (15:33), the technical term for the western foothills between the coastal plain and the Judean highlands — still the modern Hebrew geographical term. Fifth, *midbār* (מִדְבָּר), "wilderness" (15:61), here the Judean desert east of Hebron. Sixth, *bərākâ* (בְּרָכָה), "blessing" (15:19) — Achsah's request for a "gift" that the text dignifies with the weighty covenant-term rather than a mere boon.

Archaeological context for 15:63 is particularly interesting: "the Jebusites the people of Judah could not drive out." Kathleen Kenyon's 1961–1967 excavations in the City of David and Yigal Shiloh's 1978–1985 work confirmed continuous occupation through the Late Bronze and Iron I periods, with no destruction layer matching a Judahite conquest before David's capture (2 Sam 5:6–9). The text's admission of failure aligns with the archaeological record. Judges 1:21 attributes the same failure to Benjamin — an internal textual variant that scholars have long discussed (see below).

## Lens 2 — The Literature

Genre: Joshua 15 is a composite boundary-and-city-list document with an embedded narrative. Four literary movements: boundary description (15:1–12), Caleb-Achsah-Othniel narrative (15:13–19), city lists organized by district (15:20–62), and the Jerusalem-exception coda (15:63).

The boundary description (15:1–12) uses formulaic verbs of motion: the boundary "goes out" (*yāṣāʾ*), "passes along" (*ʿāvar*), "goes up" (*ʿālâ*), "turns" (*nāsav*), "ends" (*tôṣəʾôt*). This is the standard *gəbûl*-document vocabulary that Noth and later Nadav Naʾaman have analyzed. Such descriptions resemble Egyptian New Kingdom boundary texts (the Karnak topographical lists) and later Neo-Assyrian province boundaries; the closest literary parallels are found in the Samaria Ostraca and in the Mesad Hashavyahu ostracon (late seventh century BCE).

The city lists (15:20–62) are organized by district with totals at the end of each group. These totals sometimes disagree with the cities actually named — Judah's Shephelah list A (15:33–36) totals "fourteen" but names fifteen (Gederah and Gederothaim may be a dittography). These disagreements have been treated as scribal transmission evidence (see Emanuel Tov, *Textual Criticism of the Hebrew Bible*, 2012). The LXX preserves different totals and sometimes different city orderings, consistent with the general observation that Joshua's Greek version is four to five percent shorter than MT.

The Caleb-Achsah-Othniel narrative (15:13–19) is a literary jewel set in the middle of the administrative prose. It doubles a tradition also found in Judges 1:11–15, where it appears almost verbatim. Scholars (Trent Butler, *Joshua*, WBC; Richard Nelson, *Joshua*, OTL) typically regard the Judges version as primary and the Joshua version as a deliberate literary doublet, inserted here to bind the Caleb tradition of chapter 14 to Judah's broader inheritance. The narrative's most striking feature is Achsah's speech: she is one of the few named women in the book of Joshua, and her request — "Give me a blessing. Since you have given me the land of the Negev, give me also springs of water" — is a direct speech act in a text where women rarely speak. Talmud Temurah 16a takes this moment as halakhic evidence that daughters can inherit, and it reads Achsah's active claim as a model. The *bərākâ* (blessing) she asks for is economic-ecological: arable land without water is not arable.

The Jerusalem exception (15:63) is the chapter's narrative punctuation. After pages of confident allotment, the text admits failure: "the Jebusites the people of Judah could not drive out." The phrase "to this day" (*ʿad ha-yôm ha-zeh*) signals a Deuteronomistic redactor writing from a later vantage point — studied by Brevard Childs in his 1963 JBL article "A Study of the Formula 'Until This Day'" as a formula that anchors traditions in a specific monarchic present. The statement stands in tension with Judges 1:8's claim that Judah *did* take Jerusalem, and with Judges 1:21's attribution of the same failure to Benjamin. These tensions indicate multiple traditions in circulation; the redactor has preserved rather than harmonized them.

The chapter's placement in the book's argument: after the Caleb hinge of chapter 14, chapter 15 establishes Judah's centrality. It is the first and longest allotment of the Cisjordan distribution, structurally privileged over the other tribes. The redactor is making a geographic-theological argument: Judah's territory is the covenant's heartland.

Textual variants of meaning-affecting consequence: LXX omits 15:59a's Benjaminite enclave cities (which MT includes); many modern translations restore from LXX since the total count "six cities" in MT 15:59b-60 matches the LXX figure. 4QJosh^a does not preserve chapter 15 substantially. The Samaritan Pentateuch, which ends with Deuteronomy, has no Joshua tradition proper.

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

**Jewish trajectory.** Second Temple literature treats Judah's territory as the proto-Judean homeland; the book of 1 Maccabees uses Judah's borders as the geographic frame for Hasmonean ambition. Josephus (*Antiquities* 5.1.22) reproduces the allotment with his own geographic glosses. The rabbinic tradition engages Joshua 15 through two main channels. First, Achsah's request: Talmud Temurah 16a and Bava Batra 119a cite it in discussions of female inheritance and of the halakhic principle that requests to authorities should be framed with humility and specificity. Second, the Jerusalem exception: the rabbis note the tension between Joshua 15:63 and Judges 1:8, typically resolving it with a distinction between "city" and "citadel" — Judah took the lower city but not the Zion fortress, which fell only under David.

Medieval Jewish commentators. Rashi (d. 1105) on Joshua 15:8 treats the Jebusite as living in the "southern shoulder" of the hill, leaving the rest of the ridge accessible to Judahite settlement. Ibn Ezra (d. 1167) focuses on the geography itself, identifying sites with Second Temple and Talmudic-era locations known to him. Ramban (Nachmanides, d. 1270), writing from the Land of Israel in the last years of his life, shows personal investment in the topography — his commentary emphasizes that the *naḥălâ* of Judah includes the patriarchal burial-city of Hebron. Radak (David Kimchi, d. 1235) attends carefully to the Achsah narrative, praising her as a model of righteous initiative. Abarbanel (d. 1508) reads the chapter politically: the length and detail of Judah's list prefigures Judah's messianic priority (Gen 49:10). Maimonides touches Joshua 15 only obliquely; his *Hilkhot Shemittah ve-Yovel* relies on the boundary tradition for halakhic land-law.

Modern Jewish scholarship. Moshe Weinfeld (*The Promise of the Land*, 1993) treats the city lists as administrative-period material. Nahum Sarna emphasizes the theological weight of the *gôrāl*. Jon Levenson reads the Achsah pericope as an inheritance-expanding narrative of female agency. Avivah Zornberg's method illuminates the Achsah moment — her "dismount from the donkey" becomes a gesture of negotiated authority. Baruch Halpern takes the lists as historically anchored, if late-monarchic in final form. Rachel Havrelock (*River Jordan*, 2011) reads chapter 15's cartography as an ideological production of Judah's centrality. Moshe Weinfeld and Zecharia Kallai both engage the Alt-Cross district thesis, generally affirming a Josianic context for the final list. James Kugel surveys the aggadic reception. Moshe Garsiel's onomastic work on the Judahite city names is technical but rewarding.

**Christian trajectory.** Origen's *Homilies on Joshua* treats the tribal allotments allegorically as the distribution of virtues to the soul; Hebron, Debir, and the Negev cities are stages of spiritual attainment. In Homily 20 he treats Achsah's request for springs as the soul's request for the living waters of the Spirit beyond mere dry land — the dry land being the law, the springs being the gospel. Jerome (d. 420), based in Bethlehem, wrote the *Onomasticon* (translating and expanding Eusebius) which identifies many Joshua 15 sites with contemporary Byzantine-era Palestinian toponyms. Eusebius's *Onomasticon* (c. 320 CE) remains a crucial fourth-century witness to Late Roman Palestinian geography. Augustine references Judah's portion in *City of God* 17 as part of typological readings linking Judah to Christ.

Medieval Western readers: Thomas Aquinas uses Joshua 15's allotment structure in *Summa* I-II.105 on divine law's just distribution. The *Glossa Ordinaria* preserves Origen's allegorical layer for the city lists. The Reformation brought a decisive shift: Calvin's *Commentary on Joshua* (1563) treats the boundary description as sober historical-geographical record, warning against over-allegorization; the Achsah pericope he treats as an exemplar of lawful petition within family structure. Luther's remarks on Joshua are scattered; he reads the "could not drive out" texts as evidence of human weakness even amid divine gift. Menno Simons does not comment substantially on the chapter.

Critical scholarship. Martin Noth (*Das Buch Josua*, 1938/1953) treated chapter 15 as a composite, with the boundary description and the city list as originally separate documents from different periods. Albrecht Alt's Josianic-districts thesis (1925) reshaped the field; Frank Moore Cross and G. Ernest Wright (1956) extended it. Richard Nelson (*Joshua*, OTL, 1997) provides the current standard treatment: chapter 15's boundary text is older (possibly ninth-century) and the city list is Josianic. Thomas Dozeman, Trent Butler (*Joshua*, WBC), and Kallai (*Historical Geography*) all engage the dating question. Volkmar Fritz, Nadav Naʾaman, and Israel Finkelstein have continued the archaeological work; Finkelstein in particular argues for an even later (post-exilic) date for some of the city lists, against Kallai's Hezekian-Josianic dating. Douglas Knight and other form-critics have parsed the *gəbûl*-formulas.

Contemporary voices. Walter Brueggemann (*The Land*, 1977) reads the city lists not as dry bureaucracy but as theology: God's gift is concrete and itemized. Christopher Wright treats the Achsah pericope as an inheritance-ethic model. Gordon McConville and Stephen Williams's *Joshua* (Two Horizons) attends to the theological reception. Richard Hess (*Joshua*, TOTC, 1996) defends the early substratum of the geographic material, against the more skeptical critical consensus. David Howard (*Joshua*, NAC, 1998) and Trent Butler approach the lists as theological maps. Ellen Davis's agrarian theology illuminates the Shephelah–hill country–wilderness triad as three modes of human relationship to land. L. Daniel Hawk's ideology-critical reading foregrounds what the list silences: the prior inhabitants whose cities Israel inherits. Lori Rowlett's *Joshua and the Rhetoric of Violence* attends to how the boundary document legitimizes dispossession. Postcolonial critique — Robert Allen Warrior's 1989 "Canaanites, Cowboys, and Indians," extended by Musa Dube and Kwok Pui-lan — reads chapter 15 as a textual technology of displacement, making the Canaanite inhabitants geographically anonymous precisely where the Israelite tribes become meticulously named. Havrelock's *River Jordan* sits at the intersection of Jewish and critical scholarship on the chapter's geographic ideology.

## Lens 4 — The Theological Intent

**Tension 1: The sacred lot versus administrative reality.** The chapter frames allotment as *gôrāl* — sovereign, providential, sacralized. The content of the allotment, however, looks very much like a monarchic-period administrative document. Three positions.

*Position A: Both-and / canonical integration.* The lot is the theological frame; the administrative lists are its concrete historical working-out. God's sovereign allotment is not undone by the fact that it took centuries of political development to reach its form in the text. This is the canonical-theological line (Childs, Brueggemann).

*Position B: Critical-historical primacy.* The *gôrāl* framing is redactional overlay on what is fundamentally a Josianic administrative document preserving the late Judahite kingdom's actual provinces. The theology is retrojected; the lists are primary. (Alt, Cross, Nelson, Finkelstein.)

*Position C* (steelman of the least intuitive): *Theological suspicion.* The *gôrāl* language is not just redactional; it is ideologically functional. By sacralizing what was, at its historical root, a royal bureaucratic map, the text immunizes the monarchic administrative reality from criticism. Subjects of Josiah's taxing districts could not complain — the districts were not an administrative choice but a divine assignment dating back to Joshua. Rowlett and Hawk's ideology-critical reading sits here: the sacred lot is a technology of legitimation.

*Where this leaves us:* the chapter is theologically coherent only if one can hold together the claim of divine gift with the recognition that the specific form of that gift bears the fingerprints of the state apparatus that preserved and edited it.

**Tension 2: Inheritance as completion versus inheritance with holes.** The chapter's long confident lists end with a confession: Jerusalem remained Jebusite. Three positions.

*Position A: Conditional-covenantal.* The "could not drive out" notices (15:63 here; 16:10, 17:12–13 coming) are the book's honest self-correction against its own total-conquest rhetoric of chapters 10–12. The promise is real, but its full possession is conditional and ongoing. (Brueggemann, Goldingay, Wright.)

*Position B: Deferral-to-David.* The Jebusite notice is not a failure but a narrative reservation: Jerusalem is being saved for David (2 Sam 5:6–9). The redactor is managing the timeline of covenant fulfillment. (Nelson, some canonical readers.)

*Position C* (steelman of the least intuitive): *Structural failure.* The "could not drive out" admissions are the book's decisive internal testimony against the theology of total conquest. The text is telling us, against its own louder claims, that Israel never fully possessed the land even in the generation that is supposed to be the covenant's high point. The admitted failure is not a postponement but a permanent condition. The honest reader should take this testimony more seriously than the conquest-completion claims of chapters 10–12. Hawk and some critical voices argue for this reading — it requires letting the chapter's quieter verses override its louder ones.

*Where this leaves us:* Joshua 15 tells two stories at once — completed inheritance and admitted failure — and it will not let the reader pick only one.

One unresolved question: if the city lists of Joshua 15 are indeed a Josianic administrative document, does the Josianic reform movement (c. 622 BCE) read the book of Joshua, or does the book of Joshua read the Josianic reform back into Mosaic time?

## The Thread

- **Numbers 34:1–12** — the Mosaic boundary specification that Joshua 15 is formally executing; note how Judah's southern line in 15:1–4 tracks Num 34:3–5.
- **Judges 1:11–15** — the Achsah-Othniel doublet, almost verbatim; the older tradition preserved in both books.
- **2 Samuel 5:6–9** — David's eventual capture of Jerusalem, completing what Joshua 15:63 admits remained undone.
- **Genesis 49:8–12** — Jacob's blessing on Judah; the geographic expansion of chapter 15 is the material substance of the patriarchal promise.
