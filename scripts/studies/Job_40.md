# Job 40: The First of the Works of God

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

Chapter 40 marks a structural hinge. The first divine speech concludes (40:1–2), Job offers his first response (40:3–5), and the second divine speech begins (40:6–24), launching toward the great Leviathan poem of chapter 41. The chapter introduces Behemoth (בְּהֵמוֹת, *bəhēmôṯ*), an entity whose identity has divided commentators for two thousand years: massive herbivore of the Jordan marshes, mythological chaos creature, or some deliberate blending of both?

The setting remains the whirlwind (*seʿārāh*, 40:6), picking up the meteorological theophany of 38:1. The geographic horizon has narrowed. Where chapter 38 ranged through the foundations of the earth, the storehouses of snow, and the constellations, and chapter 39 surveyed the wild zoology of steppe, mountain, and desert, chapter 40's second panel settles at a single water's edge: lotus plants, marsh, reeds, willows, and the Jordan. This is the specific habitat of the hippopotamus (*Hippopotamus amphibius*), which ranged along the Nile and as far north as the Orontes in the first millennium BCE before being hunted to local extinction.

The Egyptian context is pivotal. Hippopotamus hunting was a royal motif from the Old Kingdom onwards, associated iconographically with the pharaoh's triumph over the god Seth, who could appear in hippopotamus form. The "Pyramid Texts" and the *Book of the Dead* contain incantations against hippopotamus-shaped chaos creatures. Scenes of hippopotamus hunts appear in the mastabas of Saqqara (Ti, Mereruka, Fifth–Sixth Dynasties) and in the great temple of Horus at Edfu, where the king harpoons Seth-as-hippopotamus in a ritualized pageant. Job 40:24's "Can one take him by his eyes, or pierce his nose with a snare?" has direct resonance with this Egyptian practice: the hippopotamus was taken by ropes thrown over the muzzle and harpoons driven through the mouth or nose.

But Behemoth is not *only* a hippopotamus. The name itself is grammatically the intensive plural of בְּהֵמָה (*bəhēmāh*), "beast" — literally "beastly beast" or "quintessence of beast," a plural of majesty like *ʾĕlōhîm* for God. In the Ugaritic Baal Cycle (KTU 1.5), Baal's cosmic adversary Mot (Death) is accompanied by a figure called *ʿigl ʾil ʿtk*, "the bullock of El," who is sometimes equated with the monstrous Behemoth tradition. The Egyptian chaos creature Apophis (the great serpent) and the Sethian hippopotamus both belong to the same chaoskampf imagination that the Hebrew poet is drawing from.

Textually, the LXX of Job 40 is somewhat shorter than the MT — the ancient Greek simply calls Behemoth *thēria*, "wild animals," rather than transliterating the name, suggesting that the translator did not recognize a technical mythological term. The Vulgate follows MT. 11QTargum Job preserves fragments of chapter 40 in Aramaic. The identification of Behemoth as specifically the hippopotamus appears first in Bochart's *Hierozoicon* (1663) and became the dominant modern reading; older Jewish tradition, especially the apocalyptic literature (1 Enoch 60:7–10, 4 Ezra 6:49–52, 2 Baruch 29:4), reads Behemoth as a primordial land-creature paired with Leviathan as a sea-creature, both reserved for the eschatological banquet.

Five Hebrew terms anchor the chapter. First, בְּהֵמוֹת (*bəhēmôṯ*, 40:15), the intensive plural beast-name. Second, רֵאשִׁית דַּרְכֵי־אֵל (*rēʾšîṯ darḵê-ʾēl*, 40:19), "the first of God's ways" — a phrase echoing Proverbs 8:22 where wisdom is "the first of God's ways." Behemoth is given cosmogonic priority, an astonishing status. Third, the hapax-like אַמִּיץ (*ʾammîṣ*) in 40:16, strength in the "muscles of the belly" — the anatomical realism is striking. Fourth, צֶדֶק (*ṣeḏeq*, 40:8, translated "in the right"), the legal-justice vocabulary that frames God's challenge. Fifth, the challenge formula הֲתָפֵר מִשְׁפָּטִי (*hătāpēr mišpāṭî*), "Will you annul my judgment?" (40:8) — the juridical language that makes explicit what has been implicit since chapter 38: a courtroom scene is being staged, but with Job as defendant.

## Lens 2 — The Literature

The chapter is a dramatic turning point. Habel identifies the structure precisely: speech one closes (40:1–2), Job responds in a minimalist surrender (40:3–5), speech two opens with a renewed summons (40:6–7) and a legal accusation (40:8–14), and then Behemoth is introduced (40:15–24) as the pivot to the Leviathan poem that will fill chapter 41. Newsom (NIB, *Contest of Moral Imaginations*) calls this "the second inning" — Job has not quite capitulated, and God pushes further.

Job's first response (40:3–5) is a masterpiece of compressed ambiguity. "Behold, I am of small account" (*hēn qallōṯî*) — literally, "I am light," "I weigh little." The image is of being put on scales and found insufficient. "I lay my hand on my mouth" is a gesture of deferential silence found also in Judges 18:19 and Proverbs 30:32. "I have spoken once, and I will not answer; twice, but I will proceed no further" — the numerical pattern (once–twice) is a standard wisdom-tradition escalation. But has Job *repented*? Newsom and Clines both stress that the response does not admit error. Job claims insignificance and elects silence; he does not concede that his case against God was wrong. This is what makes God's resumption of the speech in 40:6 necessary: Job has not given God what God is (apparently) after.

The second speech opens with the same summons as the first: "Dress for action like a man" (40:7 = 38:3). This is rhetorical reset. Then 40:8 raises the accusation to a new pitch: "Will you condemn me that you may be in the right?" (*hătaršîʿēnî ləmaʿan tiṣdāq*). Gordis notes that this is the sharpest statement of the book's juridical logic: Job's protest of innocence has structurally required that God be in the wrong, and God is here making that structural implication explicit. 40:9–14 then mocks Job with an invitation: if Job thinks he can do better, let him take on the universal moral-administrative task — humbling the proud, crushing the wicked, hiding them in the dust. If Job could do this, God would "acknowledge" (*ʾôdekkā*, 40:14) him. The implicit point: Job's demand for retributive justice would require omnipotent cosmic management, and Job cannot even begin this task.

Then, abruptly, 40:15: "Behold, Behemoth." The move is jarring. We are in a courtroom, and suddenly we are looking at a giant herbivore. The shift is deliberate. Alter reads Behemoth as the *exemplary* case of something Job cannot control — a creature of divine making so vast and so peaceful-but-unassailable that human moral administration is revealed as category-mistake. Behemoth grazes like an ox, lies under the lotus plants, is unmoved when the Jordan rushes at his mouth, cannot be taken with hook or snare. Fyall (*Now My Eyes Have Seen You*) argues Behemoth is both naturalistic *and* symbolic: the hippopotamus is a real creature, but its theological function is as an icon of the powers that resist human mastery — ultimately, in Fyall's reading, an icon of the death-powers ranged against God. The verse "He is the first of the works of God" (40:19) is read by Fyall and Pope as claiming for Behemoth a cosmogonic status analogous to Wisdom in Proverbs 8:22, implying something far larger than zoology.

Textually, LXX of 40:15–24 is abbreviated. The most disputed verse is 40:19b: "let him who made him bring near his sword" (MT *hāʿōśô yaggēš ḥarbô*). Some read this as "only his Maker can bring a sword against him," i.e. only God can defeat Behemoth. Others emend to get "he was made for his fellows to dominate." The ambiguity is theologically loaded: if only God can slay Behemoth, then Behemoth remains a permanent presence, a creature whose defeat is held in reserve; if Behemoth was made to be slain, the reading is different.

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

Canonically, Job 40 sits at the structural center of the book's climactic gesture: God has refused to answer Job, is now pressing the case against retributive theology, and is about to introduce Leviathan. In Jewish tradition, Behemoth quickly became a figure of eschatological speculation. The apocalyptic trajectory from 1 Enoch 60:7–10 through 4 Ezra 6:49–52, 2 Baruch 29:4, and the Talmud (Bava Batra 74b–75a) developed the tradition that Behemoth (the land monster) and Leviathan (the sea monster) were created on the fifth day and reserved, alive, for the messianic banquet, where the righteous would feast upon them. Rashi and the medieval Jewish tradition preserved this reading without apology: the messianic era would see the consumption of the great monsters, inverting their current unassailability. Maimonides, characteristically, demythologized: Behemoth is a lesson in human cognitive limits (*Guide* III.22–23), one more creature whose being Job cannot grasp. Nachmanides read Behemoth more mystically, as a name for the forces of material existence at their most primordial.

The chaoskampf reading, recovered by Gunkel and developed decisively by Levenson in *Creation and the Persistence of Evil*, reshapes the whole interpretive frame. Levenson's thesis: the creation accounts of Israel, including Job 38–41, do not present a finished, peaceful, once-and-for-all cosmos but a *partially ordered* world in which God has established order through the restraint — not the annihilation — of chaos. Behemoth and Leviathan are not mere animals and not defeated demons. They are creatures whose continued existence, under divine control but not divine elimination, testifies to a cosmos in which evil and chaos persist as real possibilities. Levenson's reading is decisive for understanding the specific move of chapter 40: God's point is not that Behemoth is cute or tame, but that Behemoth is terrifying, primordial, and *only God's to handle*. "He is the first of the works of God; let him who made him bring near his sword" reads on this interpretation as: God alone can confront this creature, but God is not doing so now. The world Job lives in is a world where Behemoth exists, uncontested by humans and held in dynamic restraint by God.

In Christian tradition, Gregory the Great's *Moralia in Job* reads Behemoth allegorically as the devil — a reading already present in the earliest Christian commentators and adopted by Origen, Chrysostom, and Augustine in various forms. The allegorical identification is attractive because of 40:19's "first of the works of God" (readable as Satan's status as the first fallen angel) and 40:24 ("can one take him by his eyes"), read as the difficulty of unmasking the devil. Aquinas softens the allegory — he thinks Behemoth refers primarily to an actual creature whose greatness testifies to God's craft, with the devil as secondary referent. Calvin's sermons on chapter 40 emphasize the humiliation of Job's pretension rather than the identity of Behemoth; for Calvin, the point is that Job cannot even master a large land animal, much less administer the cosmos.

Modern criticism has split. Clines (WBC) prefers a hippopotamus reading with mythological overtones. Habel and Hartley take Behemoth as a "supernatural monster" rooted in naturalistic observation. Pope (Anchor) leans decisively toward the mythological. Newsom takes the view that the ambiguity is the point: Behemoth is deliberately poised between the naturalistic and the mythological, between a creature one might almost imagine seeing and a creature that exceeds every category. Gutiérrez (*On Job*) reads Behemoth and the following Leviathan as the creatures that stand against the oppressive powers in Latin American experience, with God's speech declaring God alone can finally contest them — a political theology reading.

The crucial convergence: across traditions, Behemoth is consistently read as a creature whose power exceeds any merely human capacity to dominate, symbolizing in some way the limits of human control over the forces of creation. The crucial divergence: Christian tradition, especially through Gregory, has often read Behemoth christologically — as the devil whom Christ has already defeated, rendering the threat proleptically nullified. Jewish tradition (especially Levenson, Kugel) and much modern criticism resist this move, preserving Behemoth as a figure of *ongoing* chaos whom God restrains but does not eliminate. The difference matters: on the Christological reading, the world has already been substantially secured against the Behemoth-powers; on the Jewish/critical reading, the world remains exposed to them, sustained moment-by-moment by divine restraint.

## Lens 4 — The Theological Intent

**Tension 1: Is Behemoth a creature, a monster, or a mythological symbol?**

- *Zoological reading* (Bochart, much of the modern evangelical tradition, Andersen): Behemoth is the hippopotamus, a real animal described with some poetic exaggeration. The point is that God made such creatures, and Job cannot master them.
- *Mythological reading* (Pope, Fyall, Levenson): Behemoth is a chaos creature, the Hebrew counterpart to Egyptian Sethian hippopotamus and Ugaritic chaos bulls. The point is cosmological: the cosmos contains primordial adversaries whom only God can handle.
- *Deliberately ambiguous reading* (Newsom, Clines, Habel): Behemoth is both. The poem uses naturalistic realism to make a theological point that exceeds zoology. The ambiguity is productive: a hippopotamus is sublime enough to stand in for something larger.
- *Steelmanned least-intuitive reading* (the Christological-allegorical reading of Gregory, retrieved in Fyall): Behemoth is a figure of Satan or the death-powers ranged against God. This reading is hardest for modern sensibilities because it seems to do violence to the text's zoological specificity. But it takes seriously that 40:19's "first of the works of God" and 40:24's ungovernability exceed the hippopotamus's actual biology. On this reading, the chapter is revealing the spiritual architecture of the cosmos: there are powers opposed to God's purposes that only God can finally subdue, and this is what Job is being shown.

**Tension 2: Is God's challenge to Job in 40:8–14 serious or ironic?**

- *Straightforward rebuke reading* (Calvin, Aquinas, much of the tradition): God is genuinely accusing Job of having claimed the right to condemn God in order to justify himself. The challenge to take over cosmic moral management is straightforward mockery designed to produce humility.
- *Legal-rhetorical reading* (Habel, Clines): God is engaging Job's own legal framing — Job demanded a lawsuit, and God is pressing the logical consequences of Job's demand. If Job's case requires God's being unjust, then Job must show himself capable of the moral administration he implicitly claims God has botched.
- *Concession-by-contrast reading* (Newsom): By showing Job what moral administration of the cosmos would actually require — arms like God, voice like thunder, the capacity to abase every proud person and bind them in the world below — God is conceding that the moral-retributive theology Job assumed *would* require such capacities. The theological problem has been that Job and his friends all thought this was roughly how God operated. The speech reveals that no one so operates.
- *Steelmanned least-intuitive reading* (Gutiérrez, the liberationist reading): God's challenge is not mocking Job but acknowledging a legitimate moral outrage that nevertheless exceeds any human's capacity to execute. Job is not wrong to want the proud brought low and the wicked trodden down; he is wrong only in expecting that this is currently happening on a schedule calibrated to human observation. God's speech in 40:11–13 sounds less like sarcasm and more like an admission that yes, such administration is needed, but no human can perform it. Read this way, the passage is not anti-Joban but pro-Joban in its affirmation that Job's grievance is real.

*Where this leaves us:* Chapter 40 pivots from cosmological panorama to focused confrontation, and it introduces the first of two creatures who embody the limit of human mastery. Job's first response is not yet repentance; it is a strategic silence that God refuses to accept as the end. The deeper question the chapter raises — whether moral administration of the cosmos is what divine governance is, or whether it is a different kind of thing altogether — is posed but not answered.

The unresolved question: If Behemoth is "the first of the works of God," what does it mean that primordial resistance to human mastery is given cosmogonic priority? Is God introducing Job not just to the limits of human power but to the *positive status* of what resists human management?

## The Thread

**Proverbs 8:22** reads "The LORD possessed me at the beginning of his way, the first of his works of old" — Wisdom personified as *rēʾšîṯ darḵô*, precisely the phrase applied to Behemoth in Job 40:19 (*rēʾšîṯ darḵê-ʾēl*). The parallel is startling. Wisdom and Behemoth are both called "the first of God's ways." The implication is not that Behemoth is Wisdom's equal but that the cosmos contains, at its most primordial level, two things: Wisdom, the delight in which God creates, and Behemoth, the massive creaturely bulk that humans cannot master. Both are God's firstborn.

**Isaiah 30:7** calls Egypt "Rahab who sits still" (*rahaḇ hēm šāḇeṯ*), using a chaos-monster name (Rahab, related to the dragon-of-the-sea tradition) as a diminishing political epithet. The move in Isaiah suggests that chaos-monster language could be applied to historical powers — Egypt, whose hippopotamus-hunting iconography may itself be the background of Behemoth. Reading Job 40 against Isaiah 30:7 suggests that Behemoth could be read geopolitically as well as cosmologically.

**4 Ezra 6:49–52** (late first century CE) and **2 Baruch 29:4** preserve the Jewish apocalyptic tradition that Behemoth and Leviathan were created on the fifth day, separated (Behemoth to the dry land east of Eden, Leviathan to the sea), and reserved for the eschatological banquet. This tradition provides the crucial bridge between the Joban texts and Revelation 19:17–18 (the "great supper of God"), which some commentators read as an inverted-but-related image of a final eating of the chaos creatures. The canonical trajectory is that the creatures God restrains in Job 40 are those God will finally overcome at the end.

**Isaiah 27:1** ("In that day the LORD with his hard and great and strong sword will punish Leviathan the fleeing serpent, Leviathan the twisting serpent, and he will slay the dragon that is in the sea") reads the decisive eschatological defeat of Leviathan that Job 40 and 41 conspicuously withhold. Reading Job against Isaiah 27 shows the canonical trajectory: in Job, the creatures are displayed but not defeated; in Isaiah, the defeat is prophesied but deferred; in Revelation, the defeat is enacted. The Behemoth of Job 40 is the still-present creature whose final subduing belongs to the *eschaton*.
