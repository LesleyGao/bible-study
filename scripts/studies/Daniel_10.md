# Daniel 10: The Man in Linen and the War Behind the World

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

The chapter opens with the precise dating formula characteristic of the Hebrew chapters of Daniel: "In the third year of Cyrus king of Persia." This places the vision in 537 or 536 BC — roughly two years after the decree of return preserved in the **Cyrus Cylinder** (538 BC, BM 90920) and after the first wave of returnees had begun the journey back to Jerusalem under Sheshbazzar (Ezra 1:8) and Zerubbabel (Ezra 2). Daniel, by his own account, did not return; he remained in the diaspora as an aged courtier, now perhaps in his eighties if the book's chronology is taken at face value. The setting on the bank of the Tigris (10:4) places him in the heartland of the former Babylonian empire, now under Persian administration, in the satrapy that would become known as Babylonia-and-Across-the-River.

The political situation in 537 BC was unstable in a way that bears on the chapter's theology. The early returnees had begun to lay the foundations of the temple but had run into local opposition — Ezra 4 records the lawsuits and intrigues of the Samaritan and Persian-provincial officials that stalled construction for decades, until the second year of Darius (520 BC) when Haggai and Zechariah called for the work to resume. Cyrus himself was preoccupied with the eastern frontier; he would die in 530 BC fighting the Massagetae. The "Prince of Persia" who in the chapter resists the angelic messenger for twenty-one days (10:13) is read by all serious commentators as the spiritual-political patron of the Persian empire — *not* a human Persian official, but the angelic-territorial principality behind the Persian throne, in the apocalyptic-mythic conception that crystallizes in the Second Temple period.

Material culture: the standard ascetic discipline Daniel undergoes — "no delicacies, no meat or wine entered my mouth, nor did I anoint myself at all" (10:3) — reflects the *partial fast* common in post-exilic Judaism, distinct from the total fast of the Day of Atonement. The "twenty-fourth day of the first month" (10:4) places the vision *during* what would later be reckoned as the Passover and Unleavened Bread festal complex (Nisan 14–21), with Daniel still in mourning rather than feasting — a striking detail. **John Goldingay** (WBC) notes the cultic dissonance: Daniel is in penitential mode through what should be the year's most joyful season, suggesting either that the festal calendar was not yet fully observed in Babylon or that Daniel's mourning was specifically connected to the troubles besetting the early returnees.

Authorship and dating: chapter 10 begins the great unified vision-discourse of chapters 10–12, the longest sustained apocalyptic narrative in the Hebrew Bible. For the critical consensus (Collins, Newsom, Casey, Grabbe, Davies), the vision is *vaticinium ex eventu* — composed in the 160s BC and projected back into the 6th century to give a 6th-century prophet's voice to a 2nd-century crisis. For the conservative tradition (Miller, Baldwin, Archer, Young), the chapter is what it presents itself as: a genuine prophecy by a 6th-century prophet concerning events centuries later. Both traditions read chapter 10 as the prologue to the precise historical narrative that follows in chapter 11.

Archaeological and textual evidence: the Aramaic chapters of Daniel end at 7:28; chapters 8–12 are entirely in Hebrew. The Old Greek (LXX) and Theodotion versions of Daniel 10 differ significantly in detail though not in substance. The Greek Additions to Daniel — Susanna, Bel and the Dragon, the Prayer of Azariah, the Song of the Three Young Men — sit outside the protocanonical Hebrew text but were preserved in the Septuagint and in the Catholic and Orthodox canons. The eight Daniel manuscripts at Qumran preserve fragments of chapter 10 in 4QDan-c (4Q114).

Key Hebrew terms: *ish levush badim* (אִישׁ לְבוּשׁ בַּדִּים, "a man clothed in linen," 10:5); *sar malkut Paras* (שַׂר מַלְכוּת פָּרַס, "prince of the kingdom of Persia," 10:13); *Mikhael ahad ha-sarim ha-rishonim* (מִיכָאֵל אַחַד הַשָּׂרִים הָרִאשֹׁנִים, "Michael, one of the chief princes," 10:13); *be-acharit ha-yamim* (בְּאַחֲרִית הַיָּמִים, "in the latter days," 10:14) — the standard apocalyptic formula; *sefer ha-emet* (סֵפֶר הָאֱמֶת, "the book of truth," 10:21), the heavenly tablets / book of fates motif paralleled in 1 Enoch 81 and 4 Ezra 14.

## Lens 2 — The Literature

The chapter is a **vision-prologue** in classic apocalyptic form. It establishes the visionary's preparation (the three-week fast), the theophany or angelophany (10:5–6), the visionary's prostration and incapacitation (10:8–9), the angelic touch and strengthening (10:10–18), and the angelic interpretation that will follow in chapters 11–12. The pattern is consistent across Jewish apocalyptic — compare 1 Enoch 14, the Apocalypse of Abraham, and 4 Ezra. **Christopher Rowland**'s *The Open Heaven* analyzes this preparation-and-mediation pattern as the formal structure of the apocalyptic encounter.

The description of the figure in 10:5–6 is one of the most theologically dense in the OT. The cumulative imagery — linen garment, golden belt, body like beryl, face like lightning, eyes like flaming torches, limbs like burnished bronze, voice like the sound of a multitude — is picked up almost verbatim in Revelation 1:13–16, where John applies it to the glorified Son of Man. The literary borrowing is unmistakable. **Carol Newsom** notes that the imagery draws on the priestly investiture (the linen, paralleling Lev 16:4), royal regalia (the golden belt), and theophanic phenomena (the lightning, the bronze, the multitudinous voice — all standard Sinai-and-Ezekiel theophanic markers). The figure is *not* unambiguously identified within the chapter. It may or may not be the same figure as the angelic messenger who later speaks in 10:11–14; the text's careful ambiguity has invited two millennia of debate.

Textual variants: the Old Greek and Theodotion handle the figure's description somewhat differently, with Theodotion preserving the more vivid imagery that would later inform the Greek-text-citing John of Patmos. The phrase *be-acharit ha-yamim* in 10:14 is one of the standard apocalyptic temporal formulas, paralleled in Genesis 49:1 (Jacob's blessings), Numbers 24:14, Isaiah 2:2, Hosea 3:5, and Micah 4:1 — but in Daniel 10 it takes on a markedly more eschatological coloring.

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

The chapter sits at the deepest layer of Jewish angelology and Hebrew Bible cosmology. Behind it lies the Deuteronomy 32:8 tradition: in the **Masoretic Text**, "When the Most High gave to the nations their inheritance, when he divided mankind, he fixed the borders of the peoples *according to the number of the sons of Israel*" — but in the **Dead Sea Scrolls (4QDeutj)** and the **Septuagint**, the reading is *according to the number of the sons of God* (or in some Greek versions, *angels of God*). This older textual tradition reflects an Israelite-Hebraic cosmology in which Yahweh allotted the nations to subordinate divine beings while keeping Israel as his own portion. **Michael Heiser**'s *The Unseen Realm* makes this the foundation of his "Deuteronomy 32 worldview" — and Daniel 10 is the chapter where that worldview emerges most explicitly in the canonical Hebrew Bible. The "prince of Persia," the "prince of Greece," and Michael as Israel's prince form a coherent territorial-angelological system: every empire has its corresponding spiritual patron, and the conflicts of empires are mirrored by — or in fact constituted by — conflicts among the heavenly princes.

**Jewish Trajectory**: 1 Enoch (especially the Book of the Watchers, 1 Enoch 1–36, and the Book of Dreams, 1 Enoch 83–90) develops territorial-angelology extensively. The Book of Jubilees (Jub. 15:31–32) makes the angelic-territorial cosmology explicit: God allotted the nations to angelic spirits, but Israel he kept for himself. The Qumran *War Scroll* (1QM) makes Michael the angelic captain on the side of the Sons of Light against Belial. **Saadia Gaon** read the Persian and Greek princes as actual angelic beings; **Ibn Ezra** demythologized them as planetary intelligences in his Aristotelian-Neoplatonic framework; **Maimonides** read them as instances of the active intellect mediating providence. **Daniel Boyarin** (*The Jewish Gospels*) and **Larry Hurtado** (*One God, One Lord*) argue that Second Temple Jewish "two powers" theology — exalted-angelic-mediator figures alongside the high God — emerges directly from Daniel 7's Son of Man and Daniel 10's man-in-linen.

**Christian Trajectory**: The patristic tradition — **Hippolytus**, **Jerome's *Commentary on Daniel* (407)**, **Theodoret**, and through to **Calvin's *Lectures on Daniel* (1561)** — tended to read the figure of 10:5–6 as a **Christophany**, a pre-incarnate appearance of the Son. The verbal correspondence with Revelation 1:13–16 is the strongest internal-NT evidence. **Luther** read it Christologically. The Reformed and Lutheran traditions have generally maintained the Christophany reading, though modern conservative critics (Miller, Baldwin) tend toward an *exalted angelophany* reading on the grounds that the figure in 10:5–6 then summons help from Michael, which would be incongruous if the figure were the Son. The critical tradition (Collins, Newsom, **Norman Porteous**) reads the figure as Gabriel or another high angel — possibly the same figure who spoke in chapters 8 and 9, or possibly a distinct higher mediator. **Margaret Barker**'s "temple theology" treats the figure as a high-angelic mediator parallel to the Yahoel and Metatron traditions of later Jewish mysticism. **Crispin Fletcher-Louis** and **Andrei Orlov** have done substantial work on the relation of Daniel 10 to early Jewish "principal angel" traditions.

**Where they diverge**: The patristic-Reformed Christophany reading insists the figure is the pre-incarnate Christ, anchoring chapter 10 in proto-Trinitarian theology. The angelophany reading — held by most modern critical scholars and increasingly by conservative scholars — places Daniel firmly within Second Temple angelology where high-mediator figures are subordinate to God. The Heiser / Boyarin / Hurtado reading splits the difference: the figure is genuinely an exalted divine mediator, neither simply the Son nor simply an angel, reflecting a Second Temple "two powers" theology that the New Testament inherits and applies to Christ but does not invent.

## Lens 4 — The Theological Intent

**Tension 1: The identity of the man in linen.** The interpretation has divided Christian readers since the earliest commentaries. The Christophany reading (patristic, Reformed) is anchored in the verbal parallels with Revelation 1, the figure's manifestly divine glory, and the ancient Christian instinct to find Christ throughout the OT. Steelman it: every detail of the description (the linen of priestly investiture, the radiant appearance that paralyzes the prophet, the strengthening touch that recalls Ezekiel's own commissioning) corresponds to theophanic-Christophanic patterns elsewhere in the canon; John of Patmos clearly read it Christologically; Calvin's careful exegesis defends the reading rigorously. The angelophany reading (Collins, Newsom, modern critical) is anchored in the figure's request for help from Michael, the broader Second Temple angelology context, and the chapter's parallels with Gabriel's mission in chapters 8–9. Steelman it: the figure speaks in 10:13 of being "withstood" and needing Michael's help — incongruous for a divine person; the Second Temple Jewish reader would have recognized an exalted mediator-angel without needing to import later Christological categories. The Heiser / Boyarin reading: the figure represents a genuine pre-NT category of exalted divine mediator, and the apostolic Christology that lands on Christ does not invent the category but populates it with a specific name. **Where this leaves us:** The text holds the figure at the threshold of identification — too glorious for a routine angel, not unambiguously identified as the divine Self. That ambiguity may be the chapter's deliberate theological strategy.

**Tension 2: The territorial-angelological cosmology.** Does Daniel 10 teach that there are real angelic-political beings ruling over earthly empires? **Heiser** (*The Unseen Realm*) and the contemporary "Deuteronomy 32 worldview" school read it literally: there are corporeal political empires and corresponding spiritual principalities, and the latter genuinely shape the former. Pauline theology takes this seriously — Eph 6:12 ("we wrestle not against flesh and blood but against principalities, powers, the rulers of the darkness of this age") and 1 Cor 2:8 ("the rulers of this age, who are coming to nothing") presuppose Daniel 10's cosmology. **Walter Wink**'s *Powers* trilogy reads the principalities and powers as the *interiority* of corporate-political structures — neither purely spiritual nor purely material, but the spiritual aspect of corporate human reality. **Brueggemann** and the modern critical mainline tend to read the territorial-prince theology as poetic-mythic rendering of political evil. **Bultmann** would demythologize: the cosmology is a 2nd-Temple symbolic apparatus that the modern reader translates into existential or political categories. The Catholic tradition — anchored in Aquinas's angelology and reaffirmed at multiple councils — has consistently affirmed the reality of fallen angelic principalities behind political structures. Eastern Orthodox theology takes the territorial-angelological cosmology seriously and folds it into liturgical reality. Steelman the literal reading: the apocalyptic worldview is internally coherent, has supported actual moral resistance to political evil (the *principalities and powers* language helped Bonhoeffer and the Confessing Church name the German Christian apostasy), and is the most natural reading of the text. Steelman the demythologizing reading: literal angelic-political ontology is hard to integrate with modern political analysis without slipping into conspiracy-theory thinking, and Wink's reading preserves the moral content while purifying the cosmology. **Where this leaves us:** the chapter forces the reader to take seriously the claim that the politics we see is *not* the whole of what is happening — that prayer, fasting, and discernment matter precisely because there are powers behind powers. Whether one reads those powers as literally angelic, as the spiritual interiority of political structures, or as poetic rendering of historical providence, the moral imperative is similar.

**The unresolved question:** If Daniel's twenty-one days of prayer correlate with the angelic messenger's twenty-one-day delay against the prince of Persia, what does it actually mean to say that prayer "affects" the unseen realm? The text seems to assume a moral physics in which human petition is causally tied to angelic outcome. Is that mythic furniture, or is it the hidden architecture of how everything actually works?

## The Thread

**Deuteronomy 32:8 (LXX/4QDeutj reading)** — "He fixed the borders of the peoples according to the number of the sons of God." The textual ground for the territorial-angelological cosmology that Daniel 10 makes explicit. Heiser's pivotal text.

**Ezekiel 1:26–28** — Ezekiel's vision of the figure on the throne, the closest OT parallel to the man in linen of Daniel 10. The radiance, the gleaming bronze, the prophet's incapacitation — all Ezekiel's pattern. The intertextual debt is heavy.

**Revelation 1:13–16** — John of Patmos applies the description of Daniel 10:5–6 directly to the glorified Christ, with verbal parallels at every line. Whatever Daniel 10's original referent, the NT canonizes a Christological reading.

**Ephesians 6:12** — "We wrestle not against flesh and blood, but against principalities, against powers, against the rulers of the darkness of this world, against spiritual wickedness in high places." The Pauline theology of the powers presupposes Daniel 10's cosmology and translates it into the church's spiritual-warfare practice.

**Jude 9** — "Yet Michael the archangel, when contending with the devil he disputed about the body of Moses, did not bring against him a railing accusation, but said, The Lord rebuke thee." Jude assumes Daniel 10's Michael as a known figure — the angelic captain in continued conflict — and links it to a tradition (the Assumption of Moses) outside the Hebrew canon, attesting how widely Daniel 10's angelology had spread.
