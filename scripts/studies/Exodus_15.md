# Exodus 15: Who Is Like You Among the Gods?

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

If Exodus 14 is the theological hinge of the Torah, Exodus 15 is arguably its oldest surviving text. Frank Moore Cross and David Noel Freedman, in *Studies in Ancient Yahwistic Poetry* (originally 1950; revised 1975), argued that the Song of the Sea (vv. 1–18) preserves linguistic features that place its composition in the late thirteenth or twelfth century BCE — contemporary with or only slightly after the events it celebrates, and several centuries earlier than the classical Hebrew prose of the surrounding narrative. Their evidence is morphological and phonological: the preservation of the archaic 2ms suffix *-ka* rather than later *-kā*; the use of the enclitic *mem*, a Canaanite particle that dropped out of later Hebrew; archaic verbal forms without *waw*-consecutive; and parallelism with Ugaritic poetry from Ras Shamra (thirteenth century). If their dating holds, Exodus 15:1–18 is roughly contemporary with the Merneptah Stele and belongs to the same late Bronze/early Iron Age linguistic horizon as the Song of Deborah (Judges 5) and portions of Genesis 49, Numbers 23–24, and Deuteronomy 32–33.

The Song of Miriam (v. 21) — a single bicolon — is probably older still. Phyllis Trible, in "Bringing Miriam Out of the Shadows" (*Bible Review* 1989) and later in *God and the Rhetoric of Sexuality*, argued that the Miriam fragment is the kernel around which the longer Song of the Sea was expanded, with the longer poem retrospectively attributed to Moses. Cheryl Exum has developed this line of argument. If correct, the structure of the chapter is an older cultic song belonging to the prophetess Miriam (v. 20 calls her *nĕbîʾâ*, prophetess — one of only a handful of women to bear that title) recontextualized inside an expanded Mosaic victory hymn and framed by late prose.

Political and cultural horizon: the song names specific peoples (v. 14–15) — Philistia, Edom, Moab, the inhabitants of Canaan. Philistines are historically attested from the early twelfth century (Ramesses III's Medinet Habu reliefs of the Sea Peoples); their appearance in the song is often taken as a terminus post quem, though Cross and Freedman argued Philistia in this verse could refer to the earlier Peleset presence or be a slightly later editorial update. Edom and Moab as settled political entities are attested archaeologically from roughly the thirteenth to twelfth century (the transjordan transition from nomadic to sedentary patterns in this period is the subject of ongoing debate between Israel Finkelstein's low chronology and the traditional chronology defended by scholars like Kitchen).

Material and literary culture: the Song of the Sea sits in close intertextual proximity to the Ugaritic Baal Cycle, in which Baal defeats Yam (Sea) and is enthroned on his holy mountain. Cross in *Canaanite Myth and Hebrew Epic* documents the parallels in detail — the combat with the sea, the victory shout, the mountain sanctuary, the eternal kingship formula. But the transformation is as striking as the parallel: YHWH does not fight the sea personified as a rival god; YHWH uses the sea against a historical enemy, Pharaoh's army. The mythic pattern is historicized.

Marah, Elim, and the wilderness of Shur belong to the physical geography of the crossing into Sinai. Shur is the Egyptian frontier desert, east of the Delta, across which trade routes ran to the Negev and Sinai proper. Marah's bitter water is geographically plausible — much of the wilderness east of the lakes is brackish, high in magnesium and sulfur. Various identifications have been proposed; Ain Hawarah on the traditional southern Sinai route is the standard guess, though nothing is archaeologically anchored. Elim with its twelve springs and seventy palms is again traditionally identified with Wadi Gharandel. As with the sea crossing itself, I name the options (traditional southern Sinai; a more northern route via Mount Karkom argued by Emmanuel Anati; Kitchen's middle path) without picking.

Authorship and dating: the most widely held view is that the Song of the Sea is a pre-existing archaic poem incorporated into the composite narrative. The surrounding prose (vv. 19, 22–27) is generally assigned to J (the Marah episode) with P elements (v. 19 recapitulation). The Song of Miriam is either a J fragment or a standalone pre-J unit.

Archaeology and the textual history of the Song: the most important material witness is 4QpaleoExodm, a Qumran manuscript written in the old paleo-Hebrew script (used for archaizing liturgical contexts), which preserves much of Exodus including the Song. The stichography — the arrangement of poetic lines on the column — in 4QpaleoExodm and in the Masoretic text preserves a traditional brick-like pattern ("a half-brick over a whole brick"), marking the text as liturgically distinct. The Nash Papyrus and later synagogue tradition confirm that the Song was sung as a discrete unit in worship.

Key Hebrew terms:

- *ʾāšîrâ la-YHWH kî gāʾōh gāʾâ* (אָשִׁירָה לַיהוָה כִּי־גָאֹה גָּאָה) — "I will sing to YHWH, for he has triumphed gloriously" (v. 1, intensive infinitive absolute construction).
- *mî kāmōkâ bāʾēlîm* (מִי־כָמֹכָה בָּאֵלִים) — "who is like you among the gods" (v. 11).
- *nēd* (נֵד) — a heap (v. 8), the same rare word used in Joshua 3:13, 16 of the Jordan standing in a heap.
- *qādĕšekā* (קָדְשֶׁךָ) — your holy (abode), holiness-root language for a sanctuary (v. 13).
- *nĕbîʾâ* (נְבִיאָה) — prophetess (v. 20).
- *rōpʾekā* (רֹפְאֶךָ) — your healer (v. 26), from the root *rpʾ*.

## Lens 2 — The Literature

Genre: hymnic victory song (vv. 1–18), a brief antiphon (v. 21), and prose etiological narrative (vv. 22–27). The victory song belongs to a well-attested ancient Near Eastern genre, with closest parallels in the Ugaritic Baal Cycle and later in the Song of Deborah (Judges 5). Within the Hebrew Bible, it is one of a small cluster of poems — along with Deuteronomy 32, Judges 5, 2 Samuel 22 / Psalm 18, Habakkuk 3 — that Cross grouped as pre-monarchic.

Structure: the chiastic shape of the Song has been debated extensively. William Propp in the Anchor Bible surveys the major proposals. Cross argued for a tripartite structure: verses 1–5 (defeat of the Egyptians), 6–10 (praise of YHWH's right hand), 11–18 (procession to the sanctuary). Others (including Umberto Cassuto) argue for chiasm centered on verse 11 ("Who is like you among the gods?") — the rhetorical hinge. Robert Alter in *The Art of Biblical Poetry* observes that the Song uses staircase parallelism (a form also attested at Ugarit): "The LORD is my strength and my song / and he has become my salvation" (v. 2) — the second colon extends and intensifies the first rather than merely restating it. The "horse and rider" bicolon repeats at the end of verse 1 and again at verse 21, forming an envelope.

Literary devices: the anthropomorphism of YHWH is startlingly direct — "the blast of your nostrils" (v. 8), "your right hand" (vv. 6, 12), "the greatness of your arm" (v. 16). The contrast between Pharaoh's boast (v. 9, six short verbs: "I will pursue, I will overtake, I will divide, my desire shall have its fill, I will draw, my hand shall destroy") and YHWH's single breath (v. 10: "you blew with your wind") is one of the most efficient displays of verbal irony in the Hebrew Bible. Pharaoh's six verbs of conquest are undone by one exhalation.

Wordplay on *yam* runs throughout: *yam* is sea, the Canaanite god Yam is the mythic rival, and *yam sûp* is the named body of water. The poem compresses cosmology, mythology, and geography into a single syllable.

Textual variants: the Song's text is unusually stable. 4QpaleoExodm preserves almost the whole of 15:1–18 in its paleo-Hebrew stichography. The LXX of 15:11 has *en hagiois* ("among the holy ones") for *bāʾēlîm*, possibly defusing the henotheistic edge; the Samaritan retains the plural. The Mekhilta preserves a textual tradition in which verse 2's *zeh ʾēlî* ("this is my God") was read as demonstrative — the singers were pointing at the divine presence.

The Marah narrative (vv. 22–27) is etiological: the place is named *mārâ* ("bitter") because the water was bitter. The sweetening by a tree or log (*ʿēṣ*) has no natural parallel obvious in botany; the Mekhilta reads the wood as already sweet (miracle by sympathetic medicine) or as bitter (miracle by inversion). Elim's twelve springs and seventy palms (v. 27) read as a topographical miniature of the tribal twelve and the seventy elders of Numbers 11.

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

The Song of the Sea occupies a unique place in the canon. Jon Levenson treats it in *Sinai and Zion* as the charter-document of a theology of YHWH's kingship grounded in sanctuary. Verses 17–18 — "you will bring them in and plant them on your own mountain... the sanctuary which your hands have established. The LORD will reign forever and ever" — project the crossing forward toward a holy place not yet occupied. The song is both retrospective (celebrating what has happened) and proleptic (anticipating what has not).

**Jewish Interpretive Trajectory.** Second Temple: Wisdom of Solomon 10:20 poetically paraphrases the Song; Pseudo-Philo LAB 10 elaborates. Philo (*Life of Moses* 2.256–257) reads the two songs (Moses' and Miriam's) as matched choirs, male and female. Josephus is comparatively brief. Rabbinic: the Mekhilta de-Rabbi Ishmael devotes an entire tractate, *Shirata*, to the Song — ten full sections of running commentary. A famous line from Shirata: "Even a maidservant at the Sea saw what Isaiah and Ezekiel did not see." The Song is the high-water mark of Israel's direct perception of YHWH. Bavli Sotah 30b–31a discusses the mode of singing: did Moses sing each line and Israel respond; did they sing together; was it like Hallel? Rabbi Akiva and Rabbi Eliezer preserve different positions. The Song of Miriam is read as the originating tradition: the women sang first, and their song was incorporated.

Medieval: Rashi treats the Song phrase by phrase, drawing on Mekhilta and Targum. Ibn Ezra attends to the grammar (the infinitive absolute *gāʾōh gāʾâ*). Ramban sees v. 11 as asserting that there are no other gods — *ʾēlîm* is rhetorical. Maimonides (*Guide* I.46, III.32) reads the anthropomorphisms as accommodations to human understanding, not literal attributes.

Modern: Cross's work on the archaic poetry. Levenson on the sanctuary-mountain motif. Ilana Pardes on the Song as Israel's birth-cry. Pardes, building on Rachel Adler and Ellen Frankel, reads the women's tambourines in verse 20 as a cultic, not merely spontaneous, act — Israelite women in the role of celebratory singers was a recognized ancient Near Eastern institution (the *meannayot* or female singers of victory, attested in 1 Samuel 18:6–7). Heschel (*The Prophets*) reads verse 11 as pathos made theology.

**Christian Interpretive Trajectory.** Patristic: Origen (*Homilies on Exodus* 6) reads the Song as the church's eschatological song, echoed in Revelation 15:2–3. Ambrose treats verses 1–2 as the paradigm of eucharistic thanksgiving. Augustine in *City of God* 18.11 uses verse 18 ("the LORD will reign forever") as a bridge to eschatology. Gregory of Nyssa (*Life of Moses* 2.131–137) reads the song as the soul's doxology after passing through the purgative waters. Chrysostom on the Song as a model of liturgical thanksgiving.

Medieval: Aquinas cites the Song for Christ's kingship (*ST* III q. 22). Bernard of Clairvaux on verse 11 as affirming YHWH's incomparability. Reformation: Luther's Lenten sermons on the Song; Calvin's commentary rejects allegorizing excess and emphasizes historical victory.

Critical: Martin Noth treated the Song as later than the prose — the opposite of Cross's position; Noth saw it as a learned hymn composed in the monarchic period. This is one of the classical disagreements in Old Testament scholarship. Brevard Childs's canonical approach holds both at arm's length: whatever the compositional date, the Song's canonical function is clear. Propp's commentary is the standard philological reference. Fretheim reads the Song theologically as YHWH's self-revelation through violence against oppression. Walter Moberly (*The Old Testament of the Old Testament*) treats the song's theology of YHWH's kingship as the seedbed of later Zion theology.

**Convergence and divergence.** Jewish tradition has kept the Song alive liturgically: it is recited every morning in the *shacharit* prayer (P'sukei d'Zimrah section), every Sabbath, and in a heightened form on the seventh day of Passover, which commemorates the crossing. The Song is not historical memory only; it is ongoing liturgical present. Christian tradition reads the Song typologically as anticipating the Song of the Lamb in Revelation 15, as the eucharistic thanksgiving, and as the model for baptismal praise. Critical scholarship has spent a century arguing over whether the Song is archaic Mosaic-era poetry (Cross/Freedman) or monarchic-period learned hymn (Noth); the linguistic evidence on the whole favors Cross, though not without qualification. On the sensitive question of verse 11's *ʾēlîm*, Levenson has argued against readings that make early Israelite religion strictly monotheistic in the later sense; early Israel assumed the existence of other beings called *ʾēlîm* or *bĕnê ʾēlîm* and asserted YHWH's incomparability and kingship *among* them. This is not the same as classical polytheism and not the same as later philosophical monotheism.

## Lens 4 — The Theological Intent

**Tension 1: Celebration of violence.** The Song sings the drowning of Pharaoh's army with apparently unqualified joy. "The LORD is a man of war" (v. 3). How is this to be received, especially in the light of the rabbinic pushback in Bavli Megillah 10b that God silenced the angels' song because Egyptians were drowning?

- Catholic (CCC 2258, 2261 on the sanctity of life; Ratzinger, *Jesus of Nazareth* vol. 2): the Song celebrates divine justice against oppression, not bloodshed as such; the pedagogical movement of revelation carries us toward the enemy-love of the Sermon on the Mount. Aquinas (*ST* II-II q. 108 on vengeance) distinguishes divine *vindicatio* from human vengeance.
- Reformed (Calvin on Exodus 15; Herman Bavinck *Reformed Dogmatics* 3): the Song is the church's legitimate song against injustice; the violence is God's judicial act. Cornelis Van der Waal insists on the song's imprecatory function.
- Lutheran (Luther, *Bondage of the Will*; Bonhoeffer's reflections on the imprecatory Psalms): Luther embraces the hard edge — God's left-handed work of judgment is real and we must not sentimentalize it. Bonhoeffer later argued that the imprecations belong to Christ on the cross, not to the church unmediated.
- Wesleyan/Arminian: the Song celebrates deliverance, not death; the aesthetic focus is on YHWH's triumph, with the Egyptians as the instrument of self-destruction rather than the object of celebration.
- Orthodox (Schmemann *For the Life of the World*; Alexander Golitzin): the Song is cosmic liturgy; the enemy's fall represents the destruction of *thanatos*, not persons. Florovsky reads the Song in the mode of eschatological hope against the powers.
- Jewish (Mekhilta Shirata; Megillah 10b; Heschel, *The Prophets*): the tradition itself registers discomfort — the angels are silenced, though Israel sings. Rashi captures the distinction: Israel sings because they are delivered; the angels are not the ones delivered. Levenson argues that the violence is the violence necessary for the emergence of a covenant people from bondage, not violence glorified.
- Critical (Brueggemann *The Prophetic Imagination*, *Theology of the Old Testament*): the Song is counter-imperial speech. The dominant reading silences the Egyptian voice; Brueggemann insists the text's violence cannot be softened without also softening the protest against empire that it enables.

The least intuitive position, worth steelmanning, is Luther's unsentimental embrace of judgment. Luther rejects the move that either allegorizes the drowning (Origen: the passions drown) or sentimentalizes it (the Egyptians weren't really enemies). He argues that to refuse to say "the LORD is a man of war" is to refuse the God the Bible actually gives. The comfort of the Gospel, for Luther, depends on God being genuinely a warrior against what enslaves — sin, death, Pharaoh. A sanitized God cannot actually save.

**Where this leaves us:** The text celebrates; the tradition (rabbinic and patristic) registers discomfort with celebration of the enemy's death. The canonical movement is not from violence to non-violence as if the earlier is obsolete, but toward a reframing in which the violence belongs properly to God and must not be appropriated by the redeemed unmediated.

**Tension 2: *mî kāmōkâ bāʾēlîm* — the status of other gods.** Is verse 11's "who is like you among the gods" a rhetorical flourish within strict monotheism, an affirmation of henotheism (YHWH as highest among real gods), or an artifact of an earlier stage of Israelite religion?

- Catholic (CCC 2112–2114; Ratzinger *Introduction to Christianity*): the Song uses the language of the world from which Israel was called; verse 11 is rhetorical polemic, not doctrine. The development of monotheistic precision is compatible with early poetic diction.
- Reformed (Vos *Biblical Theology*; John Frame): progressive revelation — early expressions use available language; the doctrine of God's aseity is implicit from the start but articulated progressively.
- Lutheran (Luther's *Large Catechism* on the First Commandment): whatever you trust is your god; the *ʾēlîm* are the false gods of the nations, which the Song ridicules by acclaiming YHWH's incomparability.
- Wesleyan/Arminian: the song affirms exclusive devotion; other *ʾēlîm* exist as idols (no reality) or as lesser spiritual beings.
- Orthodox (Lossky *Mystical Theology*; Florovsky): the *ʾēlîm* may be angelic powers, the "sons of God" of Psalm 82 and Deuteronomy 32:8 (LXX); YHWH is the incomparable Most High.
- Jewish (Levenson *Creation and the Persistence of Evil*; John Day *Yahweh and the Gods and Goddesses of Canaan*): early Israelite religion is henotheistic-monolatrous before it becomes classically monotheistic. The *ʾēlîm* are real as members of the divine council; YHWH alone is worshipped. Rashi (following the Mekhilta) defuses this by reading *ʾēlîm* as "mighty ones" — rhetorical.
- Critical (Cross; Mark Smith *The Early History of God*; Christopher Rollston): early Israel inherited a Canaanite cosmology and progressively differentiated YHWH from the pantheon. The Song reflects a stage in that process.

The least intuitive position for most contemporary readers is the critical-Jewish reading represented by Levenson, Smith, and Day: that early Israelite religion *did* assume the existence of other divine beings and asserted YHWH's uniqueness *among* them, rather than denying their existence. Steelmanned: this reading takes the Song's grammar seriously (the plural *ʾēlîm* with the preposition *b-*, "among"), takes seriously the divine council imagery elsewhere (Deuteronomy 32:8, Psalm 82, 1 Kings 22:19–23), and refuses to anachronistically retroject later conciliar theology. The gain is exegetical honesty; the cost is friction with later credal formulation, which is why Rashi and Ramban rhetoricalize the plural.

**Where this leaves us:** The Song's grammar is open; the canonical trajectory is toward a monotheism in which other *ʾēlîm* are, depending on tradition, nothing, idols, demons, or created spiritual beings. The traditions differ on where on that trajectory verse 11 already sits.

*Unresolved question:* If the Song of Miriam (v. 21) is the older and shorter kernel — if Miriam's antiphon is the originating liturgy and Moses' longer song is expansion — what does it mean that the canonical tradition names the longer song after Moses (v. 1: "Moses and the people of Israel sang") while assigning the shorter, older fragment to the prophetess? Is this a tendentious masculinization of an originally women-led liturgy, as Trible argues, or a recognition that the covenant singer speaks for both? The chapter will not answer.

## The Thread

- **Genesis 1:6–10 and the dividing of the waters.** The waters "piled up" in verse 8 (*nēd*, "heap") use creation language. The Song reads the crossing as re-creation; exodus and Genesis are liturgically bound.
- **Judges 5, the Song of Deborah.** A second ancient victory song led by a woman prophet (Deborah is also called a *nĕbîʾâ*), also celebrating YHWH's triumph over chariots (Sisera's chariots here instead of Pharaoh's). Cross grouped Exodus 15 and Judges 5 as the two oldest substantial poetic texts in the Bible. The structural parallel — woman prophet + tambourine + song celebrating the overthrow of a chariot army — is almost certainly deliberate.
- **Habakkuk 3:8–15.** Habakkuk's theophany returns directly to the Song's imagery: "Was your wrath against the rivers, O LORD? Was your anger against the rivers, or your indignation against the sea, when you rode on your horses, on your chariot of salvation?" The later prophet re-mythologizes the Song, converting its historicized combat myth back into cosmic-combat imagery and applying it to a fresh imperial crisis.
- **Revelation 15:2–3, the Song of Moses and of the Lamb.** The apocalyptic vision places the victors by a "sea of glass mingled with fire" singing "the song of Moses, the servant of God, and the song of the Lamb." The same Song, in a transfigured key, is the eschatological liturgy. The Christian typological reading is grounded not in patristic speculation but in John's vision.
