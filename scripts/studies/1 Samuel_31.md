# 1 Samuel 31: Mount Gilboa and the Tragedy of Saul

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

The catastrophe unfolds on **Mount Gilboa** (הַר הַגִּלְבֹּעַ), the limestone ridge that closes the Jezreel Valley to the southeast. Gilboa rises about 500 meters above the valley floor — modest by Galilean standards, but commanding the strategic junction where the coastal Via Maris met the road to the Jordan and Beth-shan. Israelite forces fighting downhill against Philistine chariotry on the Jezreel plain were at a structural disadvantage. The flat valley floor neutralized Israel's hill-country advantages and enabled the Philistines' iron-rimmed chariots and composite-bow archery — the same archers who "found" Saul in 31:3, almost certainly Aegean-trained mercenaries firing the recurved composite bow that had been standard Aegean and Egyptian-influenced military technology since the Late Bronze Age. The Israelite levy, lightly armed and short on iron, had no good answer.

The geography forces the catastrophe. Once the Israelite line broke on the slopes, retreat northeast through Beth-shan was cut off; retreat south through the Wadi Harod toward Judah was the only option. The narrator's note that "the men of Israel who were on the other side of the valley and those beyond the Jordan saw that the men of Israel had fled... they abandoned their cities" (31:7) describes a strategic collapse: the Philistines now controlled not just the Jezreel valley but the trans-Jordanian Israelite enclaves, cutting off the northern tribes from the southern. This is the worst military defeat in the early monarchy and arguably the precipitating event for everything that follows in 2 Samuel.

**Beth-shan** (Tel Beit She'an) is one of the most thoroughly excavated tells in the Levant. Penn Museum work from the 1920s-1930s under Clarence Fisher and Alan Rowe, followed by Yigael Yadin's surveys and especially **Amihai Mazar's** Hebrew University excavations from 1989 onward, have produced one of the densest stratigraphic records in the southern Levant. The relevant strata are Late Bronze IIB through Iron I — the period of the Egyptian XX Dynasty (Ramesses III and successors), when Beth-shan was an Egyptian garrison town with administrative buildings, a temple complex, and stelae of Sethos I and Ramesses II/III. The Egyptian withdrawal in the late twelfth century left a power vacuum that Philistines and Canaanites jointly filled; by the late eleventh century, when the chapter is set, Beth-shan was clearly within the Philistine sphere, with characteristic bichrome and monochrome Philistine pottery present in the Iron I layers. The "temple of Ashtaroth" (31:10) where Saul's armor is displayed corresponds plausibly to one of the well-documented Late Bronze/Iron I temples on the tell — Mazar's excavations identified two paired temples in the Iron I layer that some have associated with the dual-deity worship that produced the plural form *Ashtaroth*. The "wall of Beth-shan" to which Saul's body is fastened was the city's defensive perimeter; bodies displayed on city walls were a standard Iron Age intimidation technique attested in Assyrian reliefs from Nimrud and Khorsabad and in the Mesha Stele from Moab.

Three Hebrew terms reward attention. **Areilim** (עֲרֵלִים, 31:4) — "uncircumcised" — is Saul's term of contempt for the Philistines and his deepest fear. Circumcision in Israelite ideology was the bodily marker of covenant identity; capture by the uncircumcised would mean ritualized mockery, possibly castration or genital mutilation, as a ritualized inversion of the captive's identity. Saul's request to his armor-bearer is not just a request to die quickly; it is a request to die *intact*, with his Israelite identity unviolated. The term recurs at the David-Goliath narrative (1 Sam 17:26, 36) and it has been read by Carol Meyers and others as a marker of Israelite-Philistine boundary maintenance — perhaps the most ideologically loaded ethnic distinction in the Hebrew Bible.

**Hit'allelu** (הִתְעַלְּלוּ, 31:4) — "mistreat me" — is the hithpael of *'alal*, with a connotation of repeated, sadistic abuse. The verb appears in Exodus 10:2 (YHWH's "mockery" of Egypt), Numbers 22:29 (Balaam's complaint to the donkey), and Judges 19:25 (the Levite's concubine), where it carries the specific sense of sexualized violation. Saul is afraid of being raped, displayed, and killed slowly; the cluster of meanings is unmistakable.

**Eshel** (אֵשֶׁל, 31:13) — the "tamarisk tree" under which the men of Jabesh-gilead bury Saul's bones — is significant. The same word appears in Genesis 21:33 (Abraham plants an *eshel* at Beersheba and calls on the name of YHWH) and in 1 Sam 22:6 (Saul sits under the *eshel* at Gibeah, in his court). The tamarisk is a slow-growing desert tree with deep symbolic resonance in Semitic ritual — a marker of place, a node of memory, a tree that survives drought. That Saul is buried under an *eshel* echoes the *eshel* under which he held court; the narrator is closing a circle.

**Sources and dating.** The chapter sits at the climactic close of what Rost identified as the **History of David's Rise** (HDR) — though some critics extend HDR through 2 Samuel 5 — and at the join with the **Court History** or **Succession Narrative** (Rost's other major source). The chapter has a synoptic parallel in **1 Chronicles 10**, which tracks 1 Samuel 31 closely but adds a theological coda in 10:13-14 that explicitly attributes Saul's death to his unfaithfulness, his consultation of the medium at Endor, and his failure to inquire of YHWH. The Chronicler is editorializing what 1 Samuel narrates with restraint. There is also a competing account in **2 Samuel 1**: the Amalekite messenger who arrives at Ziklag claims to have killed Saul himself at Saul's request. The two accounts cannot both be literally true. McCarter, Halpern, and McKenzie all read the Amalekite as either lying (to gain favor with David) or as preserving an alternative tradition. The Deuteronomist has placed both accounts in the canon without harmonizing them — a noteworthy editorial choice.

**4QSam-a** is fragmentary in this chapter but has been important for restoring readings; the LXX preserves a few small variants, but the basic shape of the narrative is stable across the textual tradition.

## Lens 2 — The Literature

Generically, 1 Samuel 31 is **biblical tragedy** in something close to the Aristotelian sense — and this is one of the rare places where the comparison to Greek tragedy is not anachronistic but illuminating. The chapter is a fall from height (Saul, the anointed king), through a recognizable hamartia (the failure to obey the prophetic command in chapters 13 and 15, the consultation of the medium in chapter 28), to a death that is both inevitable and freely chosen. The four suicides in five verses (the three sons fall in battle; Saul falls on his sword; the armor-bearer falls on his) compress the catastrophe into liturgical density. **David Gunn**'s *The Fate of King Saul* (1980) is the indispensable scholarly treatment: Gunn argues that Saul is the Hebrew Bible's most fully realized tragic protagonist, a figure whose vocation traps him and whose dignity in dying redeems some of the pathos. **J. Cheryl Exum**'s *Tragedy and Biblical Narrative* (1992) extends and partly revises Gunn, locating Saul within a broader category of biblical tragic figures and emphasizing the structural inevitability built into his role.

The Greek parallels are real but not perfect. The closest analog is **Sophocles' Ajax**: the warrior king, betrayed by the gods, falling on his sword to die with his honor intact rather than face capture and humiliation. Both Saul and Ajax die at sunset, both refuse to be killed by enemies, both seek through suicide a final assertion of agency in the face of divine abandonment. The differences matter too: the Hebrew narrator does not editorialize as the Greek chorus does; the moral judgments are inferred, not stated. Gunn presses the parallel; Exum complicates it.

The chapter's literary structure is binary, almost diptych-like. Verses 1-7: the battle and the four deaths. Verses 8-13: the bodies, the desecration, and the rescue. Each half ends with a movement: in the first, the Israelites flee and the Philistines occupy; in the second, the men of Jabesh-gilead retrieve and bury. The two movements mirror each other inversely. The first is dispossession (Israel loses cities); the second is repossession (Saul's bones return to Israelite ground). The narrator is, structurally, refusing to let the catastrophe end with desecration.

**Three literary devices** are particularly worth naming. First, **typological inversion** with the David-Goliath narrative. In 1 Samuel 17, David cuts off Goliath's head and takes the Philistine's armor; in 1 Samuel 31, the Philistines cut off Saul's head (31:9) and put his armor in the temple of Ashtaroth (31:10). The inversion is exact and surely deliberate. Polzin (*Samuel and the Deuteronomist*) and Brueggemann (Interpretation) both note the chiasm; Alter develops it most fully in *The David Story*. Saul, who could not be the David that defeated Goliath, becomes in death the Goliath that David defeated.

Second, **ring composition** with 1 Samuel 11. Saul's first major act as king-in-waiting was to rescue Jabesh-gilead from Nahash the Ammonite (chapter 11); his bones are now buried by the men of Jabesh-gilead in an act of pious gratitude (chapter 31). The reign opens and closes at Jabesh-gilead. Robert Alter calls this the chapter's most "astonishing" structural detail; Fokkelman (vol. 2) maps the parallels in detail.

Third, **understated reportage**. The narrator refuses every available editorial intervention. There is no theological judgment on Saul's suicide. There is no praise of Jabesh-gilead's piety. There is no condemnation of the Philistine desecration. The chapter is the most restrained narrative voice in Samuel; the events are made to speak for themselves. This restraint is itself a literary choice; the **Chronicler**, retelling the same material in 1 Chronicles 10, finds the silence intolerable and adds the explicit theological gloss the Deuteronomist withheld.

**Textual variants.** **4QSam-a** is fragmentary here but where readings are recoverable they generally agree with MT or with LXX/MT against minor scribal corruptions. **LXX** has small expansions, particularly in 31:9-10, where the Greek tradition adds the detail that Saul's head was sent specifically as a trophy. McCarter (AB) and Klein (WBC) discuss the variants without finding them meaning-affecting. The most interesting comparative textual question is the relationship to 1 Chronicles 10: the Chronicler's text is so close that it is almost certainly drawing on a recension of Samuel close to MT, with theological additions specific to the Chronicler.

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

The canonical arc here is the tragic close of Israel's first monarchic experiment and the necessary precondition for David's rise. Saul's death enables what Saul's life prevented — David's accession — but the narrative refuses to celebrate. 2 Samuel 1 will open with David's lament: "How the mighty have fallen!"

**Jewish interpretive trajectories** have wrestled hard with Saul's suicide and with the chapter's theological silence. **Josephus** (*Antiquities* 6.14) handles Saul's death with surprising sympathy, framing it as the dignified end of a noble warrior; he subtly reshapes the suicide as a death in battle. The **rabbinic tradition** is divided on whether Saul's suicide was permissible. The Babylonian Talmud (*Bava Kama* 91b) cites Saul's death as one of the rare cases where suicide is justified — to avoid mockery and torture by enemies. The medieval halakhic tradition (Maimonides in *Mishneh Torah*, *Hilkhot Rotseah*) generally follows this lenient ruling, and Saul becomes a precedent for the principle that one may take one's own life rather than submit to forced apostasy or sexual violation. This ruling has had substantial later weight in discussions of martyrdom (*kiddush ha-Shem*).

**Rashi** on 31:4 glosses Saul's suicide as a justified act of self-protection from desecration; he does not condemn it. **Radak** is more searching, raising but not resolving the question of whether Saul's suicide reflects despair or righteousness. **Ralbag** — characteristically philosophical — analyzes the moment in terms of the relative weight of the prohibition against suicide versus the obligation to avoid public profanation of God's name. **Don Isaac Abarbanel** writes at exegetical length on the chapter and is particularly attentive to the political ramifications: Saul's death produces the constitutional crisis that the next two books of Samuel will navigate. **Avivah Zornberg** has written movingly on the chapter as a study in the tragedy of vocation: the king who could not become what he was anointed to be. **Yair Zakovitch** and **Moshe Garsiel** treat the literary and apologetic dimensions; **Garsiel** especially highlights the way the narrator's silence functions as a kind of mercy toward Saul.

**Christian interpretive trajectories** have likewise struggled. **Origen** treats Saul's death allegorically as the fall of the rejected soul. **Augustine** in *City of God* (Book 1, chapters 17-27) gives the chapter sustained attention in the context of a much larger argument about whether suicide is ever permissible; Augustine condemns suicide in general but treats Saul as a difficult case, a tragic exemplum rather than a model. **Ambrose** is gentler; **John Chrysostom**, who preaches a famous series on Saul, treats the death as the consequence of the long arc of disobedience that began in chapter 13. **Gregory the Great**, in the *Moralia*, returns repeatedly to Saul as the type of the leader who falls through pride; his treatment in Book 5 is especially developed. **Aquinas** treats Saul's suicide in *Summa Theologiae* II-II, q. 64, a. 5 as an act condemned by reason and natural law, and reads the biblical narrator's silence as judgment by omission rather than approval.

**Calvin's sermons on 1 Samuel** — preached in Geneva in the 1560s and now available in a recent English translation — give the chapter substantial attention. Calvin reads Saul's death as the executed sentence of God against a king who had been judicially abandoned; the suicide is not justified but is the inevitable consequence of long resistance to God. Calvin is severe but not unsympathetic; he treats Saul as a warning rather than a target. **Luther**'s readings are briefer but converge.

The **critical era** has read the chapter through its tragic and historical dimensions. **Wellhausen** noted the tension between this account and 2 Samuel 1. **Frank Moore Cross** placed it within the Deuteronomistic History's larger judgment-pattern. **McCarter**, **Klein**, **Hertzberg**, and **Tsumura** all treat the chapter at exegetical length. **Halpern** in *David's Secret Demons* and **McKenzie** in *King David: A Biography* are characteristically political: Saul's death is an enabling condition for David's rise, and the apologetic shape of the surrounding chapters ensures that David is not implicated.

The contemporary literary readings are dominated by **David Gunn's *The Fate of King Saul***, which remains, more than four decades after publication, the central scholarly treatment. Gunn argues that Saul is not simply a failed king but a tragic figure trapped by his vocation, abandoned by God in ways the narrator does not fully justify, and dignified in his final act. **J. Cheryl Exum's *Tragedy and Biblical Narrative*** extends and complicates Gunn, locating Saul within a broader biblical tragedy. **Walter Brueggemann** (Interpretation) reads the chapter's silence as a form of compassion. **Robert Polzin** maps the inversions with chapter 17 (David-Goliath) and chapter 30 (David at Ziklag). **Robert Alter** (*The David Story*) calls the chapter "the most restrained tragic close in ancient literature." **Eugene Peterson** (*Leap over a Wall*) reads the chapter pastorally, with a chapter on Saul as the king who could not surrender. **Ellen Davis** stresses the chapter's refusal of editorial cruelty: Saul is given a dignified ending the text could easily have denied him.

## Lens 4 — The Theological Intent

Two tensions structure the chapter, each with multiple defensible readings.

**Tension 1: Is Saul's suicide a moral failure, a justified act of self-protection, or a tragic inevitability?** The text is famously silent. Three positions. (a) **The condemnation reading** — represented in classical form by Augustine and Aquinas, and in Reformed form by Calvin — holds that suicide is intrinsically wrong, that Saul's act compounds his earlier disobedience, and that the narrator's silence is judgment by omission. Steelmanned: the broader biblical canon is consistently against self-killing (cf. the implicit valuation of life in Deuteronomy 30:19); the narrator's refusal to praise Saul is itself a form of distancing. (b) **The justification reading** — represented in the Talmudic tradition (*Bava Kama* 91b), Rashi, and much later Jewish ethics — holds that Saul's suicide was a permitted act of self-protection from torture and ritualized humiliation that would have profaned God's name as well as Saul's body. Steelmanned: the threat of *hit'allelu* (sadistic abuse) is real and explicit; the principle that one may die rather than submit to certain degradations is rabbinically established with Saul as a precedent. (c) **The tragic reading** — represented by Gunn, Exum, Brueggemann, Alter, and most contemporary literary scholarship — holds that the question of moral permissibility is the wrong frame. Saul's suicide is the inevitable terminal point of a vocation he could not bear and from which he could not escape; the narrator's silence is mercy. Steelmanned: the chapter reads, formally, as tragedy; the imposition of legal categories may flatten what the narrative carefully renders as grief.

**Where this leaves us:** the three readings serve different purposes. The condemnation reading preserves a moral structure that other parts of the canon clearly require. The justification reading takes seriously the specific threat Saul faced. The tragic reading honors the chapter's literary form and its refusal of easy judgment. A faithful reading probably needs all three: Saul's act is morally problematic *and* understandable in its specific extremity *and* the terminal point of a vocation that broke him.

**Tension 2: How should we read the men of Jabesh-gilead's retrieval and burning of the bodies?** Israelite practice was burial, not cremation; the burning is anomalous. Three positions. (a) **The desecration-prevention reading** — represented by Hertzberg, McCarter, and most modern commentators — holds that the bodies were burned because they were already mutilated by the Philistines; cremation prevented further desecration if the Philistines should retake the bones. Steelmanned: the verbs cluster around the bodies as having been displayed and desecrated; cremation followed by burial of the bones (the chapter's actual sequence) is a defensible response to a specific dishonor. (b) **The honoring reading** — represented in some patristic and Jewish commentators — holds that the burning was a positive act of honor, like the burning of spices for kings (cf. 2 Chronicles 16:14, Asa's burial); the bones were preserved and properly buried. Steelmanned: the chapter's overall gesture is one of honor for Saul, not condemnation; the cremation should be read in that light. (c) **The pagan-influence reading** — represented by some critical commentators — holds that the burning reflects the trans-Jordanian setting, where Israelite practice may have differed from the western tribes; the men of Jabesh-gilead may have followed a regional custom. Steelmanned: archaeological evidence from trans-Jordan does include some burning practices; the chapter may preserve a regional variant.

**Where this leaves us:** the desecration-prevention reading is the most defensible exegetically. The chapter is making a careful distinction between the burning (functional response to mutilation) and the burial of bones (the actual honor); this is consistent with Israelite practice generally.

**An unresolved question:** what is the theological status of Saul as YHWH's anointed at the moment of his death? The text says he was anointed and not de-anointed; YHWH's spirit had departed (1 Sam 16:14), but the legal-ritual category of *mashiach* was not formally rescinded. David in 2 Samuel 1 will treat Saul as still YHWH's anointed and condemn the Amalekite for claiming to have killed him. The chapter does not theorize this gap; it simply lets it stand.

## The Thread

Three cross-references sharpen the chapter. **1 Samuel 11** — Saul's first major royal act was rescuing Jabesh-gilead from Nahash; his last burial care comes from those he saved. The reign opens and closes at Jabesh-gilead, structurally completing a ring composition that spans twenty chapters. **1 Samuel 17** — the David-Goliath narrative is here inverted: the head and armor that David took from the Philistine champion are taken from Saul by the Philistines and displayed in their temple. **2 Samuel 1** — David's lament for Saul and Jonathan ("the gazelle of Israel is slain on your high places... how the mighty have fallen") theologizes and humanizes what 1 Samuel 31 narrates with restraint; the two chapters are the necessary pair. Flag also **1 Chronicles 10**, which adds the explicit theological gloss the Deuteronomist withheld (Saul died for unfaithfulness, for consulting a medium, for not seeking YHWH); the comparison shows two different editorial ways of handling the same tragic material — the Deuteronomist's silence and the Chronicler's interpretation.
