# Daniel 7: The Son of Man and the Apocalyptic Hinge

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

Daniel 7 is the load-bearing chapter of the Hebrew Bible's apocalyptic tradition and the textual ground on which all subsequent Jewish and Christian eschatology stands. It is dated in the narrative to "the first year of Belshazzar king of Babylon" (7:1) — chronologically prior to chapter 5's Belshazzar narrative, which means the book's redactor has placed the visionary chapters out of historical sequence to mark a structural shift. The Aramaic block (2:4b–7:28) closes with this chapter; the Hebrew resumes at 8:1. The chiastic architecture of the Aramaic block, identified by **Albin Lenglet** in 1972 and accepted by **John J. Collins** (Hermeneia, 1993), **Carol Newsom** (Old Testament Library, 2014), and most subsequent commentators, pairs chapter 2 (the four-metals statue) with chapter 7 (the four beasts) as the framing four-kingdoms visions. The chapter is the Aramaic block's terminus and the apocalyptic block's overture.

The political situation behind the chapter is the question that divides the field. The conservative reading, represented by **Stephen Miller** in the *New American Commentary* (1994), **Joyce Baldwin** in the *Tyndale Old Testament Commentary* (1978), and **Gleason Archer**, places the chapter's composition in the sixth century BC under the historical Daniel, with the four kingdoms identified as Babylon, Medo-Persia, Greece, and Rome — the classical Christian sequence — and the little horn read as a future Antichrist figure with a Roman-period or end-times referent. The critical consensus, established by **Porphyry of Tyre** in the third century AD (preserved in Jerome's commentary as a hostile witness) and consolidated by **S. R. Driver**, **R. H. Charles**, **James Montgomery** (*International Critical Commentary*, 1927), **H. H. Rowley**, and the contemporary scholarship of Collins, Newsom, **Maurice Casey**, **Lester Grabbe**, and **Philip Davies**, places the chapter's redaction in the Maccabean crisis (167–164 BC), with the four kingdoms identified as Babylon, Media, Persia, and Greece, and the little horn identified as Antiochus IV Epiphanes. The two readings produce different theologies, different Christologies, and different doctrines of Scripture.

The Maccabean reading rests on several converging features. The four-kingdoms schema in chapter 2 and chapter 7 requires a Median kingdom between Babylon and Persia — a kingdom that did not historically exist as a successor to Babylon (Cyrus the Persian conquered Babylon and absorbed Media before that). The "Darius the Mede" of chapters 6 and 9 functions to populate this slot. The "ten horns" (7:7) and the little horn that displaces three of them (7:8) match the Seleucid succession reasonably well: ten kings from Seleucus I (Seleucus I, Antiochus I, Antiochus II, Seleucus II, Seleucus III, Antiochus III, Seleucus IV, Demetrius, Heliodorus, and possibly Ptolemy VI as a Seleucid claimant) with Antiochus IV displacing three rivals (his nephew Demetrius, Heliodorus the regent, and Ptolemy VI's claim) on his accession. The "time, times, and half a time" of 7:25 — three and a half years — corresponds closely to the period from the desecration of the Temple (December 167 BC) to its rededication (December 164 BC). The shift from Aramaic (chapters 2–7) to Hebrew (chapters 8–12) and the deepening of apocalyptic specificity in chapters 8 and 11 toward Antiochus IV's career is the redactor's literary signal.

The conservative reading contests these readings point by point. **John C. Whitcomb**, **Donald J. Wiseman**, Miller, and Archer argue that the four kingdoms are Babylon-Medo-Persia-Greece-Rome (treating Medo-Persia as a single empire under Persian rule, as Daniel 8:20 explicitly identifies), the little horn is a future Antichrist figure, and the precision of the prophecies is genuine predictive prophecy. The position has the advantage of taking the text's claim of sixth-century origin at face value; it has the difficulty of explaining why the precision falters after the death of Antiochus IV, since chapter 11's predictive accuracy stops at 11:39 and the apocalyptic timeline of 11:40–45 (Antiochus's death campaign) does not match what historically happened to Antiochus.

The textual situation includes the Aramaic of chapter 7 itself — Imperial Aramaic with Persian and Greek loanwords, a linguistic register that **Klaus Beyer** in *Die aramäischen Texte vom Toten Meer* (1984) and **John Collins** date to the late Persian or early Hellenistic period — and the divergence between the Old Greek (LXX) and Theodotion versions, which is significant for chapters 4–6 and notable but smaller for chapter 7. Eight Daniel manuscripts at Qumran preserve the chapter, with the consonantal text agreeing closely with the Masoretic. The four-beasts schema has Near Eastern parallels — the Akkadian *Erra Epic*, Hesiod's *Works and Days* (the four ages of decline), the Persian Zoroastrian *Bahman Yasht* (a tree with four metallic branches representing four ages), and the apocalyptic literature collected in the **1 Enoch** corpus (the Animal Apocalypse of 1 Enoch 85–90 deploys a similar beasts-and-empires schema). The schema is not Daniel's invention; he received it from a wider Near Eastern apocalyptic stream.

Key terms anchor the chapter. *Bar enash* (בַּר אֱנָשׁ, Aramaic "son of man" / "humanlike one") is the load-bearing phrase. The Aramaic *bar enash* is the standard idiomatic phrase for "human being" (cf. Psalm 8:4's Hebrew *ben adam* / *ben enosh*); its use here is deliberate but not in itself a messianic title — the title-status develops later. *Atiq yomin* (עַתִּיק יוֹמִין, "Ancient of Days") is unique to Daniel 7 in the Hebrew Bible; the iconography of white hair and white clothing draws on Canaanite-Phoenician depictions of El, the senior god of the pantheon. *Qaddishei elyonin* (קַדִּישֵׁי עֶלְיוֹנִין, "saints / holy ones of the Most High," 7:18, 22, 25, 27) is grammatically ambiguous — *elyonin* is a plural form, which can mean "the most high ones" (i.e., heavenly beings) or "of the Most High" (i.e., God's people). The ambiguity is theological. *Iddan iddanin u-pelag iddan* (עִדָּן עִדָּנִין וּפְלַג עִדָּן, "time, times, and half a time," 7:25) becomes the prophetic schema that returns in Revelation 12:14.

## Lens 2 — The Literature

The chapter is **apocalyptic** in the technical genre sense, as defined in the Society of Biblical Literature's 1979 *Semeia 14* volume, *Apocalypse: The Morphology of a Genre*, edited by **John J. Collins** — a narrative framework in which a transcendent reality is revealed through an otherworldly mediator to a human seer, disclosing both temporal (an eschatological future) and spatial (a heavenly world) dimensions. Daniel 7 is the inaugural fully-formed apocalypse in the canonical Hebrew Bible (with seeds in Isaiah 24–27, Ezekiel's chariot vision, and Zechariah). The genre's mature development happens in the Second Temple period — 1 Enoch, 4 Ezra, 2 Baruch, the Apocalypse of Abraham — and Daniel 7 is its anchor text.

The chapter's literary structure is unusually careful. Verses 1–14 are the vision proper, in three movements: the four beasts from the sea (vv. 2–8), the heavenly courtroom of the Ancient of Days (vv. 9–10), the destruction of the fourth beast and the presentation of the son of man (vv. 11–14). Verses 15–28 are the angelic interpretation, in three matching movements: the summary interpretation (vv. 15–18), Daniel's request for further detail on the fourth beast (vv. 19–22), and the expanded interpretation (vv. 23–27), with a closing notation of Daniel's distress (v. 28). The vision and the interpretation mirror each other in a tight literary symmetry that has led some commentators (Davies, Collins) to read them as the work of a single hand rather than as vision plus later commentary.

The shift from Aramaic (chapter 7) to Hebrew (chapter 8) is the chapter's most significant structural marker. The book is bilingual — chapters 1 and 8–12 in Hebrew, chapters 2:4b–7:28 in Aramaic. The Aramaic block contains both court tales (chapters 2–6) and the inaugural apocalypse (chapter 7); the Hebrew portion contains additional apocalypses (chapters 8–12). **The chiastic structure of 2–7** — chapters 2 and 7 paired as four-kingdoms visions, 3 and 6 as deliverance narratives, 4 and 5 as humbled-kings narratives — means that chapter 7 is the chiasm's terminal panel and the apocalyptic block's overture simultaneously. The redactor has deliberately placed it on the seam.

The shift from third-person narration (chapters 1–6) to first-person visionary report (chapters 7–12) is a second structural marker. The book's voice changes at chapter 7. The narrator who described Daniel from outside in chapters 1–6 becomes Daniel reporting from within in 7–12. The narrative form of the court tale gives way to the visionary form of the apocalypse. The shift is the structural argument for treating chapter 7 as a hinge.

The poetic-prose alternation in chapter 7 is also significant. The vision sequences (vv. 9–10, 13–14, 23–27) are in elevated poetic Aramaic, with parallelism, repetition, and hymnic vocabulary; the surrounding narrative is in narrative Aramaic. The use of poetry for the Ancient of Days throne scene and the son of man presentation marks them as the chapter's theological centers. The hymn embedded in 7:14 — "his dominion is an everlasting dominion which shall not pass away, and his kingdom one that shall not be destroyed" — is verbally echoed in Darius's decree at 6:26, sealing the chiastic-thematic continuity of the Aramaic block.

The four-beasts iconography is itself drawn from a wider tradition. The lion with eagles' wings (the first beast, traditionally Babylon) draws on Mesopotamian royal iconography — the *lamassu* winged-bull-lion guardian figures from Assyrian palaces. The bear (Media or Medo-Persia) is the Persian provincial-power animal in Achaemenid iconography. The leopard with four wings and four heads (Greece, with the four heads representing the four Diadochi successor kingdoms) draws on the speed-of-conquest motif. The fourth beast — terrifying, iron-toothed, ten-horned — has no specific iconographic referent and functions as the apocalyptic "beyond-form" of the previous three. Critical scholarship reads this as Greece (specifically the Seleucid kingdom under Antiochus IV); patristic and conservative Christian scholarship reads it as Rome.

Canonically, Daniel sits in the *Ketuvim* (Writings) in the Hebrew Bible, where it functions as wisdom-and-vision literature alongside Esther and Ezra-Nehemiah. Christian Bibles since the Septuagint move it into the prophets after Ezekiel, where it functions as the bridge between the prophetic tradition and the apocalyptic. The textual variants between the Old Greek (LXX) and Theodotion are real but smaller for chapter 7 than for chapters 4–6; both versions preserve the son of man passage with high agreement.

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

This is the chapter that requires the largest canonical-arc reading because it is the load-bearing intertext for the New Testament's Christology and for two thousand years of subsequent eschatology. The chapter establishes three figures that become permanent features of biblical theology: the Ancient of Days (the divine judge), the son of man (the figure given dominion), and the saints of the Most High (the people who possess the kingdom).

The Jewish trajectory is rich. The Animal Apocalypse of **1 Enoch 85–90** (probably second century BC) and the **Similitudes of 1 Enoch 37–71** (probably first century BC to first century AD) develop the four-kingdoms schema and the Son of Man figure independently of and in conversation with Daniel 7. The Similitudes feature an "Elect One" / "Son of Man" who sits on the throne of glory and judges the wicked — a figure with explicit messianic and pre-existent qualities. **Daniel Boyarin**, in *The Jewish Gospels* (2012), argues that the Similitudes and Daniel 7 together preserve a pre-Christian Jewish messianic tradition in which the Son of Man is a divine or semi-divine figure who shares the throne of God — a tradition that the historical Jesus inherited and embodied. **Larry Hurtado**'s *Lord Jesus Christ* (2003) develops a complementary argument from the Christology side. **Maurice Casey**'s *Son of Man* (1979) and *The Solution to the 'Son of Man' Problem* (2007) argue the opposite — that *bar enash* in Aramaic is a generic phrase for "human being / one in my position" and that the Daniel 7 figure is a collective representation of the saints / Israel rather than an individual messiah. Casey's reading dominated critical scholarship from 1979 through roughly 2010; Boyarin's reading has been gaining ground since.

**4 Ezra 11–13** (late first century AD) explicitly reinterprets Daniel 7's fourth beast as Rome (the eagle vision) and develops a Son of Man figure who comes from the sea. **2 Baruch 36–40** (early second century AD) does similar work. **Josephus**, *Antiquities* 10.10–11, paraphrases Daniel and reads the fourth kingdom as Rome — a Roman-Jewish reading from a writer with Flavian sponsorship. The Babylonian Talmud, *Sanhedrin* 98a, reads the son of man passage messianically; *Hagigah* 14a debates whether the "thrones" (plural) of 7:9 imply a second divine figure (the rabbi Akiva is reported to have suggested David, with sharp rebuke from R. Yose). **Saadia Gaon**'s tenth-century commentary, **Rashi**, **Ibn Ezra**, and **Don Isaac Abrabanel** read the chapter messianically, with the four kingdoms as Babylon, Persia, Greece, and Rome (not Edom — though some medievals identified the fourth as Edom = Christianity, a polemical redirection). Modern Jewish scholarship — **Robert Gordis**, **Aaron Koller**, **Daniel Boyarin** — splits between collective and individual readings of the son of man.

The Christian trajectory begins with the New Testament itself. **Mark 14:62** — Jesus's reply at his trial before the Sanhedrin: "I am, and you will see the son of man seated at the right hand of Power and coming with the clouds of heaven." This is direct citation of Daniel 7:13 conflated with Psalm 110:1, and it is the moment in Mark's gospel where Jesus's identity is most explicit. The Synoptic tradition's frequent use of "the son of man" for Jesus's self-designation (some 80 occurrences in the Synoptics, almost always on Jesus's lips) is the New Testament's most distinctive Christological category. **Mark 13:26** and parallels — the Olivet Discourse — apply Daniel 7's son of man coming on clouds to Jesus's parousia. **Revelation 1:7, 13** — "one like a son of man" with white hair (a conflation with the Ancient of Days, theologically significant) — develops Daniel 7 in apocalyptic mode. **Acts 7:55–56** — Stephen's vision of the son of man standing at the right hand of God — is the chapter's earliest Christian prayer-application.

**Hippolytus of Rome's *Commentary on Daniel*** (c. 200 AD), the earliest extant Christian biblical commentary, reads the four kingdoms as Babylon, Persia, Greece, and Rome and the little horn as the future Antichrist figure of the eschaton — establishing the patristic-Christian template. **Jerome's *Commentary on Daniel*** (407 AD), composed against Porphyry, defends the chapter's historicity, identifies the fourth beast as Rome, and reads the son of man as Christ. **Theodoret of Cyrus** in the fifth century follows Jerome. The medieval reception is enormous — **Joachim of Fiore**'s twelfth-century apocalyptic schema reads the chapter through three ages (Father, Son, Spirit) and influences Franciscan eschatology and later millenarian traditions; **Aquinas** in his *Commentary on Daniel* and the *Glossa Ordinaria* read the son of man as Christ in his divine and human natures. **Luther's *Preface to Daniel*** (1530) identifies the Pope as the little horn — the inaugural Protestant identification that becomes a Reformed tradition. **Calvin's *Lectures on Daniel*** (1561, two volumes) is exegetically careful, identifies the fourth beast as Rome, reads the son of man as Christ, and is more measured about the Pope-as-little-horn identification.

The critical trajectory begins with **Porphyry of Tyre** (preserved in Jerome) and runs through **Bertholdt** (1806), **S. R. Driver**, **R. H. Charles**'s *Critical and Exegetical Commentary on Daniel* (1929), **James Montgomery**'s *International Critical Commentary* (1927), **H. H. Rowley**, and into the contemporary consensus represented by Collins, Newsom, Casey, Davies, and Grabbe. The contemporary critical reading: four kingdoms = Babylon, Media, Persia, Greece; little horn = Antiochus IV Epiphanes; son of man = collective representation of the saints (Casey) or angelic figure (Michael, per Collins) representing Israel; the chapter's composition = Maccabean redaction of earlier traditions.

Where the trajectories diverge most sharply is on the son of man. The collective reading (Casey, Newsom, Davies) treats the figure as a corporate symbol of "the saints of the Most High" / Israel — supported by the angelic interpretation in 7:18, 22, 27 which assigns to the saints what is given to the son of man in 7:14. The angelic-individual reading (Collins) treats the figure as the archangel Michael, the heavenly representative of Israel, who returns explicitly in chapters 10 and 12. The messianic reading (Boyarin, Hurtado, the New Testament, the patristic tradition) treats the figure as a divine or semi-divine messianic individual whose vindication includes the saints. The both-and reading (**N. T. Wright**, *Jesus and the Victory of God*, 1996) treats the figure as the messianic individual who represents and embodies the saints — the figure is not either-or but both. Jewish-Christian dialogue on Daniel 7 has been more productive in the last twenty years (Boyarin, **Peter Schäfer**, **Israel Knohl**) than in any period since late antiquity.

## Lens 4 — The Theological Intent

The first tension is the identity of the son of man, which is the chapter's load-bearing theological question. The collective reading (Casey, Newsom, Davies) has the strength of the immediate context — the angel's interpretation explicitly transfers to "the saints of the Most High" (7:18, 22, 27) what is given to the son of man (7:14). The reading is exegetically tight and historically conservative in the technical sense (it asks what the original author / redactor meant in his Maccabean context). It has the weakness of not adequately accounting for the *singular* presentation of the figure in 7:13–14, where one humanlike figure (not many saints) approaches the Ancient of Days and is enthroned.

The angelic reading (Collins) treats the figure as the archangel Michael, who appears explicitly in Daniel 10:13, 21 and 12:1 as the heavenly representative of Israel. The reading has the strength of identifying a specific singular figure who is consistent with Daniel's broader angelology and who would have been recognizable in Second Temple Judaism. It has the weakness of requiring the reader to import a figure not named in chapter 7.

The messianic reading (Boyarin, Hurtado, New Testament tradition) treats the figure as a divine or semi-divine messianic individual who shares God's throne and authority. The reading has the strength of accounting for the singular presentation, the throne-sharing implication of the "thrones" in 7:9 (plural), the parallel development in 1 Enoch's Similitudes, and the interpretive history of the chapter both Jewish and Christian. It has the weakness of arguably reading later developments back into the original context — though Boyarin's argument is precisely that this messianic-divine tradition is *not* later but pre-existed the New Testament.

The both-and reading (Wright) treats the son of man as the messianic individual who represents and embodies the saints, with the saints' inheritance flowing through and from his enthronement. The reading has the strength of preserving both the singular vision and the collective interpretation; it has the weakness of being theologically maximalist in a way that some critical scholars resist as anachronistic harmonization.

Steelman the Casey collective reading. *Bar enash* in Aramaic is genuinely the standard idiomatic phrase for "human being" — its use here, opposed to the four bestial figures, signals "humanlike" or "as one of us" rather than a fixed messianic title. The angel's interpretation in 7:18 and 7:27 explicitly transfers to "the saints" what is given to the son of man — this is the chapter's own self-interpretation, and it explicitly identifies the figure with the people. The reading respects the chapter's compositional integrity in its Maccabean context, where the audience suffering under Antiochus IV needed to hear that vindication was coming for them, not for a future divine messiah. The reading was the modern critical consensus for thirty years for good reasons. Where this leaves us: the chapter's singular figure may be primarily a corporate symbol whose later development into a messianic individual is a separate stage — but the singular presentation is the seed from which that development grows.

The Catholic, Orthodox, and traditional Protestant readings all converge on the messianic-Christological reading; the Reformed traditions have historically located the chapter in the second-coming framework. The Anabaptist tradition (Yoder, *The Politics of Jesus*) has read the chapter as the heavenly vindication of nonviolent witness. Jewish readings split between collective, angelic, and messianic; Boyarin's recent work has been an important opening between Jewish and Christian readings.

The second tension is the dating problem and what it does to Christology. If the chapter was composed as Maccabean *vaticinium ex eventu* (prophecy after the fact, with the little horn as Antiochus IV), then its theological trajectory is closed within Antiochus's defeat and the Maccabean restoration of the Temple — and the New Testament's application to Jesus is a later typological extension, not the chapter's original intent. If the chapter was composed in the sixth century as genuine predictive prophecy, then the little horn points beyond Antiochus to a future Antichrist and the New Testament's application to Jesus is the chapter's intended fulfillment.

The Catholic position, articulated in Vatican II's *Dei Verbum* (1965) and in the Pontifical Biblical Commission's *Interpretation of the Bible in the Church* (1993), holds that the historical-critical method is essential but that the canonical-Christological reading is the church's authoritative interpretation — both can be true at different levels (the Maccabean original sense and the messianic-Christological canonical sense). The Eastern Orthodox tradition, expressed by **Father John Behr** and **Andrew Louth**, similarly distinguishes the historical *peshat* from the spiritual *theoria* and treats both as legitimate. Protestant traditions split: conservative evangelicals (Miller, Archer, the Chicago Statement on Biblical Inerrancy 1978) treat the predictive-prophecy reading as the only theologically acceptable option; mainline Protestant scholarship (Childs's canonical approach, Wright's both-and approach) treats the historical-critical reading as compatible with the Christological canonical reading. Jewish traditions have not had to resolve the question because they do not require Christological fulfillment.

Steelman the conservative position. Genuine predictive prophecy is not impossible if Yahweh is the God Daniel claims; the precision of the prophecies is exactly what one would expect from such a God; the New Testament's confidence that the chapter speaks of Christ is the deepest reading; and the Maccabean redactor hypothesis requires assuming what it tries to prove (that miraculous prediction is impossible). Where this leaves us: the chapter forces a theological decision about the kind of book one reads — and that decision is downstream of one's theology of revelation, not a neutral exegetical matter.

N. T. Wright's "already / not yet" reading offers a third path: the chapter's apocalyptic vision was inaugurally fulfilled in the historical Jesus's enthronement (resurrection and ascension), is currently being worked out in the church's witness, and will be consummated in the second coming. The reading does not require choosing between Maccabean and Christological referents because it locates the fulfillment in a structure that includes both Antiochus's defeat (a type) and Christ's enthronement (the fulfillment) and the parousia (the consummation).

The unresolved question. The son of man is presented; the saints possess the kingdom; the fourth beast is destroyed; the dominion is everlasting. And yet the empires kept coming — Rome after the Maccabees, the medieval succession after Rome, modern empires after that. If the chapter's vision is a closed Maccabean polemic, why has it kept being read into history's openings? If the chapter's vision is genuine prophecy, why has its consummation kept receding? Either way, the chapter has outlived its referents. The vision keeps doing work the text alone cannot account for.

## The Thread

The first cross-reference is **Mark 14:62** — Jesus before the Sanhedrin: "you will see the Son of Man seated at the right hand of Power and coming with the clouds of heaven." This is direct citation of Daniel 7:13 conflated with Psalm 110:1, and it is the moment in Mark's gospel where Jesus's identity is most explicit. The high priest tears his clothes and pronounces blasphemy. Whatever the original sense of Daniel 7's son of man, Jesus is claiming to be that figure — and his Sanhedrin trial turns on that claim. The intertext is the New Testament's most dense Christological moment.

The second is **Revelation 1:13–18** — the Patmos vision of "one like a son of man" with hair white as wool. John of Patmos has deliberately conflated Daniel 7:13's son of man with Daniel 7:9's Ancient of Days — the figures are merged into one. The merging is theologically extraordinary: it identifies the son of man with the divine throne in a way that requires either Boyarin's pre-existent messianic tradition or the New Testament's high Christology to make sense. Revelation reads Daniel 7 as already collapsed into a single divine figure.

The third is **1 Enoch 46:1–4** — the Similitudes' Son of Man, who has "the appearance of a man" and whose "face was full of graciousness, like one of the holy angels," and who "will reveal all the treasures of that which is hidden." 1 Enoch develops the Daniel 7 son of man into a more explicitly messianic, possibly pre-existent, figure who shares the throne. Whether the Similitudes are pre-Christian (Boyarin, Knohl) or contemporaneous (some recent dating) determines whether the New Testament inherited a pre-existing tradition or developed its Son of Man Christology de novo. The intertextual stakes are enormous.

The fourth is **Mark 13:14** — "the abomination of desolation … standing where it ought not be" — Jesus's apocalyptic discourse on the Mount of Olives applies Daniel's prophetic-apocalyptic vocabulary directly to the historical fall of Jerusalem in AD 70. The intertext is the chapter's typological-historical pattern: the abomination that desolated the Temple under Antiochus is the type of the desolation that would come under Rome, which is the type of the eschatological consummation. Daniel 7 is the chapter on which this typological structure rests.
