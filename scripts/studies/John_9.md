# John 9: The Man Born Blind and the Birth of *Aposynagōgos*

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

John 9 is, by widespread scholarly consensus, the single most historically transparent chapter in the Fourth Gospel — not because its surface narrative is more "factual" than other chapters, but because its narrative seams expose the late-first-century community that produced it. J. Louis Martyn's *History and Theology in the Fourth Gospel* (1968), still the most influential single monograph on John, took 9:22 as its Archimedean point. The verse — "the Jews had already agreed that if anyone should confess Jesus to be Christ, he was to be put out of the synagogue" — uses the technical Greek term *aposynagōgos* (ἀποσυνάγωγος, "expelled-from-synagogue"), a word found nowhere in extant Greek literature outside the Fourth Gospel (also at 12:42 and 16:2). Martyn argued that *aposynagōgos* is anachronistic on the lips of Jesus's contemporaries (no formal synagogue-expulsion mechanism existed in 30 CE for confessing a messianic claimant) but coheres precisely with the post-70 reorganization of Pharisaic-rabbinic Judaism at Yavneh, when the *birkat ha-minim* — the twelfth benediction of the *Amidah* cursing the *minim* (heretics) — was reformulated, traditionally credited to Samuel the Small under Rabban Gamaliel II around 80–90 CE.

Martyn's two-level reading proposed that John 9 narrates simultaneously a healing in Jerusalem c. 30 CE and the trauma of a Johannine community c. 85–95 CE whose members were being interrogated by synagogue authorities and excluded from Israel's worshipping life for confessing Jesus as Messiah. Subsequent scholarship has substantially complicated Martyn's reconstruction. Adele Reinhartz's *Cast Out of the Covenant* (2018) argues the *birkat ha-minim* hypothesis has been over-pressed: Steven Katz, Daniel Boyarin (*Border Lines*), and Ruth Langer have shown the malediction was not formally directed at Christians until much later, and the "parting of the ways" was geographically uneven and chronologically protracted. Yet even Reinhartz concedes that John 9 reflects a community in painful negotiation with Jewish identity, whatever the precise institutional mechanism. Raymond Brown's *Community of the Beloved Disciple* (1979) reconstructed a multi-stage Johannine community history from this material, and Wayne Meeks's "The Man from Heaven in Johannine Sectarianism" (1972) located the chapter's dualistic rhetoric in a sectarian community drawing strict in-group boundaries.

The chapter's archaeological grounding strengthened dramatically in 2004 when Ronny Reich and Eli Shukron, working on a sewer-line repair in the City of David, uncovered the actual first-century *Pool of Siloam* (Hebrew *Shiloach*, "sent"). The Byzantine-era pool that pilgrims had identified for centuries, located near the traditional terminus of Hezekiah's tunnel, turned out to be the wrong site. The genuine first-century pool — a monumental stepped reservoir roughly 50 meters on its longest axis — sits about 70 meters southeast and was clearly used for ritual immersion (*tevilah*) for pilgrims ascending to the Temple. The Johannine etymology, "Siloam (which means Sent)," puns on *shiloach* (the verbal root *š-l-ḥ*, "to send") and Jesus as the *apestalmenos* — the one sent from the Father, a verb that recurs more than forty times across the Gospel. The blind man washes in the Pool of the Sent and returns *blepōn* (βλέπων, "seeing") — the geography is theology.

A few Greek terms anchor the chapter. *Tuphlos ek genetēs* (τυφλὸς ἐκ γενετῆς, "blind from birth," 9:1) signals a congenital condition no first-century rabbinic source thought reversible — Origen and Chrysostom both note this is a sign exceeding all prophetic precedent. *Proskuneō* (προσκυνέω, "worshiped," 9:38) — used of the healed man's response to Jesus — is the same verb the Gospel reserves for true worship of the Father (4:23–24); Köstenberger and Brown both flag the verse as one of the Gospel's most explicit Christological worship-acts. *Aposynagōgos*, already discussed, is the keyword. The disciples' question, "Rabbi, who sinned, this man or his parents?" (9:2) presupposes the rabbinic discussion of pre-natal sin (b. Sanhedrin 91b debates whether the fetus can sin in the womb) and inherited guilt across generations (Exodus 20:5; Ezekiel 18 contests). And *ergazomai* (ἐργάζομαι, "to work," 9:4) carries the Sabbath-controversy charge: Jesus heals on the Sabbath (9:14), and "making mud" was specifically itemized in m. Shabbat 7:2 as one of the thirty-nine forbidden labors.

## Lens 2 — The Literature

John 9 is the **sixth of seven signs** in the Fourth Gospel's so-called Book of Signs (chs 1–12), and it is by some margin the most extended healing narrative in any Gospel. Mark dispatches Bartimaeus in seven verses; John gives the man born blind forty-one. Culpepper's *Anatomy of the Fourth Gospel* (1983) showed that the chapter is a meticulously crafted seven-scene drama — (1) Jesus and disciples; (2) Jesus and the man; (3) the man and his neighbors; (4) the man and the Pharisees, first interrogation; (5) the parents and the Pharisees; (6) the man and the Pharisees, second interrogation, ending in expulsion; (7) Jesus and the man, with Pharisees overhearing. The structure mirrors the trial cycle of a forensic *agōn* (ἀγών), and the chapter as a whole stages Jesus's *and* the healed man's interlocking trials before the world.

The most striking literary achievement is the protagonist's progressive Christological perception, which moves in measured steps: "the man called Jesus" (9:11) → "He is a prophet" (9:17) → "If this man were not from God, he could do nothing" (9:33) → "Lord, I believe" (9:38). Brown's Anchor Bible commentary observes that this is the most carefully calibrated character development in the Gospel, perhaps in any New Testament narrative. As the man's vision sharpens, the Pharisees move in the opposite direction — beginning with theological certainty ("we know that this man is a sinner," 9:24) and ending in the irony of self-imposed blindness ("now that you say, 'We see,' your guilt remains," 9:41). The seeing become blind; the blind see. Marianne Meye Thompson (NTL) calls this Johannine irony in its most compressed and devastating form.

The chapter pivots on the **fourth I AM saying with predicate**, "I am the light of the world" (9:5; first stated at 8:12), now dramatized rather than declared. It links to the **seventh I AM** that climaxes the public ministry — "I am the resurrection and the life" in chapter 11 — by establishing the pattern that signs *enact* the predicates. Textual variants are minimal: 9:35's "Son of Man" (P66, P75, ℵ, B, D) is firm against the Byzantine "Son of God" (A, K, Δ); the harder reading and earlier witnesses both favor *huios tou anthrōpou*. Bruce Metzger's *Textual Commentary* judges the change a later Christological harmonization, and almost all modern critical editions follow the earlier reading.

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

The canonical arc reaches back to Isaiah 35:5 ("Then the eyes of the blind shall be opened") and Isaiah 42:7 (the Servant who opens blind eyes), texts the early church read as messianic. It reaches forward to Acts 26:18 (Paul's commission to "open their eyes") and to Revelation 3:18 (the Laodicean church told to "buy from me … salve to anoint your eyes, so that you may see"). The motif of pre-natal sin links backward to the disciples' question and forward to the Augustinian doctrine of original guilt — though Jesus's reply (9:3) is closer to a Jobian refusal of theodicy than to either Pelagius's denial of inherited sin or Augustine's affirmation of it.

**Jewish interpretive trajectories.** Daniel Boyarin's *The Jewish Gospels* (2012) and *Border Lines* (2004) frame the Johannine community not as outside Judaism but as one Jewish messianic faction among many in late-Second-Temple and early rabbinic period diversity; the *aposynagōgos* of John 9 is, on Boyarin's reading, an internal Jewish quarrel that hardened only retrospectively. Adele Reinhartz, especially in *Befriending the Beloved Disciple* (2001) and *Cast Out of the Covenant* (2018), has been the sharpest contemporary critic of the Martyn paradigm, warning that uncritical use of "the Jews" (*hoi Ioudaioi*) in John has enabled centuries of Christian anti-Judaism — a warning the post-Holocaust Christian academy has had to reckon with seriously since *Nostra Aetate* (1965). Amy-Jill Levine's *The Misunderstood Jew* (2006) rebuilds the rabbinic background to disability discourse and shows how rabbinic sources are not simply "the negative foil" John imagines. John Ashton's *Understanding the Fourth Gospel* (1991, rev. 2007) reads John as a "two-level drama" but resists Martyn's institutional precision. Charlesworth's *John and Qumran* (1972) and the Dead Sea Scrolls' *Community Rule* (1QS 3:13–4:26) provide the closest extant Jewish parallels to John's light-darkness dualism.

**Christian interpretive trajectories.** Origen's *Commentary on John* reads the mud-and-spittle as figural — the Word touching dust to remake humanity, a re-Genesis. Augustine's *Tractates on the Gospel of John* (Tractate 44) makes the man-born-blind a figure of the human race born blind in Adam, re-created by the second Adam who is himself the *Lux mundi*. Chrysostom's *Eighty-Eight Homilies on John* (Homilies 56–59) emphasizes the man's growing courage (*parrhēsia*) before authority — the lay theologian outwitting the professional. Cyril of Alexandria reads the chapter Christologically as the Logos restoring the *eikōn* defaced in the Fall. Aquinas's *Lectura super Joannem* (cap. IX) integrates the patristic figural reading with a careful Aristotelian account of how clay-and-saliva can be efficient cause without being natural cause. Calvin's *Commentary on John* uses 9:3 ("that the works of God might be displayed in him") to argue against eudaemonist theodicy: God's purposes are not always retributive.

Twentieth-century criticism reshaped the field. Bultmann's *Gospel of John* (1941, ET 1971) treated the chapter as a window onto the existential crisis of decision before the Revealer. Käsemann's *Testament of Jesus* (1968) read it for its sectarian theology. Brown's *Anchor Bible* (1966–70) and *Community of the Beloved Disciple* (1979) made the chapter the keystone of community-history reconstruction. Schnackenburg's *HthKNT* commentary developed the trial motif. Barrett's *Critical and Exegetical Commentary* (1955, rev. 1978) anchored the philological work. C.K. Barrett, with characteristic dryness, called 9:22 "the most important historical sentence in the Gospel."

Contemporary commentary has continued to mine the chapter. Köstenberger's *BECNT* (2004) gives the most thorough modern philological defense of Johannine historicity. Carson's *PNTC* (1991) is the standard evangelical reading. Craig Keener's two-volume *John* (2003) marshals an enormous Greco-Roman and rabbinic comparative apparatus. Beasley-Murray's *WBC* (1987) is balanced and exegetically careful. Marianne Meye Thompson's *NTL* (2015) reads the chapter through its theological imagery of light. Francis Moloney's *Sacra Pagina* (1998) develops the literary-rhetorical structure. Gail O'Day's *NIB* essay attends to the marginalized man's growing voice. Lesslie Newbigin's *The Light Has Come* (1982) reads it pastorally as a treatise on what it costs to confess Christ in a hostile age — Newbigin writing from his missionary experience in India.

**Disability theology** is a recent and crucial trajectory. Jeremy Schipper's *Disability Studies and the Hebrew Bible* (2006) and Saul Olyan's *Disability in the Hebrew Bible* (2008) showed that the disciples' question (9:2) reflects an entrenched ancient near-eastern association of disability with divine punishment, an association rabbinic literature contested but did not eliminate. Amos Yong's *The Bible, Disability, and the Church* (2011), written from a Pentecostal disability-theologian standpoint, presses Jesus's reply: the man's blindness is *not* punishment, but neither is the healing the only meaningful theological resolution; Yong critiques cure-centric readings that erase the ongoing dignity of disabled lives. Nancy Eiesland's *The Disabled God* (1994) is the foundational text. Sandra Schneiders's *Written That You May Believe* (1999, 2003) reads the chapter as a paradigm of how women, the disabled, the unlettered, and other marginalized figures in John achieve Christological clarity that the religious establishment misses.

## Lens 4 — The Theological Intent

**Tension 1: What does Jesus's "neither this man nor his parents sinned, but that the works of God might be displayed in him" (9:3) say about disability and theodicy?**

Position A — Augustinian / Calvinist instrumental reading: the man's blindness is providentially ordered for the eventual revelation of God's glory; suffering has a hidden teleology. Calvin defends this on 9:3 against any view that would impugn divine justice; suffering serves a larger divine pedagogy. Position B — Pelagian / free-will reading: Jesus is denying any *causal* link between specific sins and specific sufferings, refusing the disciples' theodicy and leaving the question of why this man is blind unanswered; the chapter teaches us *not* to read disability as moral verdict. Position C — Disability-theology critique (Yong, Eiesland, Schipper): the cure resolves the *narrative* problem but not the *theological* one; the man's blindness was always already a theologically meaningful state, not a defect awaiting erasure. The Augustinian reading risks instrumentalizing disabled persons as occasions for divine display; the Pelagian reading risks leaving the suffering meaningless; the disability-theology reading risks evacuating the miracle of its concrete healing claim.

Where this leaves us: 9:3 is best read with the Greek *hina* clause taken as result rather than purpose ("but [the result is] that the works of God might be displayed") — a grammatical reading defended by Carson and Köstenberger that softens the instrumentalist edge. The chapter refuses retributive theodicy without requiring us to believe that the man's blindness was *for* the display; rather, the divine response *to* his blindness becomes the display. Yong's caution remains: Jesus heals one blind man on one Sabbath; the gospel cannot promise the universal cure of disability, and the Church's theological imagination must be capacious enough to include un-cured disabled bodies in its account of the *imago Dei*.

**Tension 2: How are we to read "the Jews" in John 9 after the Holocaust?**

Position A — Traditional / patristic supersessionist reading: "the Jews" in John names the religious establishment that rejected its own Messiah, and the Gospel is a clear-eyed account of that rejection; this reading underwrote centuries of Christian anti-Judaism, from Chrysostom's *Adversus Judaeos* sermons to medieval blood libels. Position B — Post-Holocaust corrective reading (Reinhartz, Levine, Brown's later work, Vatican II *Nostra Aetate* §4): "the Jews" in John is a complex narrative term whose meaning shifts contextually (sometimes "Judeans," sometimes "religious authorities," sometimes "those who oppose Jesus"); flat translation as "the Jews" has been catastrophic for Jewish-Christian relations and must be revised. Position C — Boyarin's intra-Jewish reading: the conflict is not "Christians vs. Jews" but a quarrel internal to first-century Jewish messianism, frozen retrospectively into the binary by later parting-of-the-ways narratives; the Gospel does not yet know it is a "Christian" book opposed to a "Jewish" one.

Where this leaves us: any responsible Christian reading of John 9 must take the post-Holocaust correction with full seriousness. Reinhartz's hermeneutical demand — that we read John "with eyes wide open" to its potential for harm — is non-negotiable. Yet Boyarin's framing recovers something the Vatican II rereading sometimes obscured: this was *also* a Jewish text, written by Jews about a Jewish Messiah for a community most of whom were ethnically and religiously Jewish, in painful negotiation with other Jews. The Pharisees in 9:13–34 are not "the Jews" as a people but a particular group of religious authorities engaged in an intra-Jewish hermeneutical fight. Translation, preaching, and pedagogy must reflect this. *Nostra Aetate*'s framing — that the Jewish people cannot be charged collectively with Christ's death — is the floor, not the ceiling.

One unresolved question: was there an actual community in Asia Minor c. 90 CE whose members were being formally expelled from synagogues? Martyn's reconstruction has been weakened by Reinhartz, Boyarin, and Katz, but no successor model has fully replaced it. The *aposynagōgos* triad (9:22, 12:42, 16:2) remains the strongest single piece of evidence for some such expulsion mechanism, but its institutional precision and geographical scope are genuinely uncertain.

## The Thread

The chapter looks back to Isaiah 35:5 and 42:7 (the messianic opening of blind eyes) and to John 1:9 ("the true light, which gives light to everyone, was coming into the world"). It looks forward to John 11:25 ("I am the resurrection and the life") — the seventh and climactic sign that will cap what John 9 begins, and to John 16:2 ("they will put you out of the synagogues") where the *aposynagōgos* term recurs and the community-historical situation becomes most explicit. Cross-reference also Matthew 11:5 (Jesus's response to John the Baptist citing the Isaianic-messianic catalog of healings, with the blind seeing) — a synoptic parallel that confirms early Christian recognition of blindness-healing as messianic credential. Acts 9 (Paul's blinding-and-restoration) deliberately inverts the Johannine pattern: the religious authority is blinded so that he might come to true sight, completing the chapter's irony at the level of the canon.
