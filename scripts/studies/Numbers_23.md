# Numbers 23: How Can I Curse What God Has Not Cursed?

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

Numbers 23 takes place on two successive Transjordan heights: Bamoth-baal ("high places of Baal," from the end of ch. 22), the field of Zophim on the top of Pisgah, and ultimately the top of Peor (v. 28, setting up ch. 25). These are real cultic sites. Pisgah (*pisgâ*, פִּסְגָּה) is widely identified with the ridge on Mount Nebo; the phrase *śĕdê ṣōpîm* ("field of Zophim" — "watchers/seers") may preserve the name of a professional class of ritual observers. Khirbet al-Mukhayyat on Nebo's flank has yielded Iron Age remains, and the Madaba Plains regional survey (Lawrence Geraty, Larry Herr) has documented Late Bronze/early Iron continuity across the Moabite plateau. Peor is generally associated with the headland northeast of the Dead Sea, and Baal-Peor — the specific cult version of Baal that will destroy Israel in ch. 25 — was one of a number of regional Baalim (compare Baal-Meon, Baal-Hazor, Baal-Gad) that differentiated the high god by locale.

The ritual apparatus Balaam directs is unmistakably ANE cultic divination. "Build for me here seven altars, and prepare for me here seven bulls and seven rams" (v. 1). The number seven (*šebaʿ*) is the sacred number across the Levant, as a glance at the Ugaritic Baal cycle shows — seven lightnings, seven years, seven daughters. The combination of seven altars with paired bull-and-ram offerings is closest in the archaeological record to *bārû*-type divination: Assyrian and Babylonian ritual texts describe multi-altar sacrificial complexes at which the diviner observes omens and awaits divine speech. Jo Ann Hackett's work on Deir ʿAlla and Meindert Dijkstra's studies of Northwest Semitic divination both note that the technical vocabulary of this chapter — *niḥuš*, *qesem* — is the standard ANE diviner's kit.

Three Hebrew terms carry particular weight in this chapter. First, *māšāl* (מָשָׁל), translated "discourse" or "oracle" (v. 7, v. 18), is the word for a weighty poetic utterance — used also of Job's discourses, of the prophets' oracles, of wisdom sayings. It indicates the solemn register of what follows. Second, *ʿam lĕbādād yiškōn*, "a people dwelling alone" (v. 9), deploys *bādād* — "separate, isolated" — a term used elsewhere of leprous exclusion but here turned to positive theological meaning. Third, *lōʾ-naḥaš bĕyaʿăqōb wĕlōʾ-qesem bĕyiśrāʾēl* (v. 23), "there is no enchantment against Jacob, no divination against Israel," puts the two key pagan divinatory terms into the mouth of the most famous pagan diviner in the Bible, as he declares his own craft powerless against Israel. The irony is crushing.

Archaeologically, the importance of this chapter remains the **Deir ʿAlla inscription** and what it tells us about the Transjordan context. Hoftijzer and Jo Ann Hackett's reconstruction of Combination I shows Balaam receiving a night vision from the council of the *šdyn* (gods); the plaster text mentions the "seers" who gather and receive the message. The parallel to Numbers 23 is striking: in both, Balaam withdraws, receives a divine word during the night or after ritual preparation, and reports it to a waiting audience. The Deir ʿAlla Balaam is a known type; the biblical Balaam is that type repurposed.

Balak's maneuver across multiple ritual sites reflects a common ANE theology of territorial deities. If one altar does not work, try another mountain — perhaps the god is responsive at a different cultic locale. Baruch Levine's AB commentary emphasizes this: Balak's movement from Bamoth-baal to Pisgah to Peor is not random but reflects a systematic approach to getting the god to respond. Milgrom's JPS commentary adds that the unchanging divine word *regardless* of altar site is itself a polemic against local-deity theology: YHWH is not bound to the site.

## Lens 2 — The Literature

Numbers 23 contains the first two of Balaam's four great oracles. The prose framing (vv. 1-6, 11-17, 25-30) is terse and functional; the oracles themselves are sustained Hebrew verse in a demonstrably archaic register.

The linguistic argument for the antiquity of the oracles has been made most rigorously by William Foxwell Albright (*Yahweh and the Gods of Canaan*, 1968), Frank Moore Cross (*Canaanite Myth and Hebrew Epic*, 1973), David Noel Freedman (*Pottery, Poetry, and Prophecy*), and Baruch Levine (*Numbers 21-36*, Anchor Bible, 2000). Features they identify include: the archaic *we-* conjunction in its older semantic range, retained case-vowel endings on some nouns, the archaic energic *nun* on imperfect verbs, rare vocabulary with cognates in Ugaritic (e.g., *bin-nūn* morphology), prosodic patterns closer to Ugaritic than to later biblical poetry, and the absence of certain late grammatical innovations. Their conclusion: the oracles can plausibly be dated to the 12th-10th centuries, making them among the oldest preserved Hebrew poems alongside the Song of the Sea (Exod 15), the Song of Deborah (Judg 5), and parts of Deut 32-33.

The first oracle (vv. 7-10) opens with a geographic reminder — "from Aram Balak has brought me, from the eastern mountains" — locating Balaam's origin in upper Mesopotamia. It rises through parallel rhetorical questions ("How can I curse whom God has not cursed?") to the theme of Israel's separateness: "a people dwelling alone, and not counting itself among the nations" (v. 9b). The oracle's climax is the paradoxical longing of the hostile seer for the destiny of the people he has been paid to curse: "Let me die the death of the upright, and let my end be like his" (v. 10b). The Hebrew *môt yĕšārîm* is ambiguous: "upright" may mean Jeshurun, a poetic name for Israel (so Ibn Ezra, Levine).

The second oracle (vv. 18-24) opens with a formal summons — "Rise, Balak, and hear; give ear to me, O son of Zippor" — the prophetic call-to-attention formula. It then articulates the non-volatility of the divine will: "God is not man, that he should lie, or a son of man, that he should change his mind" (v. 19). The verse uses two Hebrew verbs — *kāzab* (lie) and *niḥam* (change mind, relent) — that are elsewhere used of God; this is not a flat statement of immutability but a promise that the oracular word just given will not be rescinded. The oracle moves to Israel's security ("the LORD their God is with them, and the shout of a king is among them," v. 21 — some scholars hear a kingship note anticipating monarchy), to the Exodus tradition ("God brings them out of Egypt and is for them like the horns of the wild ox," v. 22), to the climactic denial of divinatory power against Israel ("there is no enchantment against Jacob, no divination against Israel… What has God wrought!", v. 23), and ends with the leonine image: "Behold, a people! As a lioness it rises up, and as a lion it lifts itself" (v. 24).

The two oracles form a literary diptych. The first begins in Balaam's geography and ends in Balaam's longed-for death; the second begins in Balak's geography and ends in Israel's predatory power. Each contains about forty words in Hebrew, paralleled across themes. Literary structuralists (Robert Alter, Jacob Licht, Yair Zakovitch) have noted the patterning as deliberate ascending climax: the third and fourth oracles in chapter 24 will complete the quadriptych.

Textually, the oracles are preserved with high stability across the MT, the Samaritan Pentateuch, and the Qumran fragments (4QNum-b). The LXX occasionally smooths: at v. 21, the MT's *lōʾ-hibbîṭ ʾāwen* ("he has not beheld trouble/iniquity") the LXX renders with *ouk estai mochthos* ("there will be no trouble"), losing the divine-observer nuance. At v. 22 the LXX singularizes "God brings *them*" to "he brings *him*" (Jacob). These are interpretive moves more than text-critical ones.

Genre-wise, the oracles function as prophetic *māšāl* — weighted poetic utterances — with strong formal affinity to the Blessings of Jacob (Gen 49) and the Blessings of Moses (Deut 33). The Balaam oracles complete a trilogy of archaic tribal blessings in the Pentateuch.

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

### Jewish readings

The Mishnah's condemnation of Balaam (*Sanhedrin* 10:2) sits in tension with the rabbis' uncomfortable admission — repeated in *Bamidbar Rabbah* 20 and *Sifre Numbers* — that Balaam's oracles are among the most exalted Israel-praising texts in all Scripture. The rabbis resolve the tension in several ways. One is to argue that Balaam's praise is involuntary: God binds his tongue, and what emerges is God's word, not Balaam's. A second is to note that Balaam's own preferred blessings would, had God allowed them, have been subtle curses disguised as blessings; God therefore overrode his intent entirely. A third, developed in the *Bavli* (*Sanhedrin* 105b), is the aggadic conceit that Balaam's very gift for curse — his knowing the "moment when God is angry" each day — was given to him precisely so that God might reveal in his silence during Israel's passage that divine anger was withheld.

Philo in *De Vita Mosis* 1.277-291 treats the first oracle as a genuine theophanic speech that even a wicked seer could not adulterate. Josephus paraphrases Balaam's oracles at length in *Antiquities* 4.114-125, preserving the sense that the Jewish tradition held these specific verses in high esteem.

Medieval commentators delve into the oracles with care. Rashi takes *ʿam lĕbādād yiškōn* as a statement of Israel's religious uniqueness — the people cannot be counted with the nations because they alone worship the true God. Ibn Ezra reads *ʾăḥărîtô* ("his end," v. 10) as referring to the afterlife of the righteous; Ramban develops this into a full doctrine of reward for the upright. Maimonides in the *Guide* treats the oracles as illustrations that prophecy, even at its highest non-Israelite level, is still bound by the divine will; no prophet, including Balaam, can say what God has not commanded.

In modern Jewish scholarship, Nehama Leibowitz's studies (*Iyunim bBemidbar*) meditate on v. 19 — "God is not man, that he should lie" — as the theological center of the oracle. Milgrom's JPS notes draw out the way v. 23 makes Balaam his own best witness against pagan divination: the master diviner declares divination inadequate. Sacks, in *Covenant & Conversation*, uses *ʿam lĕbādād yiškōn* to reflect on Jewish particularism in a universalist age.

### Christian readings

Patristic exegesis fastened on two verses. Origen's *Homilies on Numbers* 15-16 treat "God is not man, that he should lie" (v. 19) as an anchor text for divine immutability and the reliability of prophetic promise; this verse becomes a standard proof text in Christian theology through Augustine, Aquinas, and beyond. Origen also reads *ʿam lĕbādād yiškōn* ecclesiologically: the Church is the people who dwell apart, separated unto God. Cyril of Alexandria's *Glaphyra* similarly mines the oracles for typological capital.

Augustine, in *City of God* 18.45 and in the *Questions on the Heptateuch*, reads the oracles as evidence that pagan prophecy can genuinely be from God and can bear witness to Christ before Christ. The Sibylline Oracles, the Hermetic corpus, and Balaam's blessings were grouped together in patristic apologetic as proof that even outside Israel the nations had received hints of the truth. Ambrose's treatise *De Balaam* (or the relevant homilies) develops the moral typology: Balaam utters true words; Balaam is damned.

Aquinas treats v. 19 — "God is not man that he should lie" — repeatedly in the *Summa* (e.g., I q. 19 on God's will, I q. 16 on truth) as axiomatic for the doctrine of divine simplicity and veracity. Calvin's *Institutes* uses the same verse to ground the doctrine of predestination's unchangeability. Luther preached on the oracles as a demonstration that God's Word cannot be silenced by human greed — a characteristically Reformation application.

Critical Christian scholarship on the oracles is, as noted, dominated by the Albright-Cross-Freedman dating argument, with Hoftijzer and Jo Ann Hackett's Deir ʿAlla work grounding the Transjordan context. Wenham (TOTC), Cole (NAC), Budd (WBC), and Dozeman (FOTL) all accept some version of the "old poetry in later narrative frame" hypothesis, though they differ on the degree of redactional shaping. Radner (Brazos) and Brueggemann emphasize the theological work the oracles do within the canon, setting Israel's blessing before Israel in a voice that is not Israel's own.

### Convergences and divergences

Jewish and Christian readings converge on v. 19 as a core text of divine truthfulness. They converge on the ironic force of v. 23 — the diviner declaring divination null. They diverge sharply on *ʿam lĕbādād yiškōn* (v. 9), which Jewish tradition reads as a statement of Israel's covenantal uniqueness (and polemical writers applied against Jewish integration into the nations) and Christian tradition reads ecclesiologically (the Church) or Christologically (Christ as the "one apart"). Each tradition takes the single Hebrew phrase and routes it through its own doctrine of election. The dispute over who "the people dwelling alone" names is one of the older running arguments between the two traditions.

## Lens 4 — The Theological Intent

Two tensions.

**Tension 1: The unchangeable divine will and the freedom of intercession.** Verse 19 declares "God is not man, that he should lie, or a son of man, that he should change his mind (*yitneḥām*)." But the Bible repeatedly shows God "changing his mind" — in Exodus 32:14 after Moses' intercession, in Amos 7:3 and 7:6 after the prophet pleads, in Jonah 3:10 after Nineveh repents. The Hebrew verb *niḥam* is the same verb. How do we reconcile the statement of immutability here with the narrative of a God who relents?

*Strict immutability position (much classical Christian theology, medieval Thomism):* Verse 19 is foundational. When Scripture speaks of God "relenting," this is anthropomorphic accommodation — God's unchanging purpose was, from eternity, to forgive after intercession. The change is in the human situation, not in God. Aquinas in *ST* I q. 19 a. 7 treats the Balaam oracle as the baseline and the narrative "relentings" as phenomenal descriptions of God's real but eternal decree.

*Open/narrative position (process theology, some contemporary readings — Fretheim, Brueggemann):* The narrative depictions of God relenting are theologically weighty. Balaam's oracle is a statement not of metaphysical immutability but of the reliability of a specific word of blessing already given. God "cannot go back on" the Abrahamic blessing of Israel; that is what v. 19 actually means in context. It does not foreclose other moments in which God responds to intercession. Fretheim in *The Suffering of God* argues this is the more textually faithful reading: v. 19 is about this oracle, this moment, this blessing — not a doctrine of metaphysical changelessness.

*Steelman — rabbinic middle position:* Read the verse in context. Balaam has been paid to secure a reversal of divine speech. Balak keeps moving Balaam to new altars on the hypothesis that the divine verdict can be bought with better ritual. The oracle's denial of divine "changing" is a denial of that purchaseability. God will not be bribed or bent by ritual manipulation. But this does not mean God will not respond to genuine repentance or intercession. The *niḥam* that God refuses here is the ritual coercion; the *niḥam* God grants after Moses' intercession in Exodus 32 is the response to covenantal plea. Rabbinic exegesis (Sifre, Rashi, Ibn Ezra) distinguishes these contextually, and this distinction — steelman — holds exegetically better than the blunt doctrinal extraction either of immutability or of openness.

**Where this leaves us:** The oracle means what it says: the specific blessing on Israel is not up for purchase by pagan ritual. God is not a transactable deity. Whether this grounds a general doctrine of metaphysical immutability is a further question the text does not directly answer. The canonical reader can honor the oracle's force in context while holding open the wider theological question about God and change.

**Tension 2: Israel as "dwelling alone" — election, particularity, and the nations.** Verse 9 — *ʿam lĕbādād yiškōn ûbaggôyim lōʾ yitḥaššāb* — "a people dwelling alone, and not counting itself among the nations." Is this a theological compliment or a sociological observation? Is it prescriptive (Israel *ought* to live apart) or descriptive (Israel *does* live apart)? And what are its ethical implications?

*Strong particularist reading (much rabbinic tradition, parts of medieval Judaism, modern Orthodox thinkers):* The verse is a theological anthropology of Israel. The covenant is particular; the people is set apart; the nations cannot be reckoned as a category that includes Israel. The modern rabbinic application — Rabbi Soloveitchik, Yitz Greenberg in mild form, the more charged application by Eliezer Berkovits — is that Israel's distinctiveness is non-negotiable and resists assimilationist pressures. Leibowitz reads the verse as validation of Jewish separatism in matters of Torah and peoplehood.

*Universalist reading (some Reform Jewish, most liberal Christian):* Yes, Israel is distinct, but the distinctness is for the sake of the nations, not against them. *ʿam lĕbādād* names Israel's role as priestly people (Exod 19:6) set apart *for* service to a larger humanity. To read it as separatist isolationism is to miss its purpose. The blessings in Gen 12:3 and the nations-focused prophetic literature support this reading. Brueggemann and Goldingay would work in this direction.

*Steelman — covenantal particularity in service of universal blessing (Maimonides, Sacks, Wenham, Radner):* Refuse the either/or. The verse states a fact that is simultaneously theological: Israel's election makes it unclassifiable by the standard ANE taxonomy of nations. This is not a prescription for isolationism, nor is it a universalism that dissolves the particular. It is the claim that a people exists whose identity is grounded outside the nation-categories of the surrounding cultures. That grounding is precisely what makes the people a blessing to those nations. Maimonides works this out philosophically; Sacks develops it for a pluralist age; Radner grounds it in canonical figuration. The steelman: particularity *is* the means of universality, not its opposite.

**Where this leaves us:** The text names Israel's distinctiveness as a positive theological fact, spoken by a pagan seer. What Israel's distinctiveness is *for* — what responsibilities and obligations flow from it, how it relates to the nations — is the subject of the larger canon. Numbers 23 establishes the fact of separation; Isaiah, Jonah, Ruth, and Paul work out its implications.

**Unresolved question:** If the Balaam oracles genuinely date to the 12th-10th centuries as Albright and Cross argued, what do we make of their apparent polemic against divination as ineffective against Israel (v. 23) in a period when divination was universally practiced and accepted? Is this a pre-monarchic Israelite theological polemic already articulated? Or are the oracles anachronistically retrojected onto a pagan seer? The archaeological context of Deir ʿAlla suggests robust Transjordan divination cultures continuing into the Iron II period. The relationship between the oracles' antiquity and their theological polemic remains a live scholarly question.

## The Thread

Three non-obvious cross-references.

1. **1 Samuel 15:29 and Numbers 23:19** — The same verse, almost word for word. After Saul's disobedience with the Amalekites, Samuel says: "And also the Glory of Israel (*nēṣaḥ yiśrāʾēl*) will not lie or have regret (*lōʾ yĕnaḥēm*), for he is not a man, that he should have regret." The phrase is a direct echo of Balaam's oracle. The Deuteronomistic Historian knew the Balaam material and deployed it at a moment when Saul's kingship was being torn away. The cross-reference suggests that the "shout of a king among them" in v. 21 was heard in monarchic Israel as a foreshadowing of the Davidic dynasty; when Saul proves unworthy, Samuel reaches for Balaam's language to make the point that God's settled blessing now passes to David. The oracle on Pisgah resonates in Gilgal four centuries later.

2. **Hosea 11:9 and Numbers 23:19** — "I am God and not a man (*ʾēl ʾānōkî wĕlōʾ ʾîš*), the Holy One in your midst." Hosea inverts Balaam. Balaam says "God is not a man, that he should lie" — God will not change his promise of mercy. Hosea says "I am God and not a man" — God will *not* destroy in wrath the way a human would. The two texts use the same contrast between divine and human for opposite pastoral ends: faithful mercy when Israel is blessed (Balaam), faithful mercy when Israel deserves destruction (Hosea). The canonical witness to the Balaam oracle is that it names something prophetic literature will return to again.

3. **Romans 11:25-29 and Numbers 23:19-21** — Paul argues that "the gifts and the calling of God are irrevocable" (*ametamelēta ta charismata kai hē klēsis tou theou*, Rom 11:29). The logic is Balaam's logic: God has said, and God will do. Paul uses the principle to argue for Israel's continuing election even through unbelief. The Balaam oracle — that God has blessed and cannot be made to revoke — resurfaces as the structural premise of Paul's most sustained reflection on Israel and the Gentiles. The pagan seer's unwilling theology becomes the apostle's settled doctrine.
