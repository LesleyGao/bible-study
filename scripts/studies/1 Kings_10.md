# 1 Kings 10: The Apex of Empire and the Riddle of Glory

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

The Queen of Sheba narrative places us at the imaginative center of Iron Age IIA international trade. Sheba (Hebrew *Shəbaʾ*, שְׁבָא) corresponds to the Sabaean kingdom of southern Arabia, in what is now Yemen — the most powerful of the South Arabian polities along with Maʿīn, Qatabān, and Ḥaḍramawt. The Sabaean civilization is securely attested in the epigraphic record by the early first millennium BCE: Old Sabaic inscriptions on the great Maʿrib dam (the *ʿArim* of later Arabic tradition), the Awwām temple complex dedicated to the moon-god Almaqah, and a robust caravan economy carrying frankincense, myrrh, gold, and precious stones up the western Arabian *via odorifera* into Transjordan and the Levant. Kenneth Kitchen, who has worked extensively on South Arabian chronology (*On the Reliability of the Old Testament*; his *Documentation for Ancient Arabia* volumes), argues that a tenth-century synchronism between a Sabaean ruler and Jerusalem is plausible if one accepts an early dating of South Arabian state formation; Christian Robin and the French epigraphic school have generally pushed Sabaean state-formation slightly later, into the eighth or even seventh century, which would render the chapter's narrative anachronistic and reflect later commercial realities projected backward. The text is silent on the queen's name; Ethiopian tradition (the *Kebra Nagast*) calls her Makeda, and Arab tradition Bilqīs — both demonstrably late legendary expansions, but anchored to the historical fact that the Aksumite kingdom across the Red Sea later extended Sabaean cultural presence into the Horn of Africa, complicating the geography.

The material culture of the chapter is recognizable. Camel caravans (*gəmallîm*, גְּמַלִּים) are the standard long-distance freight technology of the Arabian trade by the late second millennium; Magnus Ottosson and Paula Wapnish on faunal remains at Tell Jemmeh confirm domesticated dromedaries in the Negev/Sinai by the late second millennium, contra older skeptical revisions. The "spices" (*bəśāmîm*, בְּשָׂמִים) are the aromatic resins — frankincense (*lebonâ*) and myrrh (*môr*) — whose monopoly the South Arabian kingdoms guarded jealously. The 120 talents of gold (verse 10) reckon to roughly four metric tons by the heavier Mesopotamian standard, an ostentatious figure that nonetheless aligns with the order of magnitude attested in Egyptian and Assyrian royal inscriptions for foreign tribute (compare the spoils Thutmose III claims from his Levantine campaigns at Karnak). Ophir (*ʾÔphîr*, אוֹפִיר), the gold source, is famously located in the Tel Qasile ostracon (Israel Museum, Iron IIB, eighth century BCE), inscribed *zhb ʾpr lbyt ḥrn š 30* — "gold of Ophir for Beth-Horon, 30 shekels" — confirming that "Ophir-gold" was a real commercial designation, not merely a literary trope. Geographic candidates range from the African Punt coast (Eritrea/Somalia) to southern Arabia to the Indus valley; Sopher and Sara Japhet have surveyed the options without resolution.

The 666 talents of annual gold income (10:14) — a figure whose later numerical reverberation in Revelation 13:18 has fueled centuries of interpretation — is approximately 22 metric tons by the heavy standard, comparable to the looted treasuries Sennacherib boasts of stripping from defeated kingdoms in his Annals. The throne of ivory overlaid with gold (*kissēʾ-šēn gādōl*, כִּסֵּא־שֵׁן גָּדוֹל) finds material parallels in the carved ivory inlay tradition recovered from Megiddo, Samaria, Arslan Tash, and Nimrud (the Salmanu-shar Fort), where royal furniture was covered with thin ivory plaques in Phoenician style. The "House of the Forest of Lebanon" was an arsenal-treasury described in 1 Kings 7:2 with cedar pillars in rows; the ceremonial gold shields stored there functioned as parade armor, the Iron Age equivalent of presentation weapons. Six steps and twelve flanking lions on the throne (10:19-20) recall both Mesopotamian throne iconography (the lion-flanked Neo-Assyrian thrones in Ashurnasirpal II's reliefs at Nimrud) and the Egyptian baboon-flanked thrones of the New Kingdom — a deliberately syncretic visual program asserting universal kingship.

A few additional Hebrew terms reward attention. The "hard questions" of verse 1 are *ḥîdôt* (חִידוֹת), the riddle-puzzles of ANE wisdom contests — the same word for Samson's wedding riddle (Judges 14) and the dark sayings of Psalm 49:4 and Proverbs 1:6. The "almug wood" (*ʾalmuggîm*, אַלְמֻגִּים, verse 11) is debated; James A. Montgomery in the ICC volume preferred sandalwood from India, while others propose Lebanese juniper. "Tarshish" (*Taršîš*, תַּרְשִׁישׁ) ships (verse 22) have generated a literature unto themselves: the Phoenician inscription from Nora in Sardinia (ninth-eighth century BCE) refers to *trss*, suggesting a western-Mediterranean smelting-port identification (perhaps Tartessos in Spain), though the term may also have generalized to mean "deep-sea-going freighter."

## Lens 2 — The Literature

The chapter is the narrative crown of the Solomon source — what scholars since Martin Noth have isolated as a distinct compositional unit (1 Kings 3-11) preserved within the Deuteronomistic History. Gwilym Jones (NCB) calls it "the catalogue of Solomonic glory," and Marc Brettler in *The Creation of History in Ancient Israel* describes the chapter's genre as royal *Hofgeschichte* — court annal in the encomiastic mode, comparable to the Egyptian "King's Novel" (*Königsnovelle*) genre identified by Alfred Hermann. The chapter's structural logic is panel-by-panel: Sheba's visit and verdict (1-13), gold and revenue inventory (14-22), the international tribute summary (23-25), and the chariot/horse trade (26-29). Each panel piles superlative on superlative.

The literary devices repay attention. The narrator stages the queen's encounter with Solomon by progressive overwhelm: she sees the wisdom, the house, the table, the seating, the attendance, the clothing, the cupbearers, the burnt offerings — and the cumulative result is climactic: *wəlōʾ-hāyâ bāh ʿôd rûaḥ*, "there was no more breath in her" (verse 5). Robert Alter in *The Art of Biblical Narrative* treats this as a textbook case of biblical hyperbole subordinated to characterization; Meir Sternberg in *The Poetics of Biblical Narrative* sees it as a classic narrator's strategy of borrowing a foreigner's outsider gaze to certify the protagonist's stature — the same device deployed with Naaman in 2 Kings 5 and the Tyrian craftsmen in 1 Kings 5. The queen's speech (6-9) is a doxology by a foreigner: she blesses YHWH and articulates the dtr's own theological claim — that YHWH "loved Israel forever" and gave Solomon to "execute justice and righteousness" (*la-ʿăśôt mišpāṭ ûṣədāqâ*). The dtr's editorial fingerprint is unmistakable.

But there is irony built into the text. The same chapter that records the apex of Solomonic glory simultaneously catalogues a series of explicit Deuteronomic violations. Deuteronomy 17:16-17 — the "Law of the King" — forbids the king from multiplying horses, returning to Egypt for horses, and accumulating gold and silver. Verses 26-29 record Solomon importing 1,400 chariots and 12,000 horsemen *from Egypt*; verses 14-22 document the silver and gold accumulation. The chapter that precedes the indictment in chapter 11 has, for any reader holding Deuteronomy in the other hand, *already* condemned itself. Walter Brueggemann in *1 & 2 Kings* (Smyth & Helwys) calls this "the dialectical glory" — splendor and apostasy occupying the same verses. Iain Provan (NIBC) and Robert Polzin in *David and the Deuteronomist* both emphasize the dtr's craft: the praise-section is the indictment when read intertextually with Deuteronomy 17.

Textual variants are significant in this stretch. The LXX of 1 Kings (3 Kingdoms in the Greek tradition) reorders Solomonic material substantially in chapters 2-11; in 10 specifically, the ordering of the gold-revenue and chariot material is preserved, but the LXX redactor has elsewhere relocated the apostasy notices, suggesting a hand reading the whole Solomonic block as a coherent argumentative unit. Julio Trebolle Barrera and Adrian Schenker have argued that LXX preserves an earlier, pre-Deuteronomistic ordering at several points; the upshot is that 1 Kings 10's juxtaposition of Sheba's praise with chariot-trade-with-Egypt is a deliberate Hebrew compositional choice, not an accident of transmission. Knoppers in *Two Nations Under God* notes that the chapter's quantitative excess (the precise figures, the inventory style) marks the Solomon source's distinctive register — counting is itself an act of theological evaluation, a topic Knoppers picks up from the David-census of 2 Samuel 24.

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

The chapter has produced extraordinary interpretive trajectories. Within the Hebrew canon, the Sheba episode is reprised in 2 Chronicles 9 with minor expansions — the Chronicler retains the praise but is less interested in the ironic juxtapositions, since he excises chapter 11's apostasy almost entirely. Within the prophets, Isaiah 60:6 ("the wealth of the nations shall come to you... they shall bring gold and frankincense") and Psalm 72:10-15 (Sheba and Seba bringing gifts to the messianic king) make Sheba a figure of eschatological tribute — the nations streaming to Zion. The Sheba motif lives on into Matthew 2's magi (gold, frankincense, myrrh — explicitly the South Arabian trade goods) and Matthew 12:42's "Queen of the South" who will rise to condemn an unbelieving generation; one greater than Solomon is here.

In Jewish tradition the chapter has accumulated extraordinary midrashic and commentarial layers. Josephus (*Antiquities* 8.165-175) calls her "Nikaulis, queen of Egypt and Ethiopia" — already conflating Sheba with later African geography — and emphasizes her wisdom-test as a contest with hard questions. The Targum Sheni to Esther preserves an elaborate riddle-tradition: Solomon answers questions about the natural and moral world. *b. Bava Batra* 15b debates whether "the queen of Sheba" should be read literally or as "the kingdom of Sheba" — Rabbi Shmuel preferred the latter, perhaps to avoid the legendary expansions. Rashi on the chapter is restrained, taking the wisdom and wealth at face value. Radak (David Kimhi) is more interpretive — he reads the queen's doxology as a model of Gentile recognition of YHWH. Ralbag (Levi ben Gershom) consistently finds rationalist lessons: Solomon's wisdom is empirically demonstrated, not magical. Don Isaac Abarbanel — whose extraordinary fifteenth-century commentary on Kings is one of the most politically sophisticated readings ever produced — sees the chapter as evidence of the political theology Solomon will betray: a king at the apex of legitimate wealth-by-trade who slides into idolatry-by-marriage in the next chapter, the inversion of right kingship. Yair Zakovitch (*The Concept of the Miracle in the Bible*) and Moshe Garsiel have explored the riddle-contest motif's ANE parallels.

In Christian reception the chapter generated one of the most elaborate typological traditions of any OT episode. Origen's *Commentary on the Song of Songs* reads the queen as a type of the church coming from afar to hear the wisdom of Christ. Augustine in *City of God* 17 treats Sheba as a type of the Gentile church's pilgrimage. Ambrose, Chrysostom, and Theodoret of Cyrus (whose *Quaestiones in Reges* offers careful philological commentary) develop the Sheba-as-church reading. Gregory the Great's *Moralia* uses the queen's recognition as a template for the soul's progression from rumor to vision. Aquinas (*Summa* and the Catena) brings the typology into scholastic synthesis. Calvin's *Sermons on 1 Kings* — preached at Geneva and recorded by stenographers, recently re-edited by Max Engammare — handle the chapter with characteristic double-mindedness: he praises Solomon's wisdom but already in the homily anticipates the chapter 11 fall, refusing to let his hearers settle into admiration.

In modern critical scholarship the chapter has been a fulcrum. Mordechai Cogan's *I Kings* (Anchor Bible) is the indispensable reference for the chapter's ANE comparanda — he documents the riddle-contest motif, the camel chronology, and the Tel Qasile Ophir ostracon with care. Marvin Sweeney (*I & II Kings*, OTL) reads the chapter as "the dtr's setup" — the height from which Solomon is about to fall. Frank Moore Cross's double-redaction theory of the DH has the Josianic edition presenting Solomon as positive paradigm and the exilic edition adding the indictments; Richard Nelson's *The Double Redaction of the Deuteronomistic History* refines the source criticism. Knoppers in *Two Nations Under God* has the most theologically penetrating reading of the chapter's structural irony. Brueggemann's *1 & 2 Kings* and Eugene Peterson's *Leap Over a Wall* (which treats David and Solomon together) dwell on the apex-precedes-fall motif; Iain Provan, Davis (*1 Kings: The Wisdom and the Folly*), and John Goldingay in his *Old Testament Theology* trilogy all return to the chapter's deliberate ambiguity. Jon Levenson on Davidic theology has emphasized that 1 Kings 10's Solomon already prefigures the eschatological king of the Psalms.

## Lens 4 — The Theological Intent

**Tension One: Is the wisdom-and-wealth here covenant blessing or covenant violation?**

Position one: traditional reading. The chapter records the fulfillment of YHWH's promise in 1 Kings 3:13 — "I will give you what you have not asked, both riches and honor." The queen of Sheba's doxology in verse 9 ("Blessed be the LORD your God, who has delighted in you and set you on the throne of Israel") is the narrative's authoritative voice. Solomon is, at this moment, the Davidic king ruling in righteousness, blessed precisely as Deuteronomy 28's blessings predict: nations stream to him with tribute, riches abound. This is the reading of much of the patristic tradition, of Aquinas, and of premodern Jewish commentary — the chapter is straightforwardly positive, the apex of a covenant-faithful reign.

Position two: the dialectical reading (Brueggemann, Polzin, Knoppers). The same verses that record blessing simultaneously document violation. The Law of the King in Deuteronomy 17:16-17 is explicit: no horse-multiplication, no return to Egypt for horses, no silver-and-gold accumulation. Verses 26-29 do all three. The narrator is staging the irony: the praise-section *is* the indictment when read intertextually. The dtr's reader is supposed to feel the cognitive dissonance — to admire and condemn in the same breath. The chapter is not naive encomium; it is sophisticated theological satire on the very category of imperial blessing.

Position three: the structural-prophetic reading (Iain Provan, Davis). The chapter is neither pure blessing nor pure indictment but a *tragic apex* — the height from which the fall is about to occur, with the fall already encoded in the height. The accumulation of horses, gold, and (implicitly) wives is the precondition for chapter 11's apostasy. The narrator is showing us not that prosperity is bad but that Solomon's particular prosperity is structured around dependencies — on Egypt, on foreign trade-monopolies, on tribute-relationships sealed through marriage — that will inevitably draw his heart from YHWH. The wealth is not the sin; the entanglements wealth requires are.

Steelman of position two: the dtr is sophisticated enough to use praise as critique. The "Law of the King" is not background noise; it is a deliberate compositional partner to chapters 3-11. Every reader of Deuteronomy who reaches 1 Kings 10:26 has the *exact* phrasing of Deuteronomy 17:16 ringing in their ears. The narrator is not naive. He knows what he is doing.

Where this leaves us: the dialectical reading is the most defensible exegetically, but pastoral preaching often slides toward position one or three. The chapter forces the question of whether prosperity *as such* can be a category of blessing in a fallen-but-redeemed people, or whether prosperity always carries the seeds of its own apostasy. The text refuses to resolve this — and that refusal is the point.

**Tension Two: What is the Queen of Sheba's status — convert, witness, or foreign foil?**

Position one: convert. Her doxology in verse 9 is read as genuine confession. Matthew 12:42's "Queen of the South" judging the unbelieving generation reinforces this — she is held up as a positive exemplar of Gentile recognition, comparable to the Ninevites under Jonah. Origen, Augustine, and the typological tradition treat her as type of the believing church.

Position two: witness, not convert. She blesses YHWH-as-Solomon's-God ("the LORD *your* God," verse 9) without committing to YHWH-as-her-own-God. She returns to her own land. The text gives no evidence of cultic transfer. She is a foreign witness whose testimony certifies Solomon's stature for the dtr's narrative purposes; her own theological state is left open. (Cogan, Sweeney.)

Position three: foreign foil — the queen exists in the text to function as outsider gaze, not as a character in her own right. Her speech is the dtr's speech in foreign mouth (Sternberg). The narrator borrows her voice to articulate the theological evaluation he wants to make.

Steelman of position one: the text records a doxology to YHWH on the lips of a foreign monarch and does not indicate she returns to other gods. Matthew's Jesus retroactively certifies her as exemplary. To read her as merely literary device is to undersell what the narrator gave us.

Where this leaves us: the chapter makes a theological point about the *visibility* of YHWH's righteousness — that it is recognizable across cultural and religious boundaries — without taking a position on the queen's individual fate. This is the Bible's usual restraint about Gentile interlocutors: their function is more theological than biographical.

Unresolved question: if 1 Kings 10's Solomon is already in covenant violation by the standards of Deuteronomy 17, why does the dtr permit the chapter's praise to stand uncorrected within the chapter itself? Why not signal the indictment until chapter 11? One possibility: the dtr trusts the reader who has Deuteronomy. Another: the praise is genuinely meant as provisional blessing that the chapter 11 apostasy will retroactively poison. The text does not say.

## The Thread

The chapter sits at the hinge of Solomon's reign. It looks back to 3:5-14 (the wisdom-and-wealth promise) and forward to 11:1-13 (the apostasy that fulfills the dialectical reading). It connects to the "Law of the King" in Deuteronomy 17:14-20 by inverse demonstration; every prohibition is documented as fulfilled. It anticipates Isaiah 60 and Psalm 72 in the Sheba-streaming-to-Zion motif. Forward in the canon, it is reprised in Matthew 12:42 and the magi of Matthew 2 (with Sheban trade goods explicit). The 666 talents of verse 14 — coincidence or numerical play with Revelation 13:18 — has fueled a rich apocalyptic reception history that John Collins and Adela Yarbro Collins have catalogued. The chapter's deepest internal echo is to chapter 11's reversal: the same wealth that makes Solomon glorious will entangle him with the foreign wives whose gods he will house on the Mount of Olives. Glory and ruin occupy the same accounting ledger.
