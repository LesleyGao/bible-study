# Revelation 11: The Measured Temple, the Murdered Witnesses, and the Kingdom That Has Come

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

The Apocalypse comes from John of Patmos, on the dominant scholarly reading written near the end of Domitian's reign, around AD 95. Irenaeus (*Against Heresies* 5.30.3) is the earliest external witness, and the dating is followed by Richard Bauckham, G.K. Beale, David Aune, Craig Koester, and Adela Yarbro Collins. The minority Neronian dating (A.T. Robinson, Kenneth Gentry) places the book before AD 70 and is dramatically consequential for chapter 11: if the book is pre-70, the temple of 11:1–2 is most plausibly the literal Jerusalem temple still standing, and the chapter becomes a prophecy of its imminent destruction. If the book is Domitianic, the temple is necessarily symbolic, since the literal building has been ash for a quarter-century.

The historical pressure on the Asia Minor churches was the imperial cult — what Steven Friesen has documented in detail in *Imperial Cults and the Apocalypse of John* — and the slow social marginalization that came with refusing to participate. Leonard Thompson cautions that the picture of systematic Domitianic persecution is overdrawn in older scholarship; the pressure was less centralized terror than the chronic chafing of dissent within a religiously organized society. Adela Yarbro Collins, in *Crisis and Catharsis*, treats the crisis as partly perceived; her *Combat Myth in the Book of Revelation* will become decisive in chapter 12 but already lurks behind the cosmic-mythic register here.

Chapter 11 stands as the second movement of Revelation's second major interlude (the first interlude was chapter 7; this one runs across chapters 10–11). Chapter 10 has reissued John's prophetic commission with the bittersweet scroll. Chapter 11 immediately presents the *content* of the renewed commission in the form of two visions — the measuring of the temple and the two witnesses — followed by the long-deferred seventh trumpet (11:15–19).

The opening vision (11:1–2) hands John a *kalamos* (κάλαμος) like a *rhabdos* (ῥάβδος, a measuring rod or staff) and instructs him to measure *ton naon tou theou* (τὸν ναὸν τοῦ θεοῦ, "the sanctuary of God"). The lexical choice is precise: *naos* designates the inner sanctuary proper, distinct from *hieron* (ἱερόν), which encompasses the entire temple complex. The altar and the worshipers are to be measured; the outer court is *not* to be measured but is given over to *ta ethnē* (τὰ ἔθνη, the nations), who will *patēsousin* (πατήσουσιν, "will trample") the holy city for forty-two months. The Ezekiel 40–48 substrate is unmistakable — the long prophetic tour of the eschatological temple, conducted by an angelic guide with a measuring rod. Zechariah 2 supplies a second measuring intertext.

The second vision (11:3–13) introduces the two witnesses, *hoi dyo martyres mou* (οἱ δύο μάρτυρές μου), prophesying for 1,260 days clothed in sackcloth. The number is exegetically dense: 1,260 days equals forty-two months equals three and a half years equals "a time, times, and half a time" — the pattern Daniel 7:25 and 12:7 use for the period of antichristian dominance. The same number recurs across Revelation 11–13 in different formulations, structurally binding these chapters together.

The witnesses themselves carry deliberate Mosaic and Elijahic typology. They have power "to shut the sky, that no rain may fall during the days of their prophesying" — Elijah in 1 Kings 17. They have "power over the waters to turn them into blood and to strike the earth with every kind of plague" — Moses in Exodus 7. Fire pours from their mouths to consume their foes — Elijah calling fire down on the messengers of Ahaziah in 2 Kings 1. They are also "the two olive trees and the two lampstands that stand before the Lord of the earth" — a direct quotation of Zechariah 4, where Joshua and Zerubbabel are figured as the two anointed ones standing before the Lord.

Their identities have been disputed across the tradition. The patristic majority read them as Moses and Elijah on typological grounds; some patristic and many medieval readers preferred Enoch and Elijah, drawing on the tradition that both men did not see death and could therefore reappear. Hippolytus argued for Enoch and Elijah, as did, much later, the dispensationalist John Walvoord. Modern symbolic readers (Bauckham, Beale, Mounce) take the witnesses as a corporate symbol: two figures because in Deuteronomy 19:15 testimony requires two witnesses, prophesying because the church's vocation is prophetic, killed because the witness is martyr-witness — the double meaning of *martyria* (μαρτυρία) as both legal testimony and martyrdom is here exploited.

The witnesses are killed by *to thērion to anabainon ek tēs abyssou* (τὸ θηρίον τὸ ἀναβαῖνον ἐκ τῆς ἀβύσσου), "the beast that rises from the abyss" — the first appearance of the beast in Revelation, anticipating chapter 13. Their bodies lie unburied in the street of "the great city, *hē polis hē megalē* (ἡ πόλις ἡ μεγάλη), that symbolically is called Sodom and Egypt, where their Lord was crucified" (11:8). The geography is multivalent. Where Christ was crucified is Jerusalem. But the city is also called Sodom (Isaiah 1:9–10's name for unfaithful Jerusalem) and Egypt (the archetypal house of bondage). And *hē polis hē megalē* will become Babylon-language in chapters 16–18. The city is and is not Jerusalem; it is and is not Rome; it is the world-system that kills the witnesses, wherever they testify.

After three and a half days a breath of life from God enters them, they rise, ascend in a cloud, an earthquake levels a tenth of the city, seven thousand die, and "the rest were terrified and gave glory to the God of heaven" (11:13). This last detail is striking: a measure of repentance attends the witnesses' vindication — Bauckham, in *The Climax of Prophecy*, makes much of this verse.

The seventh trumpet finally sounds (11:15–19). Loud voices in heaven announce: *egeneto hē basileia tou kosmou tou kyriou hēmōn kai tou Christou autou* (ἐγένετο ἡ βασιλεία τοῦ κόσμου τοῦ κυρίου ἡμῶν καὶ τοῦ Χριστοῦ αὐτοῦ), "the kingdom of the world has become the kingdom of our Lord and of his Christ." The twenty-four elders fall on their faces. The hymn's final line is the chapter's hardest: "the time for destroying the destroyers of the earth" — *diaphtheirai tous diaphtheirontas tēn gēn* (διαφθεῖραι τοὺς διαφθείροντας τὴν γῆν). Then God's temple in heaven opens and the ark of the covenant is seen — the only Revelation glimpse of the ark, recalling the cessation of access at the destruction of Solomon's temple and the legendary hiding of the ark in 2 Maccabees 2.

## Lens 2 — The Literature

Revelation 11 is the most condensed apocalyptic narrative in the book. Three movements compress what most apocalypses would unfold across multiple chapters: protective measuring (1–2), prophetic witness culminating in martyrdom and resurrection (3–13), kingdom-arrival announcement (14–19). Each movement works through Old Testament typology — Ezekiel's measured temple, Moses-Elijah-Zechariah's witnesses, Daniel's kingdom transferred — and each sustains its own micro-structure.

The witnesses' narrative arc itself echoes Christ's: prophetic ministry, killed by the beast in the great city where Jesus was crucified, lying dead three and a half days, raised and ascending in a cloud. Bauckham notes the conformity is point-by-point. The literary effect is to identify the church's vocation with Christ's — what Christ underwent, the witness undergoes; the conformity is not metaphor but the structure of discipleship.

The seventh trumpet's hymnic announcement (11:17–18) is one of Revelation's central liturgical pieces, drawing on coronation language from the royal psalms. The chapter ends with a temple-opening and the ark — a reverse-image of the chapter's opening, where the temple was *measured*. The chapter that began on earth with a measured sanctuary closes in heaven with the unmeasured temple of God thrown open.

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

Revelation 11 binds the prophetic vocation of the church to the entire arc of Israel's prophets — Moses, Elijah, Joshua and Zerubbabel, Ezekiel, Daniel, Zechariah. The gospel-shape of the witnesses' martyrdom binds it equally to Christ. The seventh trumpet's announcement places the chapter at the eschatological pivot: the kingdom *has become* the Lord's, and yet the dragon-and-beast cycle of chapters 12–14 follows. The "already/not yet" structure of New Testament eschatology is enacted in the chapter's own seam.

### Christian Interpretive Trajectory

Patristic readers were transfixed by the two witnesses. Tertullian and Hippolytus preferred Enoch and Elijah — both translated to heaven without dying, both available for return. Methodius and Origen leaned typological-symbolic. Augustine, in *City of God* XX, treats the witnesses with characteristic restraint, declining to specify identities. The patristic consensus was that the witnesses were eschatological figures, but without unanimity on identity.

Medieval interpretation, especially under Joachim of Fiore's influence, periodized the chapter. The forty-two months of trampling became a literal future period; the witnesses became the great preachers of the third age. Joachim's followers identified them variously with Francis and Dominic, or with future spiritual reformers.

Reformation interpretation made chapter 11 into one of its most contested textual battlefields. Luther read the trampling Gentiles as the papacy and the witnesses as the persecuted true church. Calvin's general silence on Revelation leaves us little. Anabaptist readers (Müntzer, the Münsterite radicals) read themselves directly as the witnesses and the established Catholic-Protestant order as the trampling Gentiles. Historicist Protestants over the next three centuries identified the witnesses with John Wycliffe and Jan Hus, or with the Old and New Testaments themselves testifying through history, or with the two Reformations (Lutheran and Calvinist, or Protestant and Anabaptist).

Critical scholarship reset the questions. Adela Yarbro Collins, Christopher Rowland (*The Open Heaven*), and Eugene Boring placed the witnesses within their Jewish-apocalyptic and prophetic-typological matrices. Bauckham's *The Climax of Prophecy* devotes substantial attention to chapter 11, arguing that the witnesses corporately symbolize the church's prophetic ministry to the nations, and that 11:13's "rest were terrified and gave glory to the God of heaven" represents the conversion of the nations through martyred testimony. Brian Blount's witness-theology in *Can I Get a Witness?* takes up the same theme from an African-American interpretive angle.

Contemporary Catholic engagement, exemplified by Wilfrid Harrington (*Sacra Pagina*), reads the witnesses as the church's prophetic vocation, with the patristic typological readings (Moses-Elijah, Enoch-Elijah) preserved as iconographically meaningful but not exegetically determinative. Sandra Schneiders has worked the broader Catholic biblical-theology questions about apocalyptic witness.

### Four Interpretive Schools

- **Preterist**: the temple of 11:1–2 is the literal Jerusalem temple before AD 70 (Robinson, Gentry); the witnesses are early Christian prophets, sometimes identified with James and Peter or with the Jewish-Christian missionary movement, killed in the trauma of the Jewish War. The forty-two months map to a specific period in the run-up to AD 70.

- **Historicist**: the temple is the visible church preserved through medieval corruption; the witnesses are either the Old and New Testaments testifying through church history (a Reformation-era reading), or specific historical figures (Wycliffe and Hus; the magisterial and radical Reformations). The 1,260 days are reckoned as 1,260 years (the day-year principle), spanning roughly the Constantinian establishment to the Reformation.

- **Futurist** (dispensational): the temple is a future rebuilt Third Temple in Jerusalem; the witnesses are literal future prophets active during the tribulation period (Walvoord, LaHaye); the forty-two months are a literal half of the seven-year tribulation. The dispensational reading takes the chapter as predictive prophecy about specific future events, often involving expectations of literal reconstruction of the Jerusalem temple.

- **Idealist**: the temple is the church preserved through every tribulation; the witnesses are the perennial prophetic ministry of the church in every age; the 1,260 days are the symbolic interval between Christ's resurrection and the parousia (Hendriksen, Caird, Mounce). The chapter teaches what is true always, not what will be true once.

### Jewish Scholarly Engagement

Daniel 7–12 is the proximate Jewish background for the chronology. Daniel 7:25's "time, times, and half a time," 12:7's same phrase, and 12:11's 1,290 days establish the apocalyptic chronology Revelation 11–13 presupposes. John Collins's *Daniel* (Hermeneia) and *The Apocalyptic Imagination* are the standard reference points. James VanderKam and Loren Stuckenbruck have shown how the chronology was developed in 1 Enoch, Jubilees, and the Qumran texts. James Charlesworth's edition of the Pseudepigrapha makes the parallel material accessible.

The temple-measurement intertext draws from Ezekiel 40–48 directly, and from the Qumran *Temple Scroll* (11Q19), which shares Revelation's interest in the eschatological temple's measurements. Daniel Boyarin's argument that Jewish and Christian textual traditions remained porous well into the second century is helpful here: Revelation 11 reads as Jewish apocalyptic in its DNA, even as it deploys this material for a Gentile-majority church.

## Lens 4 — The Theological Intent

What is God doing? God protects the prophetic-witness community of the church — measured, sealed for preservation — even as he permits the trampling of the outer court and the killing of the witnesses. God then vindicates the witnesses through resurrection and ascension, brings on the seventh trumpet, and announces the kingdom's arrival. The theological burden: the witness is real, the cost is real, and the kingdom has come even while the beast still rages.

### Tension 1: The Measured Temple — What Does the Temple Vision Mean?

The temple-vision of 11:1–2 is the chapter's hardest interpretive crux, and the four schools diverge sharply.

The **preterist** position (Robinson, Gentry) reads the temple as the literal Jerusalem temple in its final years before AD 70. The measuring is for preservation of the worshipers — the Jewish-Christian community within the temple precincts — even as the outer court and the city are given over to Roman trampling. On this reading, Revelation is a prophecy of the Jewish War, and the forty-two months map to a specific period within that war (Josephus's chronologies are sometimes invoked here). The literary case for preterism is the natural reading of *naos tou theou* in the Greek; the historical case is that a measurable Jerusalem temple existed at the moment of writing.

The **post-AD 70 symbolic** position (Bauckham, Beale, Mounce, Boring) takes the temple as the church or the people of God preserved through tribulation. The historical pressure on this reading is that the literal temple was rubble at the time of writing; the symbolic move is therefore necessary. The exegetical case rests on Paul's temple language for the church (1 Corinthians 3:16; 2 Corinthians 6:16; Ephesians 2:21), which Revelation seems to extend. The forty-two months become the symbolic period of the church under tribulation — the entire interval between the resurrection and the parousia, on the strongest idealist version of this reading.

The **futurist** dispensational position takes the temple as a future Third Temple to be rebuilt in Jerusalem before the parousia. Walvoord and LaHaye's *Left Behind* tradition has popularized this view. The case is that the chapter's Jerusalem-specificity ("where their Lord was crucified," 11:8) and its detailed measurements suggest a literal building. The forty-two months become a literal three and a half years within a literal seven-year tribulation. The position has proven exegetically resilient within its own community and theologically generative for some streams of Christian Zionism.

The **typological-idealist** position (Hendriksen, Caird) takes the temple as the church-as-people throughout history. The forty-two months are perennial — every generation of the church experiences trampling of the outer court and preservation of the inner sanctuary.

Steelman the least intuitive position: the **futurist-literal Third Temple** reading. It is least intuitive to most academic exegetes because the symbolic reading has become the scholarly default. But the futurist reading takes seriously the textual specificity that the chapter resists allegorizing — the measuring rod is concrete, the altar is concrete, "where their Lord was crucified" is concrete. The futurist insists on letting the text's spatial specificity stand. Whatever one makes of the eschatological prediction, the futurist reading correctly notices that Revelation 11 is *not* generic.

**Where this leaves us:** the dating of Revelation hinges partially on this question, and the question is interpretively unresolved. A reading that is too quick to symbolize loses the chapter's Jerusalem-specificity; a reading that is too quick to literalize commits to historical scenarios for which the textual basis is contested.

### Tension 2: The Two Witnesses — Identity and Implication

The witnesses' identity carries large ecclesial consequences.

**Historical figures** (Moses + Elijah, the patristic majority; or Enoch + Elijah, an early minority): the witnesses are specific eschatological returnees, and their ministry is a discrete future event. This reading takes the chapter's typological richness as identification rather than analogy.

**Corporate church** (Bauckham, Beale, modern symbolic majority): the witnesses are the entire Christian community in its prophetic vocation — two because testimony requires two (Deuteronomy 19:15), prophesying because the church's calling is prophetic, killed because witness is martyr-witness. The implication: every Christian is, in some measure, a witness; and the church's missional vocation is ineradicably costly.

**Specific eschatological prophets** (futurist-dispensational): two literal future prophets active during a literal future tribulation. Their identities are unspecified; their work is a future event with specific contours.

**Old and New Testaments** (some historicists): the two witnesses are the Scriptures testifying through history. The reading sounds quaint to modern ears, but it has an exegetical hook in 11:4's "two olive trees and two lampstands" — Zechariah 4's twin anointed ones — read as the twin testaments illuminating the church.

Steelman the least intuitive: the **Old and New Testaments** reading. It is least intuitive because moderns hear it as flat allegory. But it carries a real theological insight: the church's prophetic witness is *always* mediated through Scripture. There is no churchly testimony that is not, in some measure, scriptural testimony. The historicist allegory misreads the textual referent but rightly intuits the textual mediation of all genuine witness. A version of this reading, freed from the strict allegory, sits beneath the corporate-church reading: the church's witness is the scripturally formed prophetic community.

**Where this leaves us:** each reading yields a different ecclesiology. If the witnesses are literal future figures, the church's calling is ordinary missions until that future moment. If they are corporate, the church's calling is to embody martyrdom-shaped witness *now*. If they are the Scriptures, the church's witness reduces to faithful biblical proclamation. The four readings cannot be merged without dilution.

### Closing question

If the kingdom of the world has *already* become the kingdom of our Lord and of his Christ (11:15), and the witnesses are *still* killable in the great city where their Lord was crucified — then what does it mean to live as the body of the already-reigning Christ in the still-killing city?

## The Thread

- **Ezekiel 40–48** — the measured temple, read as the climax of Ezekiel alongside Revelation 11
- **1 Kings 17–19; 2 Kings 1–2** — Elijah's rain-stopping and fire-from-heaven, the typological substrate
- **Daniel 7:25; 12:7** — the 1,260 days source
- **Zechariah 4** — the two olive trees and two lampstands, directly cited in 11:4
