# Job 9: No Arbiter Between Us

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

Job's response to Bildad's first speech is set in the same patriarchal-Edomite register as the prologue, but the ancient cultural material here is specifically astronomical and mythological. Job names four celestial bodies — *Ash* (עָשׁ, "the Bear," probably Ursa Major), *Kesil* (כְּסִיל, "Orion," though literally "fool" or "insolent one"), *Kimah* (כִּימָה, "the Pleiades"), and *hadrei teman* (חַדְרֵי תֵמָן, "chambers of the south," probably the southern constellations visible from the Arabian desert but not from northern Mesopotamia). This is specifically ancient Near Eastern star lore. Marvin Pope's Anchor Bible commentary notes the parallel with the Mesopotamian star-lists preserved at Nineveh (MUL.APIN, c. 1000 BC, catalogued by Hermann Hunger and David Pingree) where the same constellations appear under Akkadian names. The Ugaritic texts from Ras Shamra include hymns to astral deities that Job's language closely resembles.

The political and material world is deep-time patriarchal, but the theological problem is sharper than anywhere else in the Hebrew canon. Job is wrestling with what Carol Newsom (*The Book of Job: A Contest of Moral Imaginations*, 2003) calls the "asymmetry of the cosmic courtroom" — a God who is simultaneously judge, prosecutor, and executioner, with no external standard of appeal. This is not a problem the surrounding cultures solved either. The Babylonian *Ludlul bel nemeqi* ("I Will Praise the Lord of Wisdom," c. 1300 BC, tablets found at Ashur and Nineveh) raises the same question, as does the Sumerian "Man and His God" (c. 2000 BC). But the Babylonian sufferer eventually gets a Marduk-theophany that restores him; the Hebrew sufferer's parallel theophany will not arrive until chapter 38, and it will not resolve his question in the way he hoped.

The dating of Job remains contested. The narrative is patriarchal; the language, especially in the dialogues, is late. Pope, David Clines, and Newsom place the composition in the sixth century BC during or after the exile, in part because the theological problem — the innocent sufferer, the apparent collapse of retributive wisdom — is a problem that pressed the exilic community hardest. Robert Gordis and Francis Andersen defend earlier dates. Ezekiel 14:14 already treats Job as legendary by the early sixth century, which sets an upper bound on the folkloric frame but does not settle the poetic dialogues.

The chapter's mythology is the strongest internal clue to its ancient milieu. Verse 13 — "beneath him bowed the helpers of Rahab" — invokes Rahab (רַהַב), the chaos-sea monster of the West Semitic creation-combat tradition. Rahab is the cousin of Ugaritic Yam and Leviathan, Akkadian Tiamat. Jon Levenson in *Creation and the Persistence of Evil* (1988) treats this as the decisive evidence that ancient Israelite creation theology never fully repudiated the combat motif the way Genesis 1 appears to do: the chaos monsters remain active agents in the poetry of Job, Isaiah, and the Psalms.

Five Hebrew terms open the chapter's interior. First, *tzadaq* (צָדַק, "to be in the right"), the legal/forensic root that dominates the entire dialogue cycle — Job uses it four times in this chapter alone. Second, *rib* (רִיב, "to contend, to file a lawsuit"), the technical covenant-lawsuit term that implies the possibility of a courtroom. Third, *mokhiach* (מוֹכִיחַ, 9:33), "arbiter" or "umpire" or "one who reaches a decision" — the book's most important missing figure, the mediator Job laments is unavailable. Fourth, *Rahab* (רַהַב) itself — the chaos-monster name. Fifth, *shevet* (שֵׁבֶט, 9:34), "rod," the instrument of the judge or the father, which Job asks God to remove before any trial can begin.

## Lens 2 — The Literature

Job 9 is Job's second major speech in the dialogues and his first extended response to Bildad. It is also the first speech where the book's core theological problem is stated with unmistakable clarity. The chapter divides into three rhetorical movements: a hymn of divine power (vv. 5-10), a protest of divine injustice (vv. 11-24), and a lament for the missing arbiter (vv. 25-35). The hymn is deliberately constructed as parody. It has the form of a doxology, with relative-clause participial construction ("who removes mountains... who shakes the earth... who commands the sun... who alone stretched out the heavens") identical to the doxologies of Amos 4:13, 5:8-9, and 9:5-6 — the so-called "Amos doxologies" that praise God's creative power. But Job's doxology is sarcastic. He is not worshipping; he is describing a power so total that no human can contest it, and contestation is precisely what he wants. Robert Alter, in *The Wisdom Books* (2010), observes that Job's hymn "turns the vocabulary of praise into the vocabulary of protest" — and that the same grammatical structure that in Amos establishes God's right to judge establishes in Job the impossibility of being judged fairly by such a power.

The genre is poetic dialogue, specifically a response-speech within the three-cycle pattern of chapters 3-27 (friend speaks; Job responds; repeat three times). The literary devices are dense. The chapter opens with a chiasm (v. 2: "How can a man be in the right before God? If one wished to contend with him, one could not answer once in a thousand times") that sets up the book's dilemma: righteousness is bounded by the impossibility of forensic contest. Verses 5-10 deploy *inclusio* through the repeated participle "who" (*ha-*), creating a litany. Verses 11-12 use rhetorical questions ("Who can turn him back? Who will say to him, 'What are you doing?'") that echo Isaiah 45:9 and will be answered — devastatingly — by the whirlwind speeches in chapters 38-41. Norman Habel, in *The Book of Job* (OTL, 1985), identifies verse 22 ("He destroys both the blameless and the wicked") as the book's central theological claim, the one line that the friends will never accept and that the narrative refuses to retract.

The most discussed textual crux is verse 33. The Masoretic text reads *lo yesh beinenu mokhiach* — "there is not between us an arbiter." But some Hebrew manuscripts and the Syriac Peshitta read *lu yesh beinenu mokhiach* — "would that there were between us an arbiter." The difference is one letter (א vs. ו) and a complete reversal of mood: the MT states a fact about the cosmos (there is no mediator); the alternative states a wish (would that there were one). Clines, in his three-volume Word Biblical Commentary, argues for the *lu* reading, seeing it as the hinge that opens into the book's increasingly desperate search for a heavenly advocate (which culminates in 16:19 and 19:25). Pope preserves the MT.

The chapter locates itself in the book's argument at the moment where Job shifts from personal lament (chapter 3) and measured protest (chapters 6-7) into full forensic challenge. Chapters 9 and 10 form a pair, both spoken to God directly rather than to the friends, and both functioning as Job's first explicit legal filing. The textual evidence at Qumran — 11QTargum Job, the Aramaic targum of Job discovered in Cave 11 in 1956 — preserves the arbiter passage with a slightly different inflection that suggests the ancient Aramaic interpreter already understood verse 33 as a lament for an absent mediator.

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

Job 9 sits in the wisdom corpus, but its deepest intertexts are the creation hymns (Psalms 8, 19, 104) and the covenant-lawsuit (Micah 6, Isaiah 1). Job is weaponizing the psalmic vocabulary of creation-praise against the psalmic assumption that the Creator is good to the creature.

### Jewish Interpretive Trajectory

**Second Temple.** The Testament of Job (1st century BC) radically transforms Job's speeches, removing most of the protest material from chapters 9-10 because the character of Job in later Hellenistic-Jewish piety had to be made patient. The 11QTargum Job from Qumran preserves the protest but softens the theological sharpness in the Aramaic, suggesting the tradition was already uneasy with Job's direct accusations.

**Rabbinic.** Bava Batra 16a reports Rava's astonishing reading: Job blasphemed in this chapter. The Talmud quotes verse 22 ("He destroys both the blameless and the wicked") and comments, "dust should be placed in the mouth of Job" for saying this. Another opinion in the same passage softens the offense: Job spoke out of affliction (*tza'ar*), and God does not hold the afflicted to their words. The divergent rabbinic responses map the book's internal tension. The midrash *Genesis Rabbah* 26.7 develops the Rahab allusion in verse 13, treating the chaos-helpers as the fallen angels of Genesis 6.

**Medieval.** Rashi (d. 1105) reads verse 22 minimally — it means only that suffering comes to both, not that divine justice is absent. The blow falls on good and bad; the reckoning is delayed, not abolished. Maimonides in *Guide for the Perplexed* III.22-23 reads Job 9 as the articulation of the Aristotelian position — that sublunar events are not ordered by divine providence to individuals but only to species. Job in Maimonides' scheme represents the view Maimonides wants to refute; the book's ultimate answer through Elihu and the whirlwind will be that providence does exist but is refined and pertains to intellectual perfection, not corporeal comfort. Nachmanides (Ramban) reads the absent arbiter as evidence that the *gilgul* (soul transmigration) framework is needed — Job's apparent injustice is accounting for sins in prior lives.

**Modern.** Jon Levenson treats 9:13 as the decisive window onto the Hebrew Bible's refusal to fully disenchant creation. The chaos-helpers of Rahab are not safely defeated; they are bowed but not eliminated. Avivah Zornberg (*The Murmuring Deep*) reads the chapter as trauma-speech: Job's argument does not cohere because coherent argument is not available to the traumatized. James Kugel (*The Great Shift*) treats verse 11 ("he passes by me, and I see him not") as the book's key theological transition — Job belongs to a world where the old "small god" of personal patronage has given way to a cosmic deity whose movements are beyond human detection.

### Christian Interpretive Trajectory

**Patristic.** Gregory the Great's *Moralia in Job* (c. 595) reads chapter 9 allegorically: Job stands for the Church lamenting the absence of Christ the Mediator, whose eventual incarnation will supply the missing *mokhiach*. The chapter becomes a prophecy of its own solution. Origen treats verse 33 as a pre-Christian yearning. Chrysostom preaches the chapter as patient protest that never crosses into sin because Job never actually curses — a reading the rabbis would find implausible.

**Medieval.** Aquinas's *Expositio super Iob* (c. 1261-65) handles verse 22 ("he destroys both the blameless and the wicked") by distinguishing temporal and eternal reward: God may allow temporal disaster to fall on the blameless because the reward awaits in the next life. Without eschatology, Thomas says, Job's complaint is unanswerable. The whole book is therefore a case for the necessity of resurrection.

**Reformation.** Calvin preached 159 sermons on Job between 1554 and 1555, and his reading of chapter 9 is the most uncomfortable in the Christian tradition. Calvin does not soften verse 22. He accepts that God's sovereignty means God does destroy both the blameless and the wicked, and that the creature has no standing to complain. Calvin's God in Job 9 is genuinely terrifying; the believer's task is not to understand but to submit. Luther's treatment is more anguished — he reads the absent arbiter as a statement of the human condition under the law, cured only by Christ the *mediator* of 1 Timothy 2:5.

**Critical.** Wellhausen and Gunkel read the chapter as evidence of the book's post-exilic theological crisis. Gerhard von Rad, in *Wisdom in Israel* (1970), treats verses 5-10 as a deliberate fracture of the wisdom-creation consensus of Proverbs 8. Clines's Word Biblical Commentary is the definitive modern philological treatment. Newsom's dialogical reading treats the chapter's three movements as representing three incompatible voices that Job cannot reconcile — the hymnic, the protesting, and the lamenting.

**Contemporary.** Gustavo Gutiérrez reads verse 24 ("The earth is given into the hand of the wicked") as the book's moment of prophetic solidarity with the oppressed — Job has moved from personal injury to structural analysis. Ellen Davis reads the chapter's creation vocabulary as mourning for a cosmos that no longer functions as advertised. Susannah Ticciati (*Job and the Disruption of Identity*) treats verses 30-31 ("if I wash myself with snow... yet you will plunge me into a pit") as Job's discovery that self-justification is structurally impossible under the conditions of divine pursuit.

### Where the Traditions Converge and Diverge

Both traditions recognize that Job 9 articulates the deepest theological problem in the wisdom corpus: the asymmetry between Creator and creature forecloses any possibility of contested justice. Both see verse 33 (the missing arbiter) as the book's central cry. But the traditions diverge on the solution. Christian tradition, from Gregory through Aquinas to Calvin, resolves the missing mediator Christologically — Christ is the *mokhiach* Job laments. This reading is deeply satisfying and deeply problematic: it collapses the question the book refuses to collapse. Jewish tradition, with its reluctance to discharge the problem into the afterlife or into a second divine figure, keeps the ache open. The absent arbiter in the Jewish reading is a permanent structural feature of the human relation to God — one that Job 9 articulates but does not overcome, and that the book's divine speeches in chapters 38-41 will also not overcome.

## Lens 4 — The Theological Intent

The author of Job 9 is constructing what Newsom calls a "failed doxology" — a hymn that, by its completeness, proves the impossibility of worship as the speaker experiences it. The theological vision is not merely that God is powerful; it is that God's power is such that human justice-claims cannot reach him, and therefore the whole apparatus of wisdom-theology (piety yields prosperity, sin yields suffering) collapses. The chapter is the first moment in the book where the retributive framework of the friends is not merely disputed but shown to be *unnecessary* — if God destroys the blameless and the wicked alike, then the categories the friends rely on have no cosmic purchase.

### Tension 1 — Is Job's Complaint Blasphemy or Faithfulness?

This is the oldest question in the reception of Job 9, and the answer shapes everything.

**Rabbinic.** Bava Batra 16a preserves both readings: Rava says Job blasphemed and "dust should be in his mouth"; another opinion says the afflicted are excused by their affliction. The rabbinic tradition will not settle the question.

**Catholic.** The Catechism of the Catholic Church does not directly address Job 9, but the magisterial tradition reads Job's complaint as a permitted and even exemplary form of lament, citing the patristic reading of James 5:11 ("you have heard of the steadfastness of Job"). Aquinas in the *Summa Theologiae* II-II.39 treats Job's complaint as a natural reaction to suffering that does not constitute sin so long as the creature does not deny divine providence absolutely. The blameless complaint is licit.

**Reformed.** Calvin, in his 34th sermon on Job, warns against too-easy vindication of Job's protest. Job sins in this chapter, Calvin argues — specifically in verse 22, where he universalizes his own experience into a cosmic claim against God's justice. Calvin's Job is a holy man who fails at a specific point, and the book's later rebuke of him in 38:2 ("Who is this that darkens counsel by words without knowledge?") vindicates Calvin's reading.

**Critical.** Newsom and Habel refuse the binary. The book is polyphonic; Job's protest is neither blasphemy nor paradigmatic faith but a genuine voice that the book's own structure preserves without subordinating. The epilogue's verdict — that Job has "spoken of me what is right" (42:7) unlike the friends — suggests the narrator sides with Job's honesty over the friends' orthodoxy, though the rebuke in 38:2 complicates this.

**Steelmanning Calvin.** The reading most modern readers resist is Calvin's: that Job sins in his protest and that the book's rebuke in 38:2 is merited. Calvin would say: any reading that leaves Job's accusation against divine justice uncorrected has made God accountable to the creature, which is the creature's fundamental category error. The Jobian complaint is powerful precisely because it is wrong, and the whirlwind speeches are God's refusal to concede the ground Job demands. A reading that makes Job a straightforward hero has domesticated the book's scandal — which is that even the faithful sufferer falls short of the cosmic standard he demands.

**Where this leaves us:** The book does not clearly adjudicate between blasphemy and faithfulness. It preserves both possibilities by making 42:7 and 38:2 both true at once. The reader is asked to hold them together.

### Tension 2 — Is There an Arbiter, and If So, Who?

The *mokhiach* of verse 33 is the book's deepest longing and its most contested theological figure.

**Christian.** From Gregory the Great onward, the *mokhiach* is Christ — the mediator between God and humanity of 1 Timothy 2:5, prefigured in Job's lament. The New Testament connection becomes explicit in Handel's Messiah, which sets Job 19:25 ("I know that my Redeemer lives") as the paradigmatic resurrection chorus. For Aquinas, the missing mediator is precisely what the Incarnation supplies, and Job's complaint is resolved at Bethlehem.

**Jewish.** The rabbis do not accept the Christological reading. Rashi treats the absent arbiter as evidence of human limitation, not as a prophecy of a divine mediator. Maimonides reads the desire for an arbiter as a failure of philosophical maturity; the intellectually perfected soul does not need a mediator because it grasps the necessary non-correspondence between divine justice and human experience. Levenson treats the cry for the arbiter as the book's honest acknowledgment that monotheism, having collapsed the ancient divine council into one God, has also collapsed the court of appeal.

**Historical-critical.** Clines treats the *mokhiach* as a literary device, not a theological proposal. Job is voicing the logical gap in the friends' forensic model — if God is both plaintiff and judge, the model fails — but the book is not advocating for a second divine figure. The theophany of chapters 38-41 answers the problem not by providing a mediator but by dissolving the forensic frame entirely.

**Steelmanning the Christian reading.** The reading many modern scholars resist is the Christological one. The steelman: the *mokhiach* longing is so structurally central to the Hebrew cry of the innocent sufferer that the New Testament's claim that Christ is the mediator is not a forced allegory but the natural resolution of a question the Hebrew text raises without answering. If the question is real, the traditional Christian answer is the most developed, and the Jewish refusal to accept it leaves the ache open — which, the steelman goes, is why Jewish philosophy from Maimonides onward has had to argue against the natural force of Job 9:33.

**Where this leaves us:** Every tradition reads this verse as one of the most important in the book. No tradition is able to leave it alone. That the verse generates incommensurable theological conclusions is probably the most honest thing one can say about it.

### One Unresolved Question

If there is no arbiter — no third party who can lay a hand on God and on you both — then what exactly is prayer doing? Is it argument, or catharsis, or something else that has no name because our category of address assumes a symmetrical court?

## The Thread

- **Psalm 8:4** — "What is man that you are mindful of him?" Psalm 8 asks the question as praise. Job has already asked it as accusation (Job 7:17), and here in chapter 9 he does not even bother — the answer is, humans are what God passes over unseen (9:11). When you read Psalm 8 in the wake of Job 9, the sweet rhetorical question curdles.
- **Isaiah 45:9** — "Woe to him who strives with his Maker, an earthen vessel with the potter!" Isaiah condemns the posture that Job 9 adopts. The prophet and the wisdom poet point in opposite directions, and the canon preserves both without reconciling them. When you reach Isaiah, remember that Job 9 was in the same scroll.
- **1 Timothy 2:5** — "For there is one God, and there is one mediator (*mesites*) between God and men, the man Christ Jesus." The Greek *mesites* is the Septuagint's word for the Hebrew *mokhiach* Job says does not exist. The New Testament's most confident theological sentence is the mirror image of Job 9:33. Read them together and hear the Christian tradition's deepest instinct: that Job's absence is the New Testament's presence.
- **Hebrews 9:15** — "Therefore he is the mediator of a new covenant." The word is *mesites* again. The Letter to the Hebrews is more relentlessly preoccupied with Job's question than any other New Testament book, even though it does not cite Job by name. When you get to Hebrews, notice how often the author reaches for the vocabulary of arbiter, guarantor, forerunner — and ask whether the Hebrew wisdom tradition's unanswered longing is what the letter is trying to answer.
