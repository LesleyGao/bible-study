# 1 Kings 12: The Division and the Calves

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

The chapter is the dtr's pivot — the central historical event the entire Deuteronomistic History exists to explain. The geography is significant. Rehoboam goes to Shechem (*Šəkem*, שְׁכֶם, modern Tell Balata, on the eastern side of modern Nablus) to be made king. This is not Jerusalem. Shechem is the old central-tribal sanctuary — the site of Joshua's covenant renewal in Joshua 24, of Abimelech's abortive monarchy in Judges 9, of Jacob's altar in Genesis 33. By going to Shechem, Rehoboam is performing a constitutive act of northern legitimation: he is asking the northern tribes to ratify his kingship over them at *their* covenantal center, not summoning them to Jerusalem to recognize a Davidic fait accompli. The choice is itself a tacit admission that the Davidic-Solomonic monarchy's grip on the north was always conditional. Tell Balata's archaeology, excavated by Ernst Sellin, G. Ernest Wright, and Edward Campbell, attests to a major Iron Age IIA fortified city — the right scale for a tribal-assembly center — although the fortification stratigraphy and the relationship to the Migdal-temple complex remain debated (compare Lawrence Stager's reassessments).

The "old men who had stood before Solomon" (12:6) versus "the young men who had grown up with him" (12:8) is a sociologically precise distinction. The Hebrew for "young men" is *yəlādîm* (יְלָדִים) — literally "boys" or "children" — a deliberately diminishing term that signals the dtr's evaluation. These are not advisors of equal standing; they are the courtier cohort raised in palatial luxury without formative experience of governance. The vocabulary of "elders" (*zəqēnîm*) versus "boys" (*yəlādîm*) deploys a venerable ANE wisdom-literary topos: the contrast between accumulated experience and reckless youth is a stock motif in Egyptian Instructions (Ptahhotep, Amenemope) and Mesopotamian wisdom (the Counsels of Wisdom). Cogan, Sweeney, and Walsh all read the chapter's framing as the dtr's wisdom-failure indictment — Rehoboam fails the wisdom test his father aced in 1 Kings 3.

The "yoke" (*ʿōl*, עֹל) and "scorpions" (*ʿaqrabbîm*, עַקְרַבִּים) image-cluster is rich. The yoke is an animal-husbandry metaphor for the labor-tax (*mas*) and corvée system Solomon used to build the Temple, the palace complex, and the Millo (cf. 1 Kings 5:13-18, 9:15-23). The scorpions are most plausibly a kind of barbed lash — a whip with metal or bone-tipped thongs, attested in Egyptian and Mesopotamian art as instruments of judicial flogging. Some readers (going back to the medieval commentators) understand them as live scorpions or scorpion-shaped rods, but the comparison to the standard *šôṭîm* ("whips") in the same verse argues for an intensified flogging instrument.

The northern secession-cry of verse 16 — "What portion do we have in David? We have no inheritance in the son of Jesse. To your tents, O Israel! Look now to your own house, David!" — is verbally near-identical to 2 Samuel 20:1, the slogan of Sheba ben Bichri's failed northern revolt during David's reign. The repetition is not coincidence; the dtr is signaling that the fault line briefly opened in Sheba's revolt (and earlier in the Saul-David transition narrative of 2 Samuel 2-5) has now opened permanently. Marc Brettler in *The Creation of History in Ancient Israel* and Steven McKenzie's *King David: A Biography* both treat the Sheba-Rehoboam slogan-link as evidence that northern resentment of Davidic Judahite hegemony was structural, not occasional.

The calf-sanctuaries at Bethel and Dan introduce the chapter's archaeological richest material. **Tel Dan** (modern Tel el-Qadi at the foot of Mount Hermon) was excavated continuously from 1966 by Avraham Biran (Hebrew Union College excavations); his findings include a massive Iron Age II *bāmâ* (high place / cultic platform) measuring some 18 by 19 meters, a four-horned altar fragment, a horned-altar courtyard, a Sacred Precinct (*temenos*) wall, and the famous Tel Dan Stele (1993-1994 discoveries) referencing the "house of David" — the earliest extra-biblical attestation of the Davidic dynasty in Old Aramaic, dated mid-ninth century BCE. The stratigraphy supports a Jeroboamic foundation phase (Stratum IVA/Iron IIA) with subsequent Omride and post-Omride elaboration. **Bethel** (commonly identified with modern Beitin, though Finkelstein and others have argued for el-Bireh) was excavated by William F. Albright and James L. Kelso (1934, 1954, 1957, 1960); the results were published in *The Excavation of Bethel* (1968). The Bethel sanctuary's archaeological signature is more contested than Dan's, with Finkelstein arguing the major sanctuary phase is later than Jeroboam I, but the literary tradition's identification of Bethel as a major royal sanctuary is clear from Amos 7:13 ("Bethel is the king's sanctuary, a temple of the kingdom").

The "calves" (*ʿăgālîm*, עֲגָלִים) themselves have been debated for over a century. Were they idols of bulls, with Israel committing straightforward bovine idolatry as in Exodus 32? Or were they bull-pedestals — divine throne-bases on which the invisible YHWH was understood to stand, parallel to the Levantine iconography in which Baal-Hadad stands on the back of a bull? The pedestal interpretation has been argued by Mark S. Smith in *The Early History of God* and *The Origins of Biblical Monotheism*, by John Day in *Yahweh and the Gods and Goddesses of Canaan*, and by Daniel Fleming's iconographic work. The argument: West Semitic theophanic art consistently shows storm-deities standing on bulls; bull-pedestals are the standard "throne" iconography in Levantine cultic furniture (compare the Phoenician *El*-on-bull cults and the Ugaritic Baal cycle's bull-imagery). Jeroboam, on this reading, did not introduce idols — he introduced a theologically ambiguous form of YHWH-worship using inherited Canaanite iconographic conventions, and the dtr's polemic deliberately collapses this distinction by quoting the Aaronic golden-calf words ("Behold your gods, O Israel" — Exodus 32:4) into Jeroboam's mouth. Other scholars (notably Frank Moore Cross and his students) have been more cautious about the pedestal hypothesis, noting that the dtr's explicit characterization is of idolatry pure and simple. Both readings are exegetically live.

A few more Hebrew terms. *Bāmôt* (בָּמוֹת, "high places," verse 31) — the dtr's standard pejorative for non-Jerusalem cult sites. *Mas* (מַס, "forced labor," verse 18) — the corvée system whose harshness produces the secession. *Ḥag* (חַג, "feast," verses 32-33) — the festival Jeroboam institutes "in the eighth month" (= October-November) one month later than the southern Feast of Tabernacles in the seventh month, an explicit ritual divergence the dtr highlights as a sin.

## Lens 2 — The Literature

The chapter is the dtr's structural fulcrum. Knoppers in *Two Nations Under God* describes it as the central event the entire DH exists to explain — why does YHWH's covenant people exist as two kingdoms rather than one? The chapter's literary architecture is panel-by-panel: the Shechem assembly and the *yəlādîm*-counsel disaster (1-15), the secession proper with its slogan and Adoram's stoning (16-20), the prophetic prevention of civil war via Shemaiah (21-24), and the Jeroboamic apostasy at Bethel-Dan (25-33). The chapter binds together political-historical narrative with theological-cultic indictment in a way that is the dtr's distinctive compositional mode.

Robert Alter, Meir Sternberg, and Jan Fokkelman have all written incisively on the chapter's narrative artistry. Alter notes the deft staging of the *yəlādîm*-scene: Rehoboam's question to the elders is sober and exploratory ("How do you advise me to answer this people?"); his question to the *yəlādîm* is already tilted ("What do you advise that we answer this people who have said to me..."). The first-person plural ("we") signals identification with the cohort; the framing assumes the people's complaint is illegitimate. The narrator is showing us a king whose mind is already made up, going through the motions of consultation. Sternberg in *The Poetics of Biblical Narrative* notes that verse 15's parenthetical theological evaluation ("for it was a turn of affairs brought about by the LORD that he might fulfill his word") is the narrator's intervention to assert dual causality — the events are simultaneously human folly and divine providence, with no contradiction. Fokkelman's *Narrative Art and Poetry in the Books of Samuel* developed the methodological tools that have been applied to this chapter's tight literary structure.

The chapter is unusually rich in literary devices. The "scorpions" line (verses 11, 14) is delivered twice — once in the *yəlādîm*'s coaching, once in Rehoboam's actual speech. The repetition is rhetorically devastating: the narrator stages Rehoboam parroting the line he has been fed, with no editorial improvement, no modulation, no political instinct. The double "yoke" / "scorpions" couplet has a chiastic compression that makes it sound proverbial — and indeed it has functioned as proverb in subsequent reception (the phrase "scorpions, not whips" entered late medieval and Renaissance political discourse via the Vulgate). The Sheba-slogan in verse 16 is allusion-as-prophecy: by citing the language of an earlier failed revolt, the narrator marks this moment as the one where the previously contained fault line opens permanently.

Textual variants are again significant. The LXX of 3 Kingdoms 12 contains the so-called "Alternative Jeroboam Account" at 12:24a-z — a substantially different telling of Jeroboam's career, his Egyptian sojourn, his Ahijah encounter, and his return. Adrian Schenker's *Septante et texte massorétique dans l'histoire la plus ancienne du texte de 1 Rois 2-14* is the major scholarly treatment; Trebolle Barrera, Knoppers, and Aejmelaeus have built on it. The Alternative Account places the prophetic mandate to Jeroboam differently and includes material on Jeroboam's mother Sarira and his Egyptian wife Ano. The implications are debated: Schenker argues LXX preserves an earlier source which MT has redactionally reworked; others (Percy Van Keulen) argue MT is the older form and LXX is a later expansion. Either way, the variant ordering testifies to the chapter's centrality — multiple traditions of Jeroboam's career circulated, and the dtr's editorial work was substantial.

The chapter also engages 2 Chronicles 10's parallel rendering. The Chronicler reproduces the secession narrative but truncates the Jeroboamic apostasy material — the calves, the high places, the festival — almost entirely. The Chronicler is interested in Judah; the northern kingdom barely exists for him after the schism. The contrast highlights the dtr's deliberate inclusion of the apostasy narrative: the dtr is committed to an account that explains both kingdoms' eventual destruction.

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

In Jewish tradition the chapter has been read as a study in political-theological catastrophe. Josephus (*Ant.* 8.211-229) follows the narrative closely and treats Rehoboam's youthful counselors as a paradigm of bad advising. The rabbinic tradition is divided on Jeroboam: some (e.g., *b. Sanhedrin* 101b-102a) treat him as the paradigm of the king who "sinned and caused others to sin" (his name becomes shorthand for cultic apostasy throughout 1-2 Kings — "the sins of Jeroboam"); others note his prophetic mandate from Ahijah (1 Kings 11) and his initial divine sanction. Rashi's commentary follows the Talmudic harshness toward Jeroboam. Radak engages the textual evidence carefully and acknowledges Jeroboam's political dilemma. Ralbag offers a rationalist analysis of the calf-establishment as Jeroboam's misjudged solution to a real political problem (cult-pilgrimage to Jerusalem would erode his political base).

Don Isaac Abarbanel's commentary on 1 Kings 12 is one of the great pieces of political-theological writing in the Jewish tradition. Abarbanel, who served at the courts of Portugal, Castile, and Naples and wrote his Kings commentary after expulsion from Spain, treats the chapter as a meditation on the structural causes of political division. He argues that the northern grievance was legitimate — Solomon's labor-tax was oppressive, and Rehoboam's failure was not merely personal folly but the failure of a regime that had ceased to understand its own legitimating conditions. Abarbanel anticipates modern political-theory readings (compare Michael Walzer, *In God's Shadow*) by treating the kingdom's division as a constitutional rather than merely moral event. Avivah Zornberg, Yair Zakovitch's source-critical work (*Jacob: Unexpected Patriarch* and his Kings work), and Moshe Garsiel all build on this tradition of structural-political reading.

Christian reception is layered. The Patristics tend to read the chapter typologically. Origen treats Rehoboam as a type of the synagogue rejecting the *yoke* of the law's mercy and being torn from its Davidic inheritance. Augustine in *City of God* 17.21 reads the division as the working of providence — YHWH's hand in the disaster. Ambrose's *De Apologia David Altera* extracts political wisdom from Rehoboam's failure. Chrysostom's homilies on Kings (preserved fragmentarily) treat the chapter as a study in the perils of bad counsel. Theodoret of Cyrus in *Quaestiones in Reges* engages the narrative with characteristic philological care. Gregory the Great's *Moralia* treats Rehoboam as the great example of the ruler who listens to flatterers. Aquinas (*Summa* II-II) takes Rehoboam's failure as an instance of the cardinal vice of pride uncoupled from prudence.

The Reformation deepened the political reading. Calvin's *Sermons on 1 Kings* preached at Geneva engage the chapter with Calvin's characteristic political-theological seriousness; he treats the chapter as a study in the limits of legitimate authority and the responsibility of rulers to listen to their people. Calvin's reading carries forward into the Reformed political theology of Beza and Junius Brutus's *Vindiciae Contra Tyrannos*, which uses Rehoboam as a paradigmatic case for the conditional nature of political authority. Luther's lectures and table talk return repeatedly to Rehoboam as the warning case against princes who scorn their counselors.

In modern critical scholarship, the chapter is foundational. Frank Moore Cross's double-redaction theory locates the chapter's anti-Jeroboam polemic primarily in the Josianic redaction (Dtr1), which had a stake in delegitimizing the northern cult to support Josiah's reform. Richard Nelson's *The Double Redaction of the Deuteronomistic History* refines the source-criticism. Mordechai Cogan's *I Kings* (AB) is the indispensable historical-philological reference, with detailed treatment of LXX variants and ANE parallels. Marvin Sweeney's *I & II Kings* (OTL) treats the chapter as the dtr's central explanatory move. Gary Knoppers's *Two Nations Under God* is the foundational modern monograph on the chapter, arguing that the dtr's account of the division is theologically more nuanced than the standard reading admits — Jeroboam is not pure villain, and Rehoboam is not pure victim. The dtr is staging a tragedy with structural causes. Walter Brueggemann's *1 & 2 Kings*, Iain Provan's *1 & 2 Kings* (NIBC), Davis (*1 Kings: The Wisdom and the Folly*), Goldingay's *Old Testament Theology*, and Eugene Peterson's pastoral readings all return to the chapter's political-theological gravity. On the calves specifically, Mark S. Smith's *The Early History of God* and John Day's *Yahweh and the Gods and Goddesses of Canaan* are essential — both argue for the bull-pedestal reading, treating Jeroboam's calves as a Levantine-iconographic theophanic stand rather than idolatry simpliciter.

## Lens 4 — The Theological Intent

**Tension One: Is the division of the kingdom YHWH's punitive judgment, YHWH's redemptive providence, or both?**

Position one: punitive judgment. The text of verse 15 is unambiguous: the disastrous turn was "from the LORD that he might fulfill his word, which the LORD spoke by Ahijah the Shilonite to Jeroboam." The division is the execution of the judgment announced in 11:11-13 against Solomon's apostasy. Rehoboam's folly is the means; the judgment is the cause. Augustine, Calvin, and the dtr-as-historian (Cogan, Sweeney) all hold variations of this reading.

Position two: providential preservation. The division is YHWH's way of preserving the Davidic-Zionic promise *while* honoring the legitimate northern grievance. By splitting the kingdom, YHWH preserves Judah and Jerusalem (where the Temple stands and the Davidic line continues) while granting the north's claim to autonomy. The reservation in 11:36 ("that David my servant may always have a lamp before me in Jerusalem") is the load-bearing theological point. Jon Levenson's work on Davidic-Zionic theology supports this reading.

Position three: structural-political tragedy under providence. Knoppers, Brueggemann, and Abarbanel: the division is genuinely tragic — it is YHWH's act, but YHWH is acting through political-structural causes (Solomon's imperial overreach, Rehoboam's *yəlādîm*-folly, the legitimate northern grievance) that have their own moral weight. The narrative refuses to choose between divine sovereignty and human responsibility; both are real, both are operative.

Steelman of position three: the chapter's craft is precisely its refusal of single-cause explanation. Verse 15 asserts divine causation; verses 1-14 narrate human folly with full sociological precision. The dtr is not naive about either — and the integration is the theological point. Reducing the chapter to either pure divine judgment or pure human folly misses the dtr's distinctive theological vision.

Where this leaves us: position three is the most exegetically defensible. The chapter holds dual causation as a theological commitment and refuses to collapse it. The pastoral implication is that political catastrophe is rarely traceable to single causes — divine providence operates through and despite human agency, and the moral responsibility of human actors is not diminished by the fact that YHWH is also at work.

**Tension Two: How should we evaluate Jeroboam's calves — pragmatic political solution, theologically ambiguous YHWH-worship, or unambiguous idolatry?**

Position one: unambiguous idolatry. The dtr's quotation of Aaron's words ("Behold your gods, O Israel, who brought you up out of the land of Egypt" — verse 28, exactly Exodus 32:4) frames Jeroboam's act as a recapitulation of the Sinai apostasy. The calves are idols; the worship is idolatry; "the sin of Jeroboam" is shorthand for the gravest cultic violation in 1-2 Kings.

Position two: theologically ambiguous YHWH-worship using Levantine iconographic conventions (Mark S. Smith, John Day, Daniel Fleming). Jeroboam is establishing YHWH-worship at Bethel and Dan with bull-pedestal iconography — the bulls function as throne-bases for the invisible YHWH, parallel to the Baal-on-bull iconography. This is not idolatry by Jeroboam's own self-understanding; it is YHWH-worship by means of inherited Canaanite cultic conventions. The dtr's polemic flattens this distinction for theological purposes.

Position three: pragmatic political compromise that became theologically corrosive. Jeroboam faced a real political problem: pilgrimage to Jerusalem would erode his political base. His solution — alternative cultic centers at Bethel and Dan — was a defensible political move, but his execution (the calves, the non-Levitical priesthood, the alternative festival calendar) drifted from acceptable cultic divergence into structural apostasy. The chapter is tragic in showing how legitimate political pressure produces theologically ruinous accommodations.

Steelman of position two: the bull-pedestal iconography is well-attested in the Levantine archaeological record; Jeroboam was working within a cultic visual grammar he and his audience understood. The dtr's assimilation to Sinai-apostasy is rhetorical, not historical. Mark S. Smith's iconographic argument is strong.

Where this leaves us: positions two and three are not opposed. Jeroboam may have intended YHWH-worship via Levantine iconography (position two), and the result was nevertheless a slow drift into syncretistic or outright idolatrous practice (position three). Position one is the dtr's theological evaluation, which has its own integrity even if it elides historical-iconographic complexity. The chapter's pastoral force comes from holding all three: good intentions plus political pressure produced a settlement that was theologically corrosive in the long run, regardless of the founder's intent.

Unresolved question: was Jeroboam's failure inevitable given his political situation, or could a different ruler have made the schism cohere with covenant faithfulness? The text gives him a real prophetic mandate (11:38) — "if you will listen to all that I command you... I will be with you and will build you a sure house." The conditional was real. The text does not say what a faithful Jeroboam would have looked like — it only narrates the failure.

## The Thread

The chapter looks back to 1 Kings 11:11-13, 26-40 (Ahijah's prophecy and Jeroboam's commissioning), to 2 Samuel 20:1 (Sheba's slogan, now repeated as permanent secession), to Joshua 24 and Judges 9 (Shechem's covenantal-tribal centrality), and decisively to Exodus 32:4 (the golden calf, whose words Jeroboam recapitulates). Forward, the chapter sets up the "sins of Jeroboam" formula that will recur across 1 Kings 13-2 Kings 17 as the dtr's stock indictment of every northern king; it sets up Hosea's prophetic ministry against Bethel cult ("Israel has spurned the good" — Hosea 8:5-6 on the calves); it sets up Amos's confrontation at Bethel with Amaziah (Amos 7:10-17); it sets up 2 Kings 17's exilic explanation of the northern kingdom's destruction; and it sets up 2 Kings 23:15-20's Josianic destruction of the Bethel altar — a destruction that the next chapter (1 Kings 13) will explicitly prophesy by name three centuries in advance. The chapter also anchors the New Testament's Samaritan-Jewish division (the woman at the well in John 4 inhabits the religious geography 1 Kings 12 created) and Stephen's speech in Acts 7. Knoppers is right: this chapter is the central historical event the entire Deuteronomistic History exists to explain, and its echoes run through both testaments.
