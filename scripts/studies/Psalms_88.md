# Psalm 88 — Going Deeper

Psalm 88 is the darkest text in the Hebrew Bible. It is a lament that does not turn. The psalmist enters the prayer in despair and exits in despair; the customary movement of biblical lament from complaint to confidence to praise is structurally refused. The Hebrew text closes with a single word — *machshakh*, "darkness" — and there is nothing after it. Walter Brueggemann famously called Ps 88 "an embarrassment to conventional faith." It is also, perhaps for that reason, one of the most pastorally important psalms in the canon. It is the psalm that grants permission. It is the warrant in Scripture for faith that does not resolve.

## Lens 1: Historical World

**Superscription.** *Shir mizmor livne-Korach lamnatseach al-machalat le'annot maskil le-Heman ha-Ezrachi* (שִׁיר מִזְמוֹר לִבְנֵי קֹרַח לַמְנַצֵּחַ עַל־מָחֲלַת לְעַנּוֹת מַשְׂכִּיל לְהֵימָן הָאֶזְרָחִי). The longest superscription in the Psalter combines five elements: a song-psalm of the Sons of Korah, addressed to the choirmaster, set to the tune *Mahalath Leannoth* (perhaps "concerning the suffering of affliction"), classified as a *maskil* (a contemplative or instructional psalm), and attributed to **Heman the Ezrahite**. Heman is named in 1 Chr 6:33 and 1 Chr 25:1–7 as one of the three Levitical chief musicians appointed by David (alongside Asaph and Ethan/Jeduthun). 1 Kings 4:31 lists Heman the Ezrahite among the wisdom figures whose wisdom Solomon surpassed, alongside Ethan the Ezrahite (the named author of Ps 89). The pairing is deliberate: Pss 88 and 89, by Heman and Ethan respectively, close Book III of the Psalter in unresolved lament.

**Gunkel's form-critical genre.** Hermann Gunkel classified Ps 88 as an *Klagelied des Einzelnen* — an Individual Lament — but also recognized it as the most extreme example of the genre, the one that breaks the form. Standard individual laments (Pss 3, 13, 22, 31, 42–43) follow a recognizable arc: invocation, complaint, petition, expression of trust, vow of praise. Ps 88 contains the first three movements but refuses the fourth and fifth. There is no turn to trust; there is no vow of praise. Claus Westermann (*The Praise of God in the Psalms*, 1965) and Patrick Miller (*They Cried to the Lord*, 1994) both treat Ps 88 as the limit-case of lament, the place where the form is pressed to its breaking-point and held there.

**Date.** Impossible to fix with confidence. The vocabulary — *Sheol*, *Abaddon*, "the Pit" (*bor*), "the land of forgetfulness" (*erets neshiyyah*) — belongs to a stable Israelite cosmology that spans the monarchic and post-exilic periods. The psalmist describes lifelong affliction (v. 15: "afflicted and close to death from my youth up"), which suggests an individual situation rather than a national crisis. Most commentators (Kraus, Tate, Goldingay, Hossfeld–Zenger) place it in the late monarchic or early post-exilic period; some have proposed a leper or a chronically ill sufferer; some have read it as a more general voice for any deathbed. Gerald Janzen (*At the Scent of Water*, 2009) reads it as a literary composition that refuses to be pinned to a single historical situation precisely so that it can serve as the prayer-of-last-resort for any sufferer.

**ANE parallels.** Mesopotamian lament-literature ("Ludlul bel nemeqi" — "I will praise the lord of wisdom"; the Babylonian Theodicy; the Sumerian "Man and His God") shares with Ps 88 the vocabulary of divine wrath, undeserved suffering, and the silence of heaven. What sets Ps 88 apart is the absence of resolution. Ludlul bel nemeqi ends with the sufferer restored and Marduk praised; the Babylonian Theodicy ends with at least a rhetorical concession. Ps 88 ends in darkness. The closest ANE parallel may be the Egyptian "Dispute of a Man with His Ba" (also called "The Man Who Was Tired of Life"), but even that text gives the soul-figure a voice of consolation. The Israelite lament is alone in refusing the consolation.

**Hebrew terms.** A vocabulary of death organizes the psalm.

- *Sheol* (שְׁאוֹל, v. 3) — the underworld, the gathering-place of all the dead.
- *Bor* (בּוֹר, vv. 4, 6) — "the pit"; literal cistern but here the grave-pit.
- *Avaddon* (אֲבַדּוֹן, v. 11) — "destruction"; the deepest region of Sheol, paired here with Sheol as a poetic intensification (cf. Job 26:6; Prov 15:11; 27:20).
- *Erets neshiyyah* (אֶרֶץ נְשִׁיָּה, v. 12) — "the land of forgetfulness"; a unique phrase in the Hebrew Bible, occurring only here, denoting the place where one is forgotten by God and humans alike.
- *Machashakhim* (מַחֲשַׁכִּים, v. 6) and *machshakh* (מַחְשָׁךְ, v. 18) — "dark places," "darkness." The same root brackets the central section.
- *Hirchaqta mimmenni ohev va-rea meyudda'ai machshakh* (הִרְחַקְתָּ מִמֶּנִּי אֹהֵב וָרֵעַ מְיֻדָּעַי מַחְשָׁךְ, v. 18) — literally "you have caused beloved and friend to be far from me; my acquaintances are darkness." *Machshakh* is the literal final word of the Hebrew text. The psalm ends in a single noun: darkness.
- *Charon* and *charonekha* (חֲרוֹנְךָ, v. 16) — "your burning anger." The psalmist attributes the suffering directly to God's wrath. There is no third-party adversary; the *adversary* in this psalm is God.
- *Sela* — appears twice (vv. 7, 10), marking the conventional musical pauses but here marking pauses *inside* the despair, not transitions toward hope.

**DSS / LXX.** Ps 88 is preserved in 4QPsa and several other Qumran fragments; no significant variants from the MT. The LXX (Ps 87 in its numbering) translates the closing word as σκότος ("darkness") and preserves the abrupt non-resolution. The Vulgate likewise keeps the closing word as *tenebrae*. Across the textual tradition, no scribal hand has tried to soften the ending. The darkness is preserved.

## Lens 2: The Literature

**Lowth's parallelism.** All three Lowthian categories appear, but Ps 88 leans on synonymous parallelism stacked into intensifying chains. v. 5: "like one set loose among the dead / like the slain that lie in the grave / like those whom you remember no more" — three synonymous lines piling death-image on death-image. The accumulation is the point: the psalmist is not making one comparison; he is exhausting the language of the grave.

**Strophic structure.** Most commentators divide the psalm into three strophes corresponding to the three petitionary cycles, marked by the two *selah* notations (vv. 7, 10):

1. **Strophe 1 (vv. 1–7):** Invocation and the descent into Sheol.
2. **Strophe 2 (vv. 8–10):** Social isolation and the rhetorical questions about the dead.
3. **Strophe 3 (vv. 11–18):** The unanswered "why" and the final word of darkness.

Some readers (Goldingay, Hossfeld–Zenger) prefer a four-strophe division; others note the threefold "I cry" structure (vv. 1–2, 9, 13) that organizes the psalm around three failed attempts to be heard.

**Inclusio.** The psalm opens with the speaker crying *day and night* (v. 1: "I cry out day and night before you") and intensifies in the morning (v. 13: "in the morning my prayer comes before you"); the temporal language of dawn and dusk frames the prayer. But the inclusio that matters most is the inclusion of *darkness*: the speaker's eyes grow dim with sorrow (v. 9) and the psalm closes with companions become *darkness* (v. 18). Light is failing throughout. Where Ps 30 ends with "weeping may tarry for the night, but joy comes with the morning," Ps 88's morning comes (v. 13) and brings no joy.

**The structural refusal.** The most important literary feature of Ps 88 is what is *not* there. Compare it to Ps 13, the textbook short lament:
- vv. 1–2 complaint ("How long, O LORD?")
- vv. 3–4 petition ("Consider and answer me")
- vv. 5–6 *turn to trust and praise* ("But I have trusted in your steadfast love; my heart shall rejoice in your salvation")

Ps 88 contains the first two movements many times over. The third movement never arrives. The psalm ends mid-lament. This structural refusal is the literary signature of the text — the form itself preaches.

**Chiasmus.** Walter Brueggemann (*The Message of the Psalms*, 1984) and Patrick Miller (*They Cried to the Lord*, 1994) both note a rough chiastic centering on the rhetorical questions of vv. 10–12 ("Do you work wonders for the dead? Do the departed rise up to praise you?"). The questions are not rhetorical embellishments; they are the theological core. The psalmist is saying: if you let me die, you lose a worshipper. The question hangs without answer.

**Selah as silence.** Erich Zenger (*A God of Vengeance?*) reads the two *selah* markers in Ps 88 as *intensifying* silences rather than transitional ones. They do not mark a turn; they mark a held breath inside despair.

## Lens 3: The Larger Story

**Book III's closing arc.** Book III of the Psalter (Pss 73–89) is the darkest of the five books. It opens with Asaph's crisis-of-faith (Ps 73), works through national disasters and royal failures, and closes with two unresolved laments: Ps 88 (individual) and Ps 89 (royal-covenant). Gerald Wilson's canonical-shape analysis (*The Editing of the Hebrew Psalter*, 1985) treats this closing pair as the deliberate hinge of the entire Psalter — Books I–III plot the rise and tragic collapse of the Davidic-messianic kingdom; Books IV–V respond. David Mitchell (*The Message of the Psalter*, 1997) extends Wilson's reading eschatologically. Ps 88's individual despair sits next to Ps 89's covenant despair; together they cap Book III with darkness, and the question of how God will respond is the question that animates the next two books.

**Jewish trajectory.** The rabbis read Ps 88 with extraordinary care. *Midrash Tehillim* on Ps 88 multiplies the death-imagery rather than softening it; the midrash interprets the threefold "like one set loose…" of v. 5 as three distinct categories of the dead (those killed by sword, those killed by famine, those killed by sickness) and refuses to allegorize the despair away. Rashi reads it as the voice of Israel in exile — the body of Israel praying as a corporate sufferer. Ibn Ezra and Radak both read it more individually but preserve the unresolved character. Modern Jewish thinkers — Jon Levenson on death and resurrection (*Resurrection and the Restoration of Israel*, 2006), Nahum Sarna on the Psalms as prayer-text, Martin Buber on the dialogical "I-Thou" pressed to its breaking point, Abraham Joshua Heschel on the prophetic protest — converge on the recognition that Ps 88 is not a failure of faith but a particularly demanding form of it. Liturgically, Ps 88 is recited in some Jewish traditions on Tisha B'Av and during periods of communal mourning. It is the prayer of the body in the grave, of the people in exile, of the soul without footing.

**Christian trajectory.** Augustine's *Enarrationes in Psalmos* on Ps 88 (Ps 87 in the Vulgate) takes the darkness with full Christological seriousness. He hears in the psalm the voice of Christ in the tomb on Holy Saturday — the descent into Hades, the silence between cross and resurrection. This Holy Saturday reading has shaped the Christian liturgical use of the psalm ever since. Athanasius reads it similarly, as Christ taking the human cry-of-dereliction into himself. Cassiodorus and Aquinas extend the Augustinian reading. Luther preached on Ps 88 with characteristic honesty: "this psalm describes the sufferings of a soul that is in agony before God, and feels nothing but the wrath of God." Calvin (*Commentary on the Psalms*) refuses to read Ps 88 as despair-without-faith — for Calvin, the very fact that the speaker still addresses God ("O LORD, God of my salvation," v. 1) is the marker of faith holding on inside the darkness. Among twentieth-and-twenty-first-century commentators, Gunkel and Mowinckel (form-critical), Westermann (theological), Kraus (historical), Hossfeld and Zenger (canonical), Goldingay (literary-theological), Tate (Word Biblical Commentary), Wenham (canonical and ethical), Brueggemann (the prophetic protest tradition), Patrick Miller (theology of the Psalms), William Brown (poetic and pastoral), Beth Tanner (feminist-theological), and David Mitchell (eschatological) all converge on the recognition that Ps 88's structural refusal of resolution is theologically intentional. It is not a defective lament; it is a faithful witness to the experience of the silence of God.

**The Holy Saturday tradition.** In the Christian liturgical year, Ps 88 is the appointed psalm for Holy Saturday — the day between Good Friday and Easter when Christ is in the tomb. The Catholic Liturgy of the Hours assigns Ps 88 to Friday Compline, the night-prayer of the week. The Anglican Book of Common Prayer assigns it to the evening of the seventeenth day of the month. In all these uses, Ps 88 is the psalm of the in-between — the prayer for the time when Easter has not yet come.

**Bonhoeffer and the modern witness.** Dietrich Bonhoeffer's *Letters and Papers from Prison* (1953) returns repeatedly to the discipline of staying with darkness rather than rushing to resolution: "Christians stand by God in his hour of grieving." Stanley Hauerwas, in his work on suffering and theodicy, treats Ps 88 as the warrant for faith without fix. Kathleen Norris's *Acedia and Me* (2008) reads Ps 88 as a permission-text for those who suffer chronic depression — the canonical assurance that the believer's despair is not exiled from worship. Kay Redfield Jamison's writings on the suicidal mind have been read alongside Ps 88 as theological resources for pastoral ministry to the mentally ill. The pastoral witness is consistent: Ps 88 exists in Scripture so that the sufferer is not alone in praying without resolution.

**NT echoes.** No NT writer cites Ps 88 explicitly. But the pattern of the cry of dereliction — Mark 15:34, "My God, my God, why have you forsaken me?" — shares its theological grammar. Hebrews 5:7 ("In the days of his flesh, Jesus offered up prayers and supplications, with loud cries and tears, to him who was able to save him from death") reads as a NT description of the kind of prayer Ps 88 records. The Christian tradition has heard Jesus's voice in Ps 88 from at least Augustine onward.

## Lens 4: Theological Intent

**Tension 1: The unresolved-darkness structure vs. the Christian pressure-to-resolve.** Much contemporary Christian piety — particularly in the prosperity-gospel and triumphalist evangelical traditions — finds Ps 88 unbearable. The psalm refuses to deliver the move that those traditions require: from suffering to victory, from cross to resurrection, from lament to praise. There has been recurring pressure to read Ps 88 as a setup for Ps 89, or as the voice of a believer who *should have* trusted more, or as Christ's voice on Holy Saturday on the way to Easter morning.

- *The pressure-to-resolve position* (often associated with prosperity-gospel readings, but also with some triumphalist evangelical and charismatic traditions) treats Ps 88's darkness as a problem to be solved. The psalmist *should* turn to praise; the fact that he does not is a defect or a temporary failure of faith.
- *The Holy-Saturday position* (Augustine, much patristic Christian reading, Catholic and Reformed liturgical tradition) reads Ps 88 as a darkness that is real but framed by the canonical resurrection-narrative. The psalm is honest about the darkness but does not have the last word in the canon.
- *The faith-without-fix position* (Brueggemann, Bonhoeffer, Hauerwas, Norris, much pastoral theology) refuses to subordinate Ps 88 to its canonical neighbors. The psalm exists, on its own terms, as a permission-text. Its function is precisely to *not* resolve.

The steelman for the faith-without-fix position is that the structural refusal of Ps 88 is not accidental. The editor who placed Ps 88 in the canon could have edited it. The Hebrew text could have ended with a verse or a half-verse of resolution. It does not. The single word *machshakh* is the deliberate final note. To read past that note in search of resolution is to refuse the witness the text is making. The strongest argument for honoring the structure as it stands is that the entire pastoral tradition of the Christian Church — at its best moments, in Augustine, in Luther, in Bonhoeffer, in Norris — has found in this psalm a resource for those whose suffering will not lift, and the resource depends on the structure being preserved.

**Tension 2: The pastoral application — what does it mean that this psalm exists?** Ps 88 raises an acute pastoral question for the Christian who reads it: how do we hold this text alongside chronic depression, suicidal ideation, terminal illness, persistent grief?

- *The therapeutic-suspicion position* (some forms of contemporary biblical counseling, some neo-puritan readings) treats Ps 88's voice as cautionary — the believer ought to move beyond such darkness through right understanding of the gospel.
- *The companionship position* (Norris, Hauerwas, much modern pastoral theology, the Christian tradition of "spiritual friendship" with the suffering) treats Ps 88 as a companion-text. The presence of this psalm in Scripture authorizes the sufferer to pray without resolution; the psalm sits alongside the sufferer rather than instructing the sufferer to leave the darkness.
- *The diagnostic-and-companion position* (the broader pastoral consensus drawing on Kay Redfield Jamison and others) holds both: the psalm is a companion *and* a diagnostic — it sits with the sufferer *and* it indicates that this kind of suffering is real enough to warrant medical and spiritual attention.

The steelman for the companionship position is that Ps 88 in Scripture is not framed as a cautionary tale. There is no editorial commentary urging the speaker to move on. The next psalm, Ps 89, is *also* unresolved. The pastoral implication — that some suffering does not lift in this life, and that faithful prayer in such suffering can simply be the prayer of darkness pressed against the silence of God — is built into the canon's structure.

**Where this leaves us.** Psalm 88 is a permission. It does not teach that despair is the truth; it teaches that despair, when it comes, is not exile from God. The believer who can pray nothing else can pray this psalm. The fact that the canon contains it means that the canon contains us — that no register of suffering is outside the people of God's prayer-life. *Machshakh* is the last word. There is something ferocious in the canonical decision to let it be the last word.

**Closing question.** If the canon's response to Ps 88 is not to overwrite the darkness but to place Ps 89 next to it — another unresolved cry — what does it mean that the people of God have, embedded in their primary prayer-book, two endings in a row that refuse the move toward resolution?
