# Psalm 110 — Go Deeper

## Lens 1: The Historical World

Psalm 110 is, by any reasonable count, **the most-cited Old Testament text in the New Testament**. It is quoted or alluded to over twenty times across the gospels, Acts, the Pauline letters, Hebrews, and Revelation. It is the single most important OT psalm for early Christology, providing both the seated-at-God's-right-hand framework and the Melchizedekian-priest framework that Hebrews develops at length. To read Ps 110 is to read the verse that may, more than any other, made the high Christology of the early church textually possible.

The superscription is *le-David mizmor* (לְדָוִד מִזְמוֹר), "A Psalm of David" — though here the standard order of words is reversed (most Davidic superscriptions read *mizmor le-David*). The minor variation may signal an older or distinct tradition.

In **Gunkel**'s form-critical taxonomy, Ps 110 is a **Königspsalm** — a royal psalm. It belongs to the same family as Pss 2, 18, 20, 21, 45, 72, 89, 101, 132, 144 — psalms that originated in the Israelite monarchy as coronation, wedding, victory, or thanksgiving liturgies for the Davidic king. Gunkel and Mowinckel located the royal psalms in the cult: Ps 110 likely served as a coronation oracle, declaring the new king's enthronement at YHWH's right hand and his combined royal-priestly office. Some scholars (Eaton, Day) have specified the Sitz as the *autumnal enthronement festival*, where the Davidic king annually re-affirmed his rule under YHWH's kingship.

Dating is contested. Three positions: (1) *early monarchy* (Eaton, Day, Mitchell) — the psalm preserves pre-exilic coronation language; (2) *late monarchy* (Goldingay, partial) — the psalm is composed for one of the late kings, possibly Josiah; (3) *Hasmonean* (a minority position from earlier critical scholarship, e.g., Duhm) — the psalm celebrates a Hasmonean priest-king. The Hasmonean dating has been largely abandoned because the Qumran 11Q5 already treats Ps 110 as scriptural in the second century BCE. Most modern scholars favor a pre-exilic origin with later canonical placement in Book V.

The opening verse is the explosive one: *ne'um Yahweh la-adoni shev li-ymini ad-ashit oyveikha hadom le-raglekha* (נְאֻם יְהוָה לַאדֹנִי שֵׁב לִימִינִי עַד־אָשִׁית אֹיְבֶיךָ הֲדֹם לְרַגְלֶיךָ), "The LORD says to my Lord: 'Sit at my right hand, until I make your enemies your footstool'" (110:1). The Hebrew is precise. *Ne'um Yahweh* (נְאֻם יְהוָה) is an oracle-formula — "the divine utterance of YHWH" — typically used by prophets to introduce God's direct speech. *La-adoni* (לַאדֹנִי) is "to my lord" — the speaker (David, in canonical attribution) addresses someone else as *adoni* (my lord), and this *adoni* is the figure being seated at YHWH's right hand. This is the verse Jesus will use in the Synoptic challenge (Mark 12:35-37 and parallels) to push his interlocutors past the simple "messiah is David's son" formulation toward the question of how David can call his own son *lord*.

The seated-at-the-right-hand image had ANE royal precedent. Egyptian pharaonic iconography depicts the king seated alongside the deity; Mesopotamian royal hymns describe kings as enthroned with the gods. But the specifically Israelite combination — YHWH speaking the oracle, the Davidic king enthroned at YHWH's right, the footstool image (cf. Ps 99:5, 132:7) — is the cultic and theological matrix.

The *Melchizedekian* oracle is the second pivot: *atta khohen le-olam al-divrati Malki-Tzedek* (אַתָּה־כֹהֵן לְעוֹלָם עַל־דִּבְרָתִי מַלְכִּי־צֶדֶק), "You are a priest forever after the order of Melchizedek" (110:4). The Hebrew *al-divrati* is unusual — meaning "according to the manner of," "in the line of," or "by reason of." Melchizedek (מַלְכִּי־צֶדֶק, "my king is righteous" or "king of righteousness") appears only once in the OT outside this psalm: Gen 14:18-20, where he is "priest of God Most High" and "king of Salem" who blesses Abraham after Abraham's victory over the eastern kings. He has no genealogy, no death recorded, no priestly succession — and these silences will become decisive in Hebrews 7's Christological argument.

The Davidic king is here declared *priest* — combining royal and priestly office. This is theologically remarkable: under Mosaic torah, kingship and priesthood were rigorously separated (the king was Davidic-Judahite; the priest was Levitical-Aaronid). To claim both for the king required appeal to a *pre-Mosaic* priestly figure — and Melchizedek was the only available candidate. Some scholars (Levenson, Hossfeld-Zenger) read this as evidence of an old Jebusite-Salem priestly tradition that David inherited when he captured Jerusalem and made it his capital. The pre-Israelite priest-king of Salem becomes the Davidic king's prototype.

The closing verses (110:5-7) describe the king's military victories — shattering kings, executing judgment among the nations, drinking from the brook. The drinking-from-the-brook image (110:7) is enigmatic; some read it as the king's refreshment in pursuit of fleeing enemies (Allen), others as a ritual element in the coronation (Mitchell connects it to Gihon, where Solomon was anointed in 1 Kgs 1:38).

The DSS preservation in 11Q5 (11QPs-a) includes Ps 110 in standard form, attesting the text in pre-Christian transmission. The LXX (Psalm 109) translates *ne'um Yahweh la-adoni* as *eipen ho kyrios tō kyriō mou* — "the Lord said to my Lord" — which becomes the form Jesus and the NT writers cite.

## Lens 2: The Literature

Ps 110 is short — only seven verses — but every verse is dense. The structure is governed by **two divine oracles**:

- **Oracle I (v. 1):** *ne'um Yahweh* — "Sit at my right hand, until I make your enemies your footstool."
- **Oracle II (v. 4):** *nishba Yahweh ve-lo yinnachem* — "The LORD has sworn and will not change his mind: 'You are a priest forever after the order of Melchizedek.'"

These two oracles structure the psalm into two halves, each with its own divine speech and its own consequence. **Strophe I (vv. 1-3)** delivers the kingship oracle and develops it: YHWH sends forth the king's scepter from Zion; the king's people offer themselves on the day of his power; the king's youth is consecrated. **Strophe II (vv. 4-7)** delivers the priesthood oracle and develops it into the king's victorious campaigns. The two oracles together establish the king as priest-king, enthroned and victorious.

**Lowth's** parallelism is mostly synthetic — each second colon advances the first rather than restating it. "The LORD says to my Lord: / 'Sit at my right hand, / until I make your enemies your footstool'" (110:1) — three cola in escalating specificity. The oracle of v. 4 is the same: *nishba Yahweh* / *ve-lo yinnachem* / *attah khohen le-olam* — the swearing, the not-repenting, the eternal priesthood.

There is a powerful **structural inclusio** built around the right-hand image. V. 1: the king sits at YHWH's right hand. V. 5: "the Lord is at your right hand" — now YHWH is at the king's right hand. The two right-hand images frame the psalm and reverse polarity: the king first sits at YHWH's right (receiving honor and protection), then YHWH stands at the king's right (executing the king's victorious campaigns). This figural reversal is central to the psalm's theological achievement: the king who is honored by sitting beside YHWH is then accompanied in battle by YHWH himself. The two right-hand images insist that royal and divine power are inseparable in this figure.

The **footstool image** (110:1) draws on ANE royal iconography of subjugated enemies depicted as footstools beneath the king's feet. The image will be picked up in Pss 8:6 and 99:5, then explosively in 1 Cor 15:25-27 and Heb 1:13, 10:13, where the *footstool* eschatology becomes structural to Christian eschatology.

There is no *selah* in Ps 110 — Book V's restraint with the marker. Prosody is mostly 3+3 stress, with the divine oracles carrying slightly heavier rhythmic weight.

The textual difficulty in v. 3 is considerable. The MT reads *be-hadrei-qodesh me-rechem mishchar lekha tal yaldutekha* (בְּהַדְרֵי־קֹדֶשׁ מֵרֶחֶם מִשְׁחָר לְךָ טַל יַלְדֻתֶיךָ) — "in holy garments, from the womb of the morning, the dew of your youth will be yours." The image is striking but obscure: holy garments, womb of the morning, dew of youth. Some manuscripts and the LXX read *be-hadrat qodesh* (in the splendor of holiness); some emend *mishchar* (morning) to *meshachti-kha* (I have begotten you), giving "from the womb, before the morning star, I have begotten you" — which would make v. 3 a begetting-formula parallel to Ps 2:7. The textual question is unresolved; the LXX rendering (*ek gastros pro heōsphorou egennēsa se*) influenced patristic Christological reading.

## Lens 3: The Larger Story

**Jewish Reception.** *Midrash Tehillim* on Ps 110 attributes the psalm to David and reads it messianically — the figure addressed as *adoni* is the messiah, who is to come. The midrash already identifies the priest-king of v. 4 with the messianic figure, drawing on Zechariah's "branch" who will be both priest and king (Zech 6:13). Rabbinic tradition is divided about whether the addressee is Abraham (so some early midrashim, treating *la-adoni* as David's address to his ancestor), David himself in some retrospective Davidic voice, or the messiah. The latter position becomes dominant in later Jewish tradition, though the Christian appropriation made many medieval Jewish commentators cautious about messianic readings.

**Rashi** (eleventh century) reads the *adoni* as Abraham — the speaker is Eliezer, Abraham's servant, addressing his master. This reading is partly defensive (it deflects the Christological appropriation) and partly traditional (some early midrash supports it). **Ibn Ezra** wavers between the Abrahamic and the messianic readings. **Radak** reads the psalm Davidically — David addresses David, in a kind of self-reflective royal psalm; the *adoni* is David himself in a literary voicing. **Nachmanides** treats the priesthood of v. 4 carefully, arguing that the Davidic king's priesthood is a metaphor of devotion rather than a literal cultic function.

In modern Jewish scholarship, **Levenson** has reopened the messianic reading on its own terms: Ps 110 is a messianic-royal psalm of the late monarchy, and it is *not* originally Christological; it is a Davidic-messianic vision that Christian tradition has appropriated. Levenson's work makes possible a reading in which Jewish and Christian traditions can both claim Ps 110 as messianic without one collapsing into the other. **Daniel Boyarin** in *The Jewish Gospels* has argued that the Son of Man / Messiah Christology of the gospels, including the use of Ps 110, is itself Jewish — second-temple Judaism had a "two powers in heaven" tradition (the elder and the younger divine figure) that made Ps 110's *Yahweh*-and-*adoni* construction theologically intelligible in Jewish terms before Christianity. The Christology of Mark 12 and Hebrews is Jewish exegesis of Ps 110 in a Jewish second-temple matrix.

**Christian Reception.** **Augustine** (*Enarrationes* on Ps 109 LXX) treats Ps 110 as the Psalter's most explicit Christological text. The *adoni* is Christ; the seated-at-the-right-hand is the ascension; the footstool eschatology is Christ's final victory; the Melchizedekian priesthood is Christ's eternal high priesthood (which Augustine reads through Hebrews). Augustine's reading establishes the pattern that Western Christian tradition will follow.

**Cassiodorus** develops the four-stage Christological reading: incarnation (v. 3, the "begetting"), enthronement (v. 1), eternal priesthood (v. 4), eschatological victory (vv. 5-7). **Aquinas** in his *Postilla super Psalmos* and in *Summa Theologiae* III.22 treats Ps 110:4 as the foundational text for Christ's eternal priesthood — drawing the *triplex munus* (prophet, priest, king) primarily from this verse. Aquinas's reading is then codified in Catholic theology of Christ's priesthood (CCC §1544-1545: "Christ, high priest and unique mediator," cites Ps 110 as the text Hebrews develops).

**Luther** (*Operationes*) reads Ps 110 with passionate Christological intensity — the priest-king who is enthroned, who is begotten of God, who reigns eternally. **Calvin** (1557 commentary) treats Ps 110 as the great Christological psalm and defends the messianic-Christological reading at length against contemporary Jewish counter-readings (Calvin engages Rashi's Abrahamic reading directly and rejects it). Calvin develops the *triplex munus* doctrine on the basis of Ps 110: Christ is prophet (revealing God), priest (interceding for sinners — Ps 110:4), king (reigning at God's right hand — Ps 110:1).

In modern critical scholarship, **Gunkel** classed Ps 110 as a royal coronation psalm and was relatively cautious about Christological appropriation. **Mowinckel** placed it in the autumn enthronement festival. **Westermann** read it as a programmatic royal psalm. **Kraus** developed the early-monarchy dating. **Hossfeld-Zenger** treat Ps 110 as the climax of the Davidic cluster (Pss 108-110) within Book V and read it as the canonical-eschatological turn: the post-exilic editor placed Ps 110 here as a messianic-eschatological hope after the Davidic monarchy's actual collapse. **Goldingay** is sympathetic to the messianic-eschatological reading. **Mitchell** in *The Message of the Psalter* makes Ps 110 the structural center of Book V's eschatological program. **Brevard Childs** read Ps 110 as both an originally-royal psalm and a canonically-messianic psalm — both readings are correct; the text bears both, and the canonical placement in Book V (after the Davidic collapse) gives the messianic-eschatological reading its weight.

**Brueggemann** has insisted that Ps 110's military violence (vv. 5-7) — shattering kings, filling nations with corpses — must not be domesticated. The royal-psalm tradition includes a militant-imperial dimension that Christian Christology has often spiritualized but cannot eliminate from the text. **Tanner** (in the NICOT volume) develops the canonical placement carefully.

**NT Use — the Most-Cited OT Text.** A partial inventory:

- **Mark 12:35-37 / Matt 22:41-46 / Luke 20:41-44** — Jesus's own use. In the Synoptic challenge, Jesus poses Ps 110:1 to his interlocutors: *if David calls the messiah "lord," how is the messiah David's son?* Jesus uses the verse to push the messianic identity beyond mere Davidic descent. This is one of the few places in the gospels where Jesus engages in scriptural exegesis on his own terms, and the psalm he chooses is Ps 110.

- **Mark 14:62 / Matt 26:64 / Luke 22:69** — At his trial, Jesus combines Ps 110:1 with Dan 7:13: *you will see the Son of Man seated at the right hand of Power.* The combination is the climactic Christological self-disclosure of the trial scene.

- **Acts 2:34-35** — Peter's Pentecost sermon: David did not ascend to the heavens, but he says, "The Lord said to my Lord, sit at my right hand." Peter uses Ps 110:1 as the textual proof that the *exalted* Christ — not David — is the addressee. The ascension is interpreted Ps-110:1-wise.

- **Acts 5:31, 7:55-56** — Stephen's vision and the apostolic preaching continue the right-hand framework.

- **Rom 8:34** — Christ is "at the right hand of God, who indeed is interceding for us" — fusing Ps 110:1 (right hand) with Ps 110:4 (priesthood).

- **1 Cor 15:25-27** — "He must reign until he has put all his enemies under his feet" — direct citation of Ps 110:1's footstool image as the structure of eschatology.

- **Eph 1:20-22** — God seated Christ "at his right hand in the heavenly places" with "all things under his feet" — combination of Ps 110:1 and Ps 8:6.

- **Col 3:1; 1 Pet 3:22** — right-hand language integrated into baptismal-ethical exhortation.

- **Heb 1:13** — Ps 110:1 cited at the climax of the angels-vs-Son catena.

- **Heb 5:6, 5:10, 6:20, 7:11, 7:15, 7:17, 7:21, 7:24, 7:28** — Ps 110:4 is the *foundation text* for the Melchizedekian Christology of Hebrews 5-7. Hebrews 7 develops the typology at length: Melchizedek's lack of genealogy and death make him a *type* of the eternal Christ; Christ's priesthood is *not* Levitical (which would be problematic, since Christ was of Judah) but Melchizedekian — a priesthood older than Aaron, sworn by oath, eternal, mediated through one who entered behind the veil.

- **Heb 10:12-13** — Christ "sat down at the right hand of God, waiting from that time until his enemies should be made a footstool for his feet." The whole Christology of Hebrews is built on Ps 110:1 + 110:4.

- **Rev 3:21** — Christ, having conquered, sits with the Father on his throne. The right-hand framework expands to include the saints' co-enthronement.

This concentration is unparalleled. No other OT text functions for the NT writers as Ps 110 does.

## Lens 4: The Theological Intent

**Tension 1: Original royal psalm vs. canonical-messianic reading.**

*Position A: Original-royal reading (Gunkel, Mowinckel, Kraus).* Ps 110 was composed for the Davidic monarchy as a coronation psalm. The *adoni* was the Davidic king; the right-hand seating was royal honor; the priesthood was the Davidic king's combined royal-priestly office (Salem tradition); the military victories were the king's expected campaigns. On this reading, Ps 110 was originally about the Davidic king and was only later read messianically when the monarchy collapsed.

*Position B: Canonical-messianic reading (Mitchell, Hossfeld-Zenger).* The Psalter's editors placed Ps 110 in Book V *after* the Davidic monarchy's collapse and the Mosaic enthronement of YHWH (Book IV). The canonical placement reads the psalm eschatologically: the Davidic king of the original cult has become the messianic figure of the post-exilic hope. The original-royal meaning is preserved, but the canonical meaning is messianic.

*Position C: Both — Childs's canonical-critical synthesis.* The text is both. It was originally royal; it is canonically messianic. The editors did not erase the original meaning, and they did not impose a wholly new meaning. They placed the royal psalm in a canonical context that gave the original royal language its messianic resonance.

**Steelman:** Childs's synthesis is broadly correct and is now the consensus among most major commentators. The original cultic Sitz was royal coronation; the canonical placement makes the psalm messianic-eschatological. Both readings are textually responsible. The Christian appropriation is then a further step of canonical development that takes the messianic reading and identifies the messiah as Christ.

**Tension 2: The Melchizedekian priesthood — what does it mean?**

The Christological development in Hebrews 5-7 raises a question: what is the theological force of Christ being a priest *not* in the Levitical line but in the Melchizedekian line?

*Position A: Catholic theology of Christ's eternal priesthood.* Melchizedek's priesthood is *forever*; Christ's priesthood is *forever*. Christ's priesthood is therefore not subject to succession, not bound to the Mosaic temple, not limited by death. The Catholic understanding of Christ's eternal priesthood, mediated through the eucharist (CCC §1544-1545; Vatican II *Lumen Gentium* §10), grounds the Catholic theology of priestly ministry as participating in Christ's one priesthood. The *priest forever* of Ps 110:4 grounds the church's eucharistic life.

*Position B: Reformed *triplex munus* and once-for-all priesthood.* Calvin and the Reformed tradition take Ps 110:4 as the foundation for Christ's *unique* and *unrepeatable* priesthood. Christ is priest forever in the sense that his single self-offering on the cross is eternally efficacious; there is no continuing priesthood that needs to be re-presented. The *triplex munus* (prophet, priest, king) builds Christology on Ps 110: Christ is *king* (v. 1, right hand), *priest* (v. 4, Melchizedekian), and *prophet* (the broader Psalter context). Reformed theology then resists any view of priestly ministry that would supplement or repeat Christ's once-for-all priestly act.

*Position C: Eastern Orthodox high-priestly Christology (Maximus the Confessor).* Maximus and the Greek tradition develop Christ's eternal high priesthood as ongoing intercession in the heavenly sanctuary. Christ's priesthood is not finished at the cross but continues in the eternal liturgy of heaven, in which the church's earthly liturgy participates. Ps 110:4's *forever* is not just temporal duration but eternal-liturgical reality. The Orthodox Divine Liturgy is the earthly participation in this heavenly priestly action.

**Steelman:** All three traditions read Hebrews carefully and arrive at different but defensible positions. The Catholic tradition emphasizes the continuing priestly mediation, the Reformed tradition emphasizes the once-for-all efficacy, the Orthodox tradition emphasizes the eternal-liturgical present tense. Ecumenical convergence has been possible (the Lima document, the Catholic-Reformed dialogues) by recognizing that all three are reading Hebrews and Ps 110:4 as central, and that the differences are about *how* Christ's eternal priesthood is mediated to the church, not whether it is.

**Tension 3: The Markan-Synoptic Jesus-tradition and the Pharisee-Sadducee debate.**

When Jesus poses Ps 110:1 to his interlocutors in Mark 12:35-37, what is the rhetorical context? Jesus is in the temple, exchanging hostile questions with various Jerusalem authorities. He has just refuted the Sadducees on resurrection (Mark 12:18-27) by appealing to Exod 3 as proof that the patriarchs are alive — a reading the Pharisees would have endorsed. He then turns to his own challenge: how can the messiah be David's son if David calls him *lord* (Ps 110:1)? Jesus is exploiting an exegetical lever the Pharisees would have recognized — the question of how to handle the *adoni* address — and using it to advance a higher Christology than the merely Davidic-political one. The Pharisees in Mark are silenced (Mark 12:34); the Sadducees were already routed. Jesus uses Ps 110 to take exegetical territory beyond what either party held.

**Where this leaves us.** Ps 110 is the most concentrated Christological text in the OT — but only when read canonically. It was originally a royal coronation psalm with combined royal-priestly office. It was canonically placed in Book V as messianic-eschatological hope after the Davidic collapse. It was used by Jesus himself in the Synoptic challenge to push messianic identity past the merely-Davidic. It was developed by Hebrews into the foundation of Christ's eternal Melchizedekian priesthood. It was developed by Catholic, Reformed, and Orthodox traditions into different but compatible Christologies of Christ's eternal priestly mediation. It teaches the church that the figure at YHWH's right hand is both honored and accompanied — sat beside, then walked with into battle. It teaches that the priest-king category, recovered through Melchizedek, makes possible a Christology the merely-Davidic frame could not have supported. And it teaches that the verse the church has cited more than any other in the OT was the verse Jesus himself reached for when he wanted to ask the question that mattered most: how is the messiah the lord David called *adoni*?

**Closing question.** If Ps 110:1 is the verse Jesus chose for his most pointed exegetical challenge, and if it has remained the verse the NT writers reach for more than any other when they say what they think Christ is, what does it mean that the same psalm holds together a coronation oracle, a priestly oracle, and a battle scene with corpses — and what would it cost the church to learn to hold all three together rather than choosing only the throne-room scenes that comfort us?
