# Isaiah 61 — Go Deeper

## The Thread

Isaiah 61 is the chapter Jesus chose. When he stood up in the Nazareth synagogue at the start of his public ministry, this was the scroll handed to him, and these were the words he read aloud — *the Spirit of the Lord is upon me, because he has anointed me to bring good news to the poor* — before sitting down and saying, *today this Scripture has been fulfilled in your hearing* (Luke 4:21). Of all the texts in the Hebrew Bible Jesus could have made his manifesto, he chose this one. And he stopped reading mid-sentence. The line he did not read — *the day of vengeance of our God* — has shaped Christian eschatology for two thousand years by its conspicuous absence.

The chapter sits near the climax of Third Isaiah (chs. 56-66), the post-exilic collection traditionally distinguished from First Isaiah (chs. 1-39, eighth-century Jerusalem) and Second Isaiah (chs. 40-55, exilic Babylon). It is a Spirit-anointed prophet's self-introduction, a Jubilee-proclamation, a vision of comprehensive restoration. It became, in Jesus's hands, the charter of a new ministry — and the unread half of verse 2 became a question Christians and Jews have answered very differently.

## Lens 1 — Historical World

The dominant critical position since Bernhard Duhm's 1892 *Das Buch Jesaia* divides Isaiah into three layers: First Isaiah from the eighth century BC (Assyrian crisis); Second Isaiah from the exile (mid-sixth century, Babylon); Third Isaiah from the early restoration period (late sixth to fifth century BC, Persian-period Yehud). Chapters 56-66 — the *Trito-Isaiah* unit — are widely treated as anonymous post-exilic prophecy from a community shaped by the legacy of the Babylonian-exile prophet but now wrestling with the disappointments of the return.

Mainstream-critical scholarship (Claus Westermann's *Isaiah 40-66*, Joseph Blenkinsopp's Anchor Yale *Isaiah 56-66*, Paul Hanson's *The Dawn of Apocalyptic*, John Goldingay's commentaries, Jacob Stromberg's *Isaiah After Exile*, Patricia Tull) reads Third Isaiah as composite — a small prophetic circle, perhaps disciples of the Second-Isaiah voice, addressing the social and religious tensions of the rebuilt-Jerusalem community. Hanson famously argued the section reflects an inner-Jewish conflict between visionary-prophetic and hierocratic-priestly factions; Stromberg and Childs emphasize editorial-redactional shaping that ties the whole book together as a literary unity even if the underlying compositions are layered.

The unified-authorship defense (J. Alec Motyer's *Prophecy of Isaiah*, John Oswalt's NICOT volumes) argues that the eighth-century Isaiah ben Amoz could prophesy about events centuries away (Cyrus by name in 44:28, the post-exilic restoration), that the New Testament treats the book as a single voice (John 12:38-41 cites both First and Second Isaiah and attributes both to *the prophet Isaiah*), and that thematic unity (the Holy One of Israel, Zion's destiny, servant motifs) outweighs stylistic variation. Brevard Childs's canonical approach takes a middle path: the historical layering is real, but the canonical-final-form is the theologically authoritative voice and should be read as such.

The Great Isaiah Scroll (1QIsa-a) from Qumran Cave 1 — copied around 125 BC — preserves all 66 chapters as a single scroll with no manuscript division between sections. This means that by the second century BC the book circulated as a unity, even if its compositional history was layered. The manuscript evidence cannot decide the authorship debate, but it does establish that the *received* book of Isaiah is the book Jesus would have known.

Isaiah 61 itself opens with a Spirit-anointed prophetic figure who speaks in the first person. Hebrew *ruach Adonai Yahweh alai ya'an mashach Yahweh oti* (רוּחַ אֲדֹנָי יְהוִה עָלָי יַעַן מָשַׁח יְהוָה אֹתִי) — *the Spirit of the Lord God is upon me, because the Lord has anointed me*. The verb *mashach* (מָשַׁח) is the root of *mashiach* (מָשִׁיחַ) — *messiah*, the *anointed one*. Critical scholarship debates whether the anointed speaker here is the Servant of Second Isaiah (chs. 42, 49, 50, 52-53) reappearing in Third Isaiah, a separate prophetic figure, or a corporate-Israel voice. Westermann reads it as the Trito-Isaiah prophet self-identifying; Blenkinsopp leans toward an individual prophetic figure who has absorbed Servant motifs; Hanson reads visionary-prophetic group identity.

The mission described is unmistakably Jubilee. *To proclaim liberty to the captives* — Hebrew *liqro li-shvuyim deror* (לִקְרֹא לִשְׁבוּיִם דְּרוֹר) — uses *deror* (דְּרוֹר), the same word in Leviticus 25:10 for the Jubilee release: *you shall proclaim liberty throughout the land*. *The year of the Lord's favor* (*shenat ratzon la-Yahweh*) is the Jubilee year — every fiftieth year, debts cancelled, slaves freed, ancestral land restored. The prophet announces a Jubilee not for one year but as the inaugural act of comprehensive restoration: ruined cities rebuilt, mourners comforted, ashes exchanged for beauty. Verses 4-7 then promise that strangers will tend Israel's flocks while Israel functions as priests of the nations — a reversal of exile humiliation, a double-portion vindication.

The chapter ends in marriage and garden imagery (61:10-11): the speaker, clothed in salvation as a bridegroom and a bride, anticipates Yahweh causing righteousness to *sprout up* (*tatzmiach*, תַּצְמִיחַ) before all nations — language that will return in Isaiah 62 with the Hephzibah-Beulah names.

## Lens 2 — Literature

The chapter is prophetic-poetry of the restoration-oracle type, with three movements:

**First-person commission (61:1-3).** The speaker introduces himself by Spirit-anointing and lists his sevenfold mission — good news, healing, liberty, jubilee-proclamation, comfort, beauty-for-ashes, oaks-of-righteousness. The literary form echoes the prophetic call narratives (Isa 6, Jer 1, Ezek 1-3) but compressed into a self-presentation rather than a vision-report. The structure is *X instead of Y* parallelism: headdress instead of ashes, oil of gladness instead of mourning, garment of praise instead of faint spirit.

**Restoration-oracle (61:4-9).** A shift to the second-person plural address. *They* (the mourners-now-comforted) will rebuild ancient ruins. Strangers will labor for Israel; Israel will be priests of the nations and eat their wealth. The double-portion (*mishneh*, מִשְׁנֶה) is the inheritance-language of the firstborn (Deut 21:17) — Israel as Yahweh's firstborn (Exod 4:22) restored to first-place status.

**Bridal-vesting psalm (61:10-11).** First-person again, but now lyric thanksgiving. The garments of salvation (*bigdei yesha*, בִּגְדֵי יֶשַׁע), the robe of righteousness (*me'il tzedakah*, מְעִיל צְדָקָה), the bridegroom-and-bride simile, and the closing garden-image of righteousness *sprouting up* before the nations.

The literary architecture moves from *anointing* (vocation given) to *restoration* (vocation enacted) to *adornment* (vocation celebrated). The chapter is structurally beautiful — and the Spirit-anointed mission of v. 1 finds its consummation in the bridegroom-bride image of v. 10, marrying prophetic-vocation to covenantal-marriage.

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

**Within the prophetic canon.** Isaiah 61 stands at the center of Third Isaiah's vision of Yahweh's comprehensive restoration. It draws on Servant-Songs (esp. 42:1-7 — *I have put my Spirit upon him*; *to bring out the prisoners from the dungeon*), on Jubilee-Levitical theology (Lev 25), and on the Exodus-language of liberty. It will be re-keyed in Isaiah 62 (the watchman, the marriage-names) and reach its eschatological climax in 65-66 (new heavens, new earth).

**Jewish trajectory.** The medieval Jewish commentators — Rashi, Ibn Ezra, Radak — read the speaker variously as the prophet himself (Rashi: Isaiah speaking of his own mission), as the Messiah (Ibn Ezra leaves the door open), or as corporate Israel (Radak emphasizes the people). The Targum Jonathan paraphrase of 61:1 reads, *the prophet said: the spirit of prophecy from before the Lord God is upon me*. In rabbinic literature the *year of favor* and *day of vengeance* are sometimes paired with the *two days of the Messiah* — the comprehensive restoration includes both grace to Israel and judgment on Israel's oppressors. The Jewish tradition has generally read 61:2 in its entirety: the day of vengeance is part of the redemption, not an optional addendum. The *Yom Naqam* (day of vengeance) is the day Yahweh's enemies meet justice and Israel's wrongs are righted.

**Christian trajectory.** From the second century onward, Christians read 61:1-2 as a direct prediction of Jesus, made unmistakable by Jesus's own application of the text to himself in Luke 4. Justin Martyr cites it; Origen and the Cappadocians use it as a Spirit-Christology proof; medieval Christian liturgy embeds 61:10 in Marian devotion (the *garments of salvation* applied to Mary as Mother of God in some traditions). The Reformation read 61:1-3 as the gospel-mission of Christ — Calvin, the Geneva Bible, and the Westminster divines all treated Jesus's Nazareth-sermon application as decisive.

**New Testament use — the central event.** Luke 4:16-21 is the foundational text. Jesus enters the Nazareth synagogue on the Sabbath, *as was his custom*, and is given the scroll of Isaiah. He unrolls it, finds the place, and reads. Luke quotes the LXX of Isaiah 61:1-2a with one significant addition (*to set at liberty those who are oppressed* from Isa 58:6) — and a more significant *omission*: Jesus stops at *to proclaim the year of the Lord's favor*. He does not read *and the day of vengeance of our God*. He rolls up the scroll, hands it back, sits down, and says: *today this Scripture has been fulfilled in your hearing*.

Mainstream NT scholarship — N.T. Wright (*Jesus and the Victory of God*; *Luke for Everyone*), Joel B. Green (*The Gospel of Luke*, NICNT), Joseph Fitzmyer (Anchor Yale *Luke*) — treats this as Jesus's deliberate manifesto. The omission is theologically loaded. Wright argues that Jesus is announcing the in-breaking of the year of favor (the inaugurated kingdom, Jubilee enacted) while *deferring* the day of vengeance — not denying it but holding it back to the eschaton. Green emphasizes the Lukan theme of reversal: good news to the poor, freedom for prisoners, sight for the blind. Fitzmyer reads the omission as Lukan-Christological: Jesus's first-coming mission is grace; the day of vengeance is not abolished but held until the parousia.

This is the architecture of Christian eschatology: first-coming as the year of favor, second-coming as the day of vengeance. The two halves of Isaiah 61:2 mapped onto the two advents of Christ. The famous comma in some Christian devotional readings between *favor* and *vengeance* is, in this view, the entire span of the church age.

Luke develops the theme: the Nazareth-sermon ends in attempted lynching (Luke 4:28-30) — when Jesus universalizes God's favor (the widow of Zarephath, Naaman the Syrian), the synagogue tries to throw him off a cliff. The Spirit-anointed Jubilee for the *poor* turns out to include Gentiles. Acts 10:38 echoes the same Christology — *God anointed Jesus of Nazareth with the Holy Spirit and with power*. Acts 4:27 calls Jesus *your holy servant whom you anointed*. The early-church preaching of Jesus is structured by Isaiah 61.

The chapter also feeds Pauline gospel-theology. Paul's *garments of salvation* and *robe of righteousness* (61:10) are everywhere in his clothing-imagery — *put on Christ* (Gal 3:27), *put on the new self* (Eph 4:24), *the breastplate of righteousness* (Eph 6:14). The bridegroom-bride image (61:10) anchors Christ-and-church ecclesiology (Eph 5:22-33; Rev 19:7; 21:2).

## Lens 4 — Theological Intent

**Tension 1: Why did Jesus stop reading?** The deliberate omission of *the day of vengeance of our God* is one of the most consequential interpretive moves in Christian history. The mainstream Christian reading — Wright, Green, Fitzmyer, but also Catholic (CCC §714 cites Isa 61:1-2 as fulfilled in Christ) and Reformed (Geneva Bible, Calvin) — treats the omission as Christological-eschatological: Jesus's first-coming inaugurates the year of favor; the day of vengeance is deferred to the second-coming. The two halves of v. 2 are not separated in Isaiah, but they *are* separated in salvation history.

The Jewish reading runs differently. The day of vengeance is not a problem to be solved but a constitutive part of redemption. Israel's vindication requires the judgment of Israel's oppressors — Babylon, Edom, the powers that crushed Jerusalem. To proclaim Jubilee without judgment-of-oppressors is to leave the systemic injustice in place. The Jewish tradition does not read *vengeance* as personal vindictiveness but as covenantal-justice: Yahweh setting right what oppressors broke.

Steelman the Jewish reading: a gospel that announces grace without justice is a half-gospel. If the Babylonian empire that destroyed the temple, deported the population, and broke the covenant-people goes unjudged, then the *liberty to the captives* is incomplete. The Jewish prophetic tradition refuses to bifurcate grace and judgment; they are two faces of the same redemption. The Christian-bifurcation (first-coming grace, second-coming judgment) is itself a *theological* move that Isaiah's own text does not make.

Steelman the Christian reading: Jesus's omission was not arbitrary. It announced a *new phase* of salvation history — the long age of patient grace, in which the gospel is preached to all nations and the day of vengeance is held back precisely so that more may repent (cf. 2 Pet 3:9). The day of vengeance is not denied; it is *coming*, and its delay is itself mercy. Wright argues that Jesus reframed *vengeance* itself — what looked like vengeance against Rome was instead absorbed into the cross, where Jesus took the day of vengeance into himself.

**Tension 2: Liberation theology.** *The Spirit of the Lord is upon me to bring good news to the poor* — in twentieth-century Latin American liberation theology (Gustavo Gutiérrez's *A Theology of Liberation*, Leonardo Boff, Jon Sobrino), Isaiah 61 became a foundational text for the *preferential option for the poor*. The Jubilee-economic content of the prophecy — debt cancellation, slave-release, land-return — was read as direct critique of structures that crush the poor.

Mainstream Catholic teaching (CCC §2448; Pope John Paul II's *Sollicitudo Rei Socialis*; Pope Francis's *Evangelii Gaudium*) has affirmed the preferential-option-for-the-poor as authentic Catholic social teaching, while distancing itself from Marxist-revolutionary readings. The 1984 CDF *Instruction on Certain Aspects of the Theology of Liberation* (Cardinal Ratzinger) cautioned against reducing salvation to political liberation while affirming the gospel-imperative to attend to the poor. The two-stage Catholic posture: yes to liberation as a dimension of the gospel; no to liberation as the *whole* gospel.

Pentecostal and charismatic readings emphasize *the Spirit of the Lord is upon me* as a present-tense promise — Spirit-anointing for ministry. From early Pentecostalism (Azusa Street, 1906) to global Pentecostalism today, Isaiah 61:1-3 functions as the charter of Spirit-empowered preaching, healing, and deliverance. The criticism: at its worst, charismatic appropriation can collapse the *anointing* into an individual-experience and lose the structural-Jubilee content the text demands.

**Tension 3: Who is the speaker?** Critical scholarship (Westermann, Blenkinsopp) reads the speaker as the Trito-Isaiah prophet — a Spirit-anointed restoration-prophet. Christian tradition reads the speaker as the Messiah, on the warrant of Jesus's own application. These two readings are not strictly incompatible: Christian theology can affirm a *typological* prophetic-figure in the historical text who finds his fullest realization in Christ. The question is how the text reads if Jesus had not picked it up. Critical scholarship's answer: a beautiful Spirit-anointed Jubilee-prophet announcing restoration to post-exilic Yehud. Christian-typological answer: that prophet's voice was always pointing past himself.

**Tension 4: The Jubilee that wasn't.** Most historians of Israel agree the Jubilee year of Leviticus 25 was rarely if ever practically implemented. The *deror* (release) was an ideal, not a sustained social institution. Jesus's announcement that the Jubilee is *fulfilled today* therefore claims a fulfillment that Israel itself had never seen. Christian tradition has read this as the gospel: the Jubilee Israel could not enact, Christ enacts. The challenge: Christian history has often treated the Jubilee as *spiritualized* (forgiveness of sin) at the expense of its economic-structural content. Pope John Paul II's call for debt-relief in the Jubilee year 2000 attempted to recover the structural reading.

**Where this leaves us.** Isaiah 61 is the chapter where the Spirit-anointed mission announces a comprehensive restoration — economic, social, emotional, covenantal — and where Jesus made his most consequential claim about himself. The omission of *the day of vengeance* is not an exegetical detail; it is a theological architecture. It is also a question Jews and Christians answer differently. The Christian reading splits 61:2 across two advents; the Jewish reading holds it together as a single redemptive vision. Both are honest readings of the text; neither is forced.

The contemporary question — what is *good news to the poor* — has economic, political, ecclesial, and Spirit-empowerment dimensions, and Christian traditions have weighted these differently across the centuries. Liberation theology, Pentecostal Spirit-anointing, Catholic social teaching, Reformed gospel-preaching all draw water from this well, sometimes pulling in different directions. The text resists capture by any single tradition.

**Closing question.** If Jesus stopped reading at *the year of the Lord's favor* and left *the day of vengeance* unread, what does it mean for the church to live in the unread half of the verse — to preach Jubilee-favor while a world groaning under unjudged evil waits, and waits, for vengeance not yet come? Is the delay mercy, or is it complicity?
