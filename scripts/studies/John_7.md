# John 7: Tabernacles, Living Water, and the Hidden Messiah

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

The chapter places Jesus inside one of the great pilgrimage festivals of the Jewish liturgical year: ἡ ἑορτὴ τῶν Ἰουδαίων ἡ σκηνοπηγία (7:2), "the Jews' Feast of Booths." Tabernacles (*Sukkot*, סוכות) was one of the three pilgrimage feasts (Deut 16:16), held the 15th-22nd of Tishrei (roughly late September to mid-October). Originally an agricultural harvest festival (Lev 23:33-43), it had by the late Second Temple period accumulated rich symbolic and liturgical significance: the booths (*sukkot*) commemorated Israel's wilderness wandering, while two distinctive temple ceremonies — the water-pouring rite (*nisuk ha-mayim*) and the lamp-lighting in the Court of Women — gave the feast a dramatic public character.

The Mishnah's tractate *Sukkah*, codified around 200 CE but preserving earlier oral tradition, is our richest source for these ceremonies. *Sukkah* 4-5 describes the water-pouring rite: each morning of the feast, a priest would descend with a golden flagon to the Pool of Siloam (חַשִּׁילוֹחַ, *Shiloach* — "the sent one"), draw water, return through the Water Gate to the temple, and pour it on the altar simultaneously with the morning wine libation. The rite was understood as a celebration of God's provision of water in the wilderness (recalling the rock at Horeb, Exodus 17) and as eschatological prayer for rain — Zechariah 14:16-17 had explicitly tied Tabernacles observance to God's provision of rain. *Sukkah* 5:1 famously says: "He who has not seen the joy of the place of water-drawing has never seen joy in his life."

The lamp-lighting ceremony (*Sukkah* 5:2-4) was equally dramatic. In the Court of Women, four enormous golden candelabra were erected, each fifty cubits high (about 75 feet), with four bowls each. Priests climbed ladders to fill them with oil, using the worn-out tunics of priests as wicks. The Mishnah says the light was so great that "there was not a courtyard in Jerusalem that did not reflect the light of the place of water-drawing." Around these lamps, the most pious men of Jerusalem danced through the night with torches, singing songs of praise. The ceremony commemorated the pillar of fire that led Israel through the wilderness.

Both ceremonies frame John 7-8 directly. Jesus's "if anyone thirsts, let him come to me and drink… out of his heart will flow rivers of living water" (7:37-38) is delivered "on the last day of the feast, the great day" (7:37) — almost certainly the seventh day, *Hoshana Rabbah*, when the water-pouring procession reached its climax with seven circuits of the altar. And his "I am the light of the world" in 8:12 is delivered in continuity, against the backdrop of the great menorah lights.

Mary Coloe's *God Dwells With Us: Temple Symbolism in the Fourth Gospel* (2001) has been foundational for reading John 7-8 against this liturgical backdrop. Brown (Anchor Bible) and Köstenberger (BECNT) both develop the temple-festival framework extensively. The point is not just decorative: Jesus is explicitly claiming to embody what the temple ceremonies enact.

The Pool of Siloam itself — the source of the water for the rite — was rediscovered in stages. The traditional Byzantine "Pool of Siloam" near the southern end of Hezekiah's Tunnel was long known. But in 2004, drainage work in the City of David exposed a much larger first-century stepped pool, excavated subsequently by Ronny Reich and Eli Shukron. This is almost certainly the Second-Temple-period Siloam, the actual site of the Tabernacles water-drawing.

Three Greek terms anchor the chapter:

**σκηνοπηγία** (*skēnopēgia*) — literally "tent-pitching," John's term for Tabernacles (7:2). The cognate verb *skēnoō* — "to tent, dwell" — is what John uses in 1:14 ("the Word became flesh and *dwelt* among us"). The verbal echo is intentional: Jesus *tabernacles* among Israel during the festival of *tabernacling*.

**ζῶν ὕδωρ** (*zōn hydōr*, "living water") — at 7:38, technical for fresh / flowing water (as opposed to cistern water), with rich Old Testament resonance: Jeremiah 2:13 (YHWH the fountain of living water), Zechariah 14:8 (living waters flowing from Jerusalem in the eschatological day), Ezekiel 47 (river flowing from the temple). 

**δίδραχμον** is not in our text, but **καιρός** (*kairos*, 7:6, 7:8) — "appointed time" or "season" — is. Jesus's "my *kairos* has not yet come" plays against his brothers' "*your* time is always here." The contrast is between Jesus's eschatologically-determined hour and ordinary chronological time.

## Lens 2 — The Literature

Structurally, John 7 inaugurates the long Tabernacles-narrative that runs through 8:59 (or 10:21, depending on how one chunks the dedication-feast material). Brown reads chapters 7-8 as a single dramatic unit; Köstenberger and Carson generally treat them together; Bultmann's reordering hypothesis (which moved chapter 7's halves around to "restore" original order) has been mostly abandoned. The literary architecture is dialogic and dramatic — it reads more like a play than a discourse, with Jesus moving through public and private spaces, with crowd voices providing chorus, with chief priests and Pharisees as antagonists.

The chapter contains seven distinct dialogue scenes: with his brothers (7:1-9), with the searching crowd (7:10-13), with the temple crowd on his teaching (7:14-24), with the Jerusalemites on his identity (7:25-31), with the agents sent to arrest him (7:32-36), with the festival crowd at the climax (7:37-44), and finally the temple police's report and Nicodemus's intervention (7:45-52). The compressed, scenic structure has led Mark Stibbe (*John as Storyteller*) and R. Alan Culpepper (*Anatomy of the Fourth Gospel*) to read the chapter as showcase for John's narrative artistry.

The chapter is also the gospel's most concentrated study in **Johannine misunderstanding** — a literary technique Culpepper identified as one of the gospel's signatures. Multiple parties get Jesus wrong in distinct ways: his brothers misread his reluctance as cowardice (7:3-5); "the Jews" debate his origin against false Davidic / Bethlehemite criteria (7:41-42 — they don't know Jesus was born in Bethlehem, but the dramatic irony is double-layered: even *if* they knew, they would still need to reckon with his heavenly origin); the crowd debates whether the messiah's origin should be hidden (7:27, reflecting a Jewish tradition that the messiah would be hidden until revealed — found in Justin Martyr's *Dialogue with Trypho* 8 and 110, and in 1 Enoch 48:6-7 and 4 Ezra 13:51-52).

The most famous **textual-critical and grammatical crux** in John 7 is verses 37-39: "If anyone thirsts, let him come to me and drink. Whoever believes in me, as the Scripture has said, 'Out of his heart will flow rivers of living water.'" The Greek punctuation is undetermined in early manuscripts (which had no punctuation), and two readings have warred for centuries:

- **Christological reading**: "If anyone thirsts, let him come to me. And let him drink who believes in me. As the Scripture has said, out of *his* (Christ's) heart will flow rivers of living water." On this reading, the source of the rivers is Christ. This is favored by the Western Fathers (Justin, Hippolytus, Cyprian, ambiguously by Origen), by some Eastern Fathers, and by Brown (Anchor Bible). Brown's argument: the Old Testament background (Ezekiel 47 — water flowing from the temple; Zechariah 14:8) makes Christ-as-temple the natural reading; the κοιλία ("belly," "heart," "innermost being") fits temple imagery (cf. 19:34, water from Jesus's side); and the Spirit theme of 7:39 specifies Christ as the one who gives the Spirit.

- **Believer reading**: "If anyone thirsts, let him come to me and drink, whoever believes in me. As the Scripture has said, out of *his* (the believer's) heart will flow rivers of living water." This is favored by Origen (in some passages), most Greek Fathers, the medieval tradition, the AV/KJV, and most modern English translations including ESV. The argument: Greek syntax most naturally reads ἐκ τῆς κοιλίας αὐτοῦ as referring back to "the one who believes."

Carson, Köstenberger, Beasley-Murray, and Thompson lean Christological; Schnackenburg and Brown more so; Calvin and the Reformed tradition tend to read believer; ESV (and most contemporary English versions) reflect the believer reading textually but allow for Christological in commentary. The narrator's gloss in 7:39 ("this he said about the Spirit") settles the *referent* of the living water (the Spirit) but not the syntactic question of *whose* heart is the source.

The **Scripture citation** in 7:38 ("as the Scripture has said") has no precise Old Testament referent — it appears to be a pastiche of Isaiah 12:3, 44:3, 58:11, Zechariah 14:8, Ezekiel 47:1-12, and possibly Proverbs 18:4. Brown discusses this as a "midrashic" citation, similar to other Johannine Scripture-cluster references.

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

Patristic engagement with John 7 cluster around several questions. The "the Christ comes from Bethlehem" debate (7:41-42) generated extensive harmonizing exposition: Origen, Chrysostom, Cyril, and Augustine all engage the apparent ignorance of Jesus's Bethlehem birth among "the Jews." Augustine's *Tractates* 28-32 walk through the chapter with characteristic theological care, treating the brothers' unbelief, Jesus's hidden ascent to the feast, and the living water saying.

Origen's *Commentary on John* (Book 13 covers 4:13-54 and adjacent material; later books touched on chapter 7) reads the living water as the Spirit-mediated knowledge of Scripture. Chrysostom's *88 Homilies* (especially homilies 48-52) gives lively pastoral exposition, attentive to crowd dynamics and Jesus's strategic teaching. Cyril of Alexandria reads 7:37-39 in Spirit-Christological key. Aquinas's *Lectura super Joannem* on chapter 7 discusses the question of Jesus's brothers (advancing the Catholic perpetual-virginity reading: the *adelphoi* are kin, not bloodbrothers — a position challenged by the Helvidian tradition Jerome critiqued, which Protestants generally accept).

The question of Jesus's brothers (ἀδελφοί) deserves note. Three positions persist:

- **Helvidian / Protestant**: Mary's biological children with Joseph after Jesus's birth (Tertullian, Helvidius, the Reformed tradition, most modern Protestant scholarship).
- **Epiphanian**: Joseph's children from a previous marriage (Greek Orthodox tradition, Eastern Fathers, some Anglicans).
- **Hieronymian / Roman Catholic**: cousins or other kin (Jerome's *Against Helvidius*, Roman Catholic dogma since Pope Damasus, *Catechism of the Catholic Church* 499-500).

Calvin's *Commentary on John* on chapter 7 is rich. Calvin's exposition of 7:17 ("if anyone's will is to do God's will, he will know whether the teaching is from God") is widely cited as an epistemological claim about the connection between obedience and theological understanding — the moral preconditions of theological knowledge. Calvin reads 7:37-39 as Christological with strong pneumatological emphasis.

Modern Catholic engagement runs through Joseph Ratzinger / Benedict XVI's *Jesus of Nazareth* (the temple-festival framing is central), through von Balthasar's *Theo-Drama* (the narrative-dramatic structure of the chapter is exactly the kind of theological drama Balthasar privileges). Karl Barth's *Church Dogmatics* engages the "I have not come of my own accord" theme as Christological self-disclosure.

Twentieth-century Johannine scholarship is summarized in the major commentaries: Brown (Anchor Bible, two volumes, foundational), Schnackenburg (HthKNT), Bultmann (existential reading; he reordered chapters 5-7 to put 7:15-24 with chapter 5), Barrett (CGTC, careful Greek), Beasley-Murray (WBC), Köstenberger (BECNT), Carson (PNTC), Keener (two volumes, massive Greco-Roman context), Thompson (NTL), Moloney (Sacra Pagina), O'Day (NIB), Newbigin (*The Light Has Come*), Brant (Paideia, narrative-dramatic). Mary Coloe's temple-symbolism work and Stibbe's narrative criticism are essential supplements. Catrin Williams's *I Am He* is critical for the Christological self-disclosure that builds through these chapters.

The Jewish interpretive trajectory illuminates John 7 differently. Daniel Boyarin's *Jewish Gospels* and *Border Lines* situate Johannine Christology within late-Second-Temple Jewish messianic plurality — including the "two-powers in heaven" tradition that the rabbis later anathematized. The hidden-messiah motif of 7:27 reflects authentic Jewish tradition (4 Ezra, 1 Enoch). Adele Reinhartz, in *Cast Out of the Covenant*, attends to how the chapter's "the Jews" language (especially in 7:1, 7:11, 7:13, 7:35, 7:1) functions: Reinhartz argues this is increasingly the language of a community in conflict with the synagogue, with potential for supersessionist deployment. J. Louis Martyn's *History and Theology in the Fourth Gospel* (originally 1968) influentially read the chapter against the *birkat ha-minim* — the rabbinic curse against heretics inserted into the synagogue prayer in the late first century, allegedly forcing Christian Jews out of the synagogue. Martyn's reconstruction has been challenged (the dating and content of the *birkat* are uncertain; Boyarin and others argue Christian/Jewish separation was more gradual than Martyn allowed), but it shaped a generation of Johannine community history.

Amy-Jill Levine's *Misunderstood Jew*, John Ashton's *Understanding the Fourth Gospel*, and Charlesworth's *John and Qumran* round out Jewish-context reading. The dispersion-among-the-Greeks reference in 7:35 is a striking textual artifact of the gospel's awareness of Diaspora Jewish life.

Feminist readings — Sandra Schneiders's *Written That You May Believe* and Reinhartz — note that John 7's discourse is overwhelmingly male; women's voices are absent (the Samaritan woman of chapter 4 stands as a marked contrast).

## Lens 4 — The Theological Intent

**Tension One: How do we read Jesus's apparent deceit in 7:8-10 — saying "I am not going up to this feast" and then going?**

*Position 1: The textual-variant escape.* Some early manuscripts (𝔓⁶⁶*, Codex Bezae) read "I am not *yet* (οὔπω) going up." The variant softens the apparent contradiction. Many ancient interpreters — Chrysostom, Augustine in places, Cyril — preferred this reading. Steelman: the variant is well-attested in early manuscripts and resolves the moral problem; *oupō* fits Johannine vocabulary. Counter-steelman: most modern critical editions (NA28, UBS5) read οὐκ ("not") as more difficult and therefore more probably original; *oupō* looks like scribal harmonization.

*Position 2: Truthful in context.* Jesus's "I am not going up to *this* feast" means going up *publicly, as a pilgrim, with my brothers, on the festal procession*. He goes up secretly, on his own time, by his own appointed *kairos* — a different mode of attendance. Steelman: Jesus's larger statement is about *kairos* ("my time has not yet come," 7:6, 7:8), and the contrast with his brothers' assumption that he should go up as a public spectacle ("show yourself to the world," 7:4) is the chapter's point. The change-of-mind reading misses what Jesus is saying about the timing and mode of his self-disclosure.

*Position 3: Honest change of plans.* Jesus says one thing and then does another, on prophetic prerogative — like Jonah, like Jeremiah, like figures whose actions are dictated by the unfolding will of God rather than their stated intentions. Steelman: this preserves Jesus's freedom and agency without strain; the chapter does not mark his action as deceptive. Counter-steelman: traditional Christian theology has resisted attributing falsehood or even apparent falsehood to Jesus, and this reading has historically been a minority position.

*Where this leaves us:* The middle reading — that Jesus is refusing his brothers' framing of how and when he should go up to the feast — is probably the strongest exegetically. Jesus's "*kairos*" language throughout the chapter is doing real work: he refuses the public-relations theology his brothers are pushing, then fulfills his own appointed time on his own terms. The chapter is a study in the difference between human chronology (always available, always now) and eschatological *kairos* (appointed, withheld, given).

**Tension Two: What does Jesus mean by "if anyone's will is to do God's will, he will know" (7:17)?**

*Position 1: Moral epistemology.* Calvin, Augustine, Newman, and a long tradition read 7:17 as a claim about the moral preconditions of theological knowledge. Truth is not received by detached intellect alone; it is received in the will's posture toward God's will. The verse is an answer to Pilate's "what is truth?" before Pilate asks it. Steelman: the verse's structure is conditional and epistemological; the will's orientation is the prerequisite for the intellect's recognition. This is a deep claim about how religious knowledge works, and it has shaped centuries of Christian spirituality.

*Position 2: Polemical specifics.* The verse is not a general epistemology but a specific claim against Jesus's specific opponents: their failure to "do God's will" (specifically, to recognize Jesus as God's sent one) is what blocks their recognition of his teaching. Steelman: the chapter's narrative context is polemical; Jesus is responding to those who marvel at his learning while seeking to kill him.

*Position 3: Pneumatological reading.* The "knowing" of 7:17 is the Spirit-given recognition that John 7:39 (later in the chapter) anticipates. The verse describes how the Spirit operates, not how human moral effort produces knowledge. Steelman: 7:17 fits within Johannine pneumatology; the Paraclete teaches and reminds (14:26), and the Spirit of truth guides into all truth (16:13).

*Where this leaves us:* The three readings can be held together. Theological knowledge is morally conditioned, contextually polemical, and pneumatologically given. What 7:17 forecloses is the assumption that pure objective inquiry, detached from the will's orientation, can yield religious truth. Modernity's epistemological assumptions (Kant onward) sit uneasily with this verse, which is why it remained powerful for figures like Newman, Bonhoeffer, and contemporary theologians of the moral self.

**One unresolved question:** What does 7:39 mean — "the Spirit had not been given, because Jesus was not yet glorified"? The Spirit of God is active throughout the Old Testament; the Spirit descends on Jesus at his baptism (1:32-33); how can John say the Spirit "had not been given"? Brown, Carson, and most commentators take it as a claim about a particular *post-glorification* mode of Spirit-presence — the Pentecost-Paraclete sending — distinct from Spirit-activity prior to Jesus's death and resurrection. But this raises questions about continuity and discontinuity in pneumatology that the chapter does not resolve and that feed into the long debate over the *filioque*, the dating of Spirit-reception (chapter 20:22 versus Acts 2), and the relationship of Old-Testament Spirit-activity to New-Covenant Spirit-indwelling.

## The Thread

The *skēnoō* of 1:14 ("the Word became flesh and tabernacled among us") meets its festival in chapter 7's *skēnopēgia*. The living-water theme echoes back to chapter 4 (the Samaritan woman, "a spring of water welling up to eternal life") and forward to 19:34 (water and blood from Jesus's pierced side) — the same image at three points in the gospel, deepening as it returns. The "I will be with you a little longer, and then I am going to him who sent me" (7:33-34) anticipates the Farewell Discourse of 13-17 and the high-priestly prayer of 17. And Nicodemus's quiet defense of due process (7:50-51) closes a small narrative arc that began in chapter 3 (Nicodemus by night) and will reach its end in chapter 19, when he and Joseph of Arimathea bury Jesus — the only character John traces from skeptical inquiry to costly discipleship.
