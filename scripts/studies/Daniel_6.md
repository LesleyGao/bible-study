# Daniel 6: The Lions' Den and the Politics of Conscience

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

The chapter opens with a Persian administrative reorganization — 120 satraps over the kingdom, three high officials over them, Daniel one of the three — and immediately presents the most stubborn historical problem in the book of Daniel: who is Darius the Mede? The text places him as the king who "received the kingdom" (5:31, 6:1) immediately after the fall of Babylon in 539 BC, ruling at age sixty-two. The historical record contains no Median king named Darius in this period. Cyrus the Great, a Persian, conquered Babylon. Darius I "the Great" came to the Persian throne in 522 BC, more than a decade later, and he was Persian, not Median. The Greek and Babylonian sources — Herodotus, Xenophon's *Cyropaedia*, the Nabonidus Chronicle, the Cyrus Cylinder, the Behistun Inscription — know nothing of an interim Median monarch.

The proposals fall into a few camps. **Donald J. Wiseman**, in *Notes on Some Problems in the Book of Daniel* (1965), argued that "Darius the Mede" is a throne name or alternate designation for Cyrus the Persian himself, treating Daniel 6:28 ("during the reign of Darius and the reign of Cyrus the Persian") as a Hebrew waw-explicative — "during the reign of Darius, that is, the reign of Cyrus." The grammatical move is plausible in attested Aramaic and Hebrew constructions, and Wiseman supports it with the observation that Cyrus had Median lineage on his mother's side and ruled Babylon initially through Median administrative structures. **John C. Whitcomb**'s *Darius the Mede* (1959) argued instead for identifying him with Gubaru (Ugbaru in some texts), the general who took Babylon for Cyrus and is recorded in the Nabonidus Chronicle as having been installed as governor and as having died shortly after — in this view, "Darius" is a regnal designation distinct from the personal name. **Stephen Miller** in the *New American Commentary* and **Joyce Baldwin** in the *Tyndale Old Testament Commentary* survey both options and lean toward Gubaru while acknowledging the Wiseman alternative.

The critical reading, represented by **John J. Collins** in the Hermeneia volume, **Carol Newsom** in the Old Testament Library volume, **Lester Grabbe**, **H. H. Rowley** (whose 1935 *Darius the Mede and the Four World Empires in the Book of Daniel* remains the definitive critical treatment), and **James Montgomery** in the *International Critical Commentary*, treats Darius the Mede as a literary construct. The figure conflates traditions about Darius I and the Median predecessors of Persia into a single character whose function is to fill the four-kingdoms schema's slot for Media. The schema in chapters 2 and 7 — Babylon, Media, Persia, Greece — requires a Median kingdom between Babylon and Persia. History knows no such kingdom; the Medes were absorbed by Cyrus before he took Babylon. The redactor who needed a Median king to make the schema work invented or inherited Darius the Mede. The 120 satraps is the giveaway: Darius I organized the empire into roughly twenty satrapies, while 120 is closer to the administrative total under later Persian kings, and the number reappears in the Greek versions of Esther 1:1 (where Ahasuerus rules over 127 provinces). The "law of the Medes and Persians that cannot be revoked" (6:8, 12, 15) is a literary topos that recurs in Esther 1:19 and 8:8 and is otherwise unattested in Persian administrative practice — Persian kings frequently revoked their own decrees.

The material culture of the chapter is genuinely Persian. The satrapal system was established by Cyrus and refined by Darius I; the term *šatrap* is Old Persian *xšaçapāvan*, "protector of the realm." Royal seals and signets are well attested in the Persepolis Fortification Tablets. The lions' den itself has parallels in Assyrian and Persian royal practice — Ashurbanipal's lion-hunt reliefs (now in the British Museum) attest to the keeping of lions for royal entertainment, and Persian kings continued the practice. The three-times-daily prayer toward Jerusalem (6:10) draws on Solomon's prayer in 1 Kings 8:48, which establishes Jerusalem as the orientation point for diaspora prayer; the practice of *minhah*, *shaharit*, and *ma'ariv* — three daily prayers — is rabbinically codified in *Berakhot* but has roots in Second Temple piety, and Psalm 55:17 ("evening and morning and at noon I utter my complaint") is the likely scriptural backdrop.

The dating of the book of Daniel runs through chapter 6 differently than through chapter 5. Where chapter 5's historical problems concern Belshazzar's lineage (a soluble issue under accession-language conventions), chapter 6's problems concern the existence of a kingdom (Media after Babylon) and a king (Darius the Mede) for which the conservative tradition has had to construct identifications rather than reconcile descriptions. **Wiseman**'s and **Whitcomb**'s reconstructions are honest scholarly attempts and deserve a fair hearing. The critical reading, dominant since **Porphyry of Tyre**'s third-century argument and consolidated by **S. R. Driver**, **R. H. Charles**, and **James Montgomery**, treats the difficulty as one piece of converging evidence for a Maccabean redaction in which the book's author worked from a four-kingdoms schema rather than from sixth-century Babylonian and Persian primary sources.

The textual situation includes the Aramaic block (2:4b–7:28), of which chapter 6 is the penultimate panel. The Old Greek (LXX) version of chapter 6 differs from Theodotion's revision and from the Masoretic text in ways that suggest two recensions; the LXX has the conspirators' children and wives thrown into the den (a detail the Masoretic text preserves at 6:24) while expanding the doxological material. The **Greek Additions to Daniel** include Bel and the Dragon, which contains a second lions'-den narrative — Daniel is thrown to seven lions for six days and is fed by Habakkuk, who is transported by an angel from Judea — clearly a parallel tradition that draws on chapter 6 and may reflect a developing Daniel cycle in late Second Temple Judaism.

Key terms anchor the chapter. *Dāt* (דָּת, Aramaic "law / decree," a Persian loanword from *dāta*) appears repeatedly in the chapter (6:5, 6:8, 6:12, 6:15) — its Persian origin is itself a chronological marker; *dāt* enters Aramaic in the Persian period and remains a technical term. *Qodam* (קֳדָם, "before," used of standing before God or king) marks the constant ambiguity of Daniel's posture — before whom does he ultimately stand? *Tushlan* (תֻּשְׁלַחַת, "deliver / rescue") is the verbal root in Darius's question and Daniel's reply (6:14, 16, 20). *Bar Elahin* (בַּר אֱלָהִין, Aramaic "son of the gods" — Dan 3:25) is not in chapter 6 but its angelological cousin, the *malak* (מַלְאָךְ, "messenger / angel") who shuts the lions' mouths (6:22), is the figure who returns in chapter 7's heavenly courtroom and chapters 10–12's apocalyptic mediation.

## Lens 2 — The Literature

Chapter 6 is a court tale of vindication, the structural counterpart to chapter 3 in the Aramaic block's chiasm. **Albin Lenglet**'s 1972 chiastic analysis of Daniel 2–7, accepted by Collins, Newsom, and most subsequent commentators, pairs chapters 3 and 6 (deliverance from execution by foreign king's decree). The parallels are tight: a royal decree that conflicts with Jewish observance (image-worship in 3, prayer-prohibition in 6); refusal to comply on theological grounds; condemnation to a public execution by an element historically associated with judgment (fire, lions); miraculous deliverance through divine agency; royal proclamation of the God of Israel; punishment of the accusers. The differences are illuminating. In chapter 3, three Hebrews face the furnace; in chapter 6, Daniel alone faces the lions. In chapter 3, the king is the active persecutor; in chapter 6, the king is a reluctant agent manipulated by his own officials. In chapter 3, the deliverance comes through a fourth figure in the fire; in chapter 6, it comes through an angel who shuts the lions' mouths. The progression sharpens the political theology: the threat to faithful presence in pagan administration is not always the king — sometimes it is the system, the law that has trapped the king himself.

The chapter's narrative pacing is unusually careful. The opening (6:1–3) establishes Daniel's preeminence; the middle (6:4–9) traces the conspiracy from motive to legal instrument; the central panel (6:10–18) shows Daniel's prayer, accusation, condemnation, and the king's distress; the deliverance (6:19–23) is brief and almost matter-of-fact; the aftermath (6:24–28) handles the punishment of the conspirators and the king's decree. The narrator declines to dramatize the night in the den — a detail the post-biblical tradition (Bel and the Dragon, the medieval mystery plays) consistently expanded. The restraint is theological: the chapter is not about Daniel's heroism but about God's faithfulness to a faithful man.

The royal-decree form that closes chapters 3, 4, and 6 is here at its most polished. Darius's proclamation in 6:25–27 is a poetic doxology — the closest the Aramaic block comes to a hymn — that names God as "the living God, enduring forever," whose "kingdom shall never be destroyed." The phrasing anticipates the apocalyptic visions of chapter 7 verbatim: "his dominion is an everlasting dominion, and his kingdom shall never be destroyed" (7:14). The court-tale section is preparing the apocalyptic section's vocabulary. **Klaus Koch**'s observation in *Das Buch Daniel* (1980) that the Aramaic block is structurally a single composition — not loose tales — runs through this verbal echo.

Genre-wise the chapter is what **Lawrence Wills** (*The Jewish Novel in the Ancient World*, 1995) calls the "tale of the courtier under threat" — a Hellenistic-period diaspora narrative form that includes Joseph in Egypt, Esther at Susa, Tobit and Ahikar in Nineveh, and Daniel in Babylon-Persia. The form's function is paradigmatic: it teaches a dispersion community how to navigate the tension between political integration (Daniel rises to the top of Persian administration) and religious fidelity (he refuses to compromise on prayer). The chapter does not resolve the tension; it dramatizes it.

Canonical placement matters. Daniel 6 sits in the Hebrew Bible's *Ketuvim* alongside Esther — and the parallels with Esther are deep. Both stories center on a court conspiracy against a Jewish official; both turn on an irrevocable Persian decree; both end with the conspirators destroyed and the king issuing a counter-decree. Esther was likely composed in dialogue with Daniel or vice versa; the *Babylonian Talmud, Megillah* 13b explicitly pairs them. Christian Bibles since the Septuagint move Daniel into the prophets, which reframes chapter 6 as an apocalyptic prelude rather than a wisdom-literature companion to Esther.

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

Chapter 6 sits in the canonical arc as the bridge between exile-as-punishment and exile-as-vocation. The early prophetic tradition (Jeremiah, Ezekiel) reads exile as Yahweh's covenant judgment on Judah's idolatry and injustice; the later tradition begins to read it as Israel's mission to the nations. The Servant Songs of Isaiah 40–55 are the theological hinge; Daniel 6 is its narrative realization. Daniel does not return from Babylon. He stays. He rises in foreign administration. He prays toward Jerusalem but does not go there. The chapter's vision is of a faithful presence in pagan power that does not require either assimilation or withdrawal.

The Jewish trajectory of the chapter is rich. **1 Maccabees 2:60** — the dying Mattathias's exhortation to his sons — cites chapter 6 directly: "Daniel, because of his innocence, was delivered from the mouth of the lions." The chapter functioned in the Maccabean period as a paradigm of resistance to Hellenization, and the Maccabean redaction of Daniel may be the moment at which the chapter received its current literary form. **Josephus**, *Antiquities* 10.10–11, paraphrases chapter 6 with elaborations and treats Daniel as a virtually divinely inspired statesman. The Babylonian Talmud, *Berakhot* 31b, reads Daniel's three-times-daily prayer as the scriptural basis for the rabbinic three-prayer system; *Sanhedrin* 95a discusses the lions; *Sotah* 12b treats the conspirators' destruction. **Saadia Gaon**'s tenth-century commentary, **Rashi**, **Ibn Ezra**, and **Don Isaac Abrabanel** (whose extensive fifteenth-century commentary remains the most thorough Jewish treatment) read the chapter as a paradigm of *kiddush hashem* — the sanctification of the divine name through public faithfulness. Modern Jewish scholarship — **Robert Gordis**, **Aaron Koller** in *Esther in Ancient Jewish Thought* (with comparative attention to Daniel) — emphasizes the diaspora-political reading: how to be a Jew in foreign power without becoming foreign or fleeing.

The Christian trajectory begins with **Hippolytus of Rome's *Commentary on Daniel*** (c. 200 AD), the earliest extant Christian biblical commentary, which reads Daniel in the lions' den as a type of the resurrection — sealed in a stone-mouthed pit at evening, alive at dawn, vindicated. The typology is structural and was decisive for the chapter's reception. **Tertullian** uses Daniel 6 in *De Oratione* as a paradigm of prayer that costs. **Jerome's *Commentary on Daniel*** (407 AD), composed against Porphyry, defends the chapter's historicity and reads Daniel's posture as the church's posture under Roman persecution. **Theodoret of Cyrus** in the fifth century follows Jerome. The medieval reception is enormous — Daniel in the lions' den becomes one of the most depicted scenes in Christian art, from the Roman catacombs (third century) through Bernward of Hildesheim's bronze doors (c. 1015) to Rubens. **Aquinas**'s *Commentary on Daniel* and the *Glossa Ordinaria* read the chapter through the typology of resurrection. **Luther's *Preface to Daniel*** (1530) reads it as a paradigm of Christian witness under tyranny; **Calvin's *Lectures on Daniel*** (1561) is exegetically careful, defends the chapter's historicity, and uses Daniel's prayer as a paradigm of public faithfulness.

The critical trajectory begins with **Porphyry of Tyre** (preserved in Jerome) and runs through Bertholdt, Driver, R. H. Charles, Montgomery, Rowley, and into the contemporary consensus represented by Collins, Newsom, **Maurice Casey**, **Philip Davies**, and Grabbe. The contemporary critical reading treats chapter 6 as a court tale of late Persian or early Hellenistic origin, redacted into the Maccabean book. The Darius-the-Mede problem and the "law of the Medes and Persians" topos are read as evidence for a redactor working from literary tradition rather than primary historical sources.

Where the trajectories diverge is on the historical question (Wiseman/Whitcomb/Miller's identification of Darius vs. the critical literary-construct reading) and on the political-theological question (whether the chapter authorizes resistance, accommodation, or both). The Jewish reading has historically emphasized faithful presence; the Christian reading has historically emphasized resurrection typology; the critical reading has emphasized resistance literature; and the modern political theology of conscience pulls all three together.

## Lens 4 — The Theological Intent

The first tension is the politics of civil disobedience. Daniel obeys the decree by his actions toward king and law; he disobeys by continuing his prayer. He does not hide; he prays publicly toward open windows facing Jerusalem (6:10), at the same hours, in the same posture, "as he had done previously." The chapter does not authorize quietism — Daniel's act is visible, deliberate, and unmistakable as resistance. But it also does not authorize revolt — Daniel does not rally allies, organize opposition, or denounce the king. The mode is conscientious refusal, executed in plain sight, accepting the legal consequence.

The traditions read this differently. The **Anabaptist** tradition, codified in the **Schleitheim Confession** of 1527 and developed by **John Howard Yoder** in *The Politics of Jesus* (1972), treats Daniel as a paradigm of nonviolent witness that accepts the cost of refusal without taking up the sword. The **Reformed** tradition of resistance, developed in **Theodore Beza**'s *De jure magistratuum* (1574) and the anonymous *Vindiciae Contra Tyrannos* (1579), treats Daniel as a paradigm of lawful resistance through proper channels — Daniel does not lead a rebellion, but his refusal authorizes lesser magistrates to resist tyrannical decrees. The **Catholic** tradition, articulated by **Aquinas** in *Summa Theologica* II-II q. 104 and developed in Vatican II's *Dignitatis Humanae* (1965), distinguishes positive law from natural and divine law and treats unjust decrees as not binding in conscience — a tradition that runs through John Paul II's writings on the Polish church under communism. The **Eastern Orthodox** tradition, expressed in the writings of confessors like **Solzhenitsyn** and **Patriarch Tikhon** of Moscow under early Soviet rule, treats Daniel's posture as the paradigm of *martyria* — witness through endurance.

**Dietrich Bonhoeffer**'s *Ethics* (composed 1940–43, published posthumously) represents the hardest and most consequential modern reading. Bonhoeffer used Daniel 3 and 6 as paradigms of Christian conscience under totalitarian power but went beyond them — his participation in the Abwehr conspiracy against Hitler was not what Daniel did, and Bonhoeffer knew it. The question Daniel 6 leaves is whether conscientious refusal is the maximum a Christian (or Jew) may do, or whether deeper resistance — what Bonhoeffer called "the boundary case of free responsibility" — is sometimes required. The chapter does not answer.

Steelman the quietist reading. Daniel does nothing political. He prays. He prays at home. He prays toward Jerusalem. He does not denounce Darius, organize the Jewish exiles, or threaten the satraps. The chapter's political theology is exhausted by faithfulness in the room with the door shut. Everything else — the lions, the angel, Darius's decree — is God's work, not Daniel's. The reading is honest to the text and is the reason the chapter has been so important to traditions of suffering rather than activist witness. Where this leaves us: the chapter authorizes prayer as resistance more than it authorizes resistance as policy.

The second tension is the desolation problem in the conservative-critical divide. If the historical Darius the Mede is genuinely a literary construct (Collins, Newsom, the critical consensus), what does it cost to read Daniel as Scripture? The Catholic position, articulated in **Vatican II's *Dei Verbum*** (1965), holds that Scripture teaches "without error" the truth God willed for the sake of salvation, leaving room for incidental historical compression — chapter 6's theological truth (faithful presence in pagan power, divine deliverance through human cost) is not threatened by the historicity of Darius the Mede. The Eastern Orthodox tradition similarly distinguishes salvific intent from incidental detail. Protestants split. The conservative evangelical position (Miller, Archer, Baldwin, Wiseman, the Chicago Statement on Biblical Inerrancy 1978) treats the historical reconstruction (Darius = Cyrus or Gubaru) as defensible and necessary, on the grounds that any concession on detail erodes the doctrine. The mainline Protestant position (**Brevard Childs**'s canonical approach, the Yale school) treats the figure as canonically incorporated and theologically inert — what matters is Darius's narrative function, not his historicity. Jewish readings have never required strict historical inerrancy. Critical scholarship is uninterested in the doctrinal stakes.

Steelman Wiseman's reconstruction. Cyrus the Persian had Median lineage; he ruled Babylon initially through Median structures; the Aramaic *waw* in 6:28 can be explicative ("Darius, that is, Cyrus"); the throne-name use of "Darius" is attested in late tradition. The reading is not absurd. It is also not comfortable; it requires a series of plausible but unprovable moves. Where this leaves us: the chapter, more than chapter 5, forces a decision about what kind of historical accuracy the doctrine of Scripture requires, and the answer is not obvious from the text alone.

The unresolved question. Daniel prays toward Jerusalem from a city he will never leave. He becomes a Persian official under a Persian-Median king who is barely recognizable in the historical record. He never returns to the Land. He dies in exile. The chapter ends with his prosperity (6:28) but not with his homecoming. What is the theological status of a faithful diaspora life that never goes home? The chapter is the New Testament's question — the question of every church scattered across an empire that is not its city — written six centuries early.

## The Thread

The non-obvious cross-reference is **1 Kings 8:48** — Solomon's dedication of the temple, in which he prays that exiles "in the land of their captivity" will pray "toward their land … and the city you have chosen, and the house I have built for your name." Daniel 6:10 is the explicit fulfillment of Solomon's request. The window facing Jerusalem is not Daniel's invention; it is his covenantal obedience to a prayer made three centuries earlier in a temple now destroyed. The chapter is canonical exegesis in narrative form.

The second is **Hebrews 11:33** — "who through faith … stopped the mouths of lions." The author of Hebrews lifts chapter 6 into the gallery of faithful witnesses but does not name Daniel; he assumes the reader will identify the reference. The intertext shapes how early Christians read Daniel: not as a historical hero but as a paradigm of the faith that endures unto vindication. Hebrews 11:38 — "of whom the world was not worthy" — is the closest the New Testament comes to a theology of diaspora.

The third is **Luke 6:11–16** and **Acts 5:27–42** — the parallel structure between Daniel 6 and the early apostolic preaching trials. In both, religious refusal of an official decree leads to legal sanction; in both, the refusal is staged in plain sight; in both, the higher-court verdict (heaven's, not the Sanhedrin's) reverses the lower. Luke's depiction of Peter and John is shaped by Daniel.

The fourth is the iconographic tradition — Daniel in the lions' den is one of the most depicted scenes in the Roman catacombs (third and fourth centuries), where it functioned as a resurrection emblem for Christians awaiting their own deliverance from imperial violence. The image's persistence through Christian art — Bernward's bronze doors (c. 1015), the medieval mystery plays, Rubens — is the chapter's own afterlife as resistance literature in a tradition that frequently lost its political nerve. The catacombs read the chapter better than most subsequent commentaries.
