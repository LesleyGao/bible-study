# Ezekiel 31: The World-Tree, the Garden of God, and the Cedar's Descent to Sheol

## Lens 1 — Historical World

Ezekiel 31 is dated *the eleventh year, in the third month, on the first day* (31:1), which corresponds to **June 21, 587 BC** by the standard chronology — about two months after the dated oracle of 30:20 (April 587 BC) and just weeks before Jerusalem's wall was breached on the ninth of Tammuz (cf. Jer 39:2; 52:6). The chapter therefore comes from the climactic months of the Judean catastrophe, when news of Pharaoh Hophra's failed relief intervention had already reached the *gōlâ* community at the Chebar Canal. **Daniel Block** (NICOT) and **Walther Zimmerli** (Hermeneia) both read the chapter as the prophet's theological response to the dashed Egyptian hope: the *gōlâ* community had pinned its political hopes on Pharaoh's intervention, the intervention had failed, and Ezekiel turns to the deepest mythological-theological register available to deconstruct Egyptian pretension.

The historical reference of the chapter is the **fall of Assyria** (612 BC, the destruction of Nineveh) used as a typological warning to Egypt. Ezekiel 31:3 names *Assyria* (אַשּׁוּר, *ʾaššûr*) explicitly as the cedar comparison-point; some scholars (notably **Cooke**, ICC, and **Eichrodt**, OTL) emend MT *ʾaššûr* to *təʾaššûr* (a kind of cedar or cypress), reading the verse as describing simply *a cedar in Lebanon* without naming Assyria. **Greenberg** (Anchor Yale) and **Block** defend MT *Assyria* on lectio-difficilior grounds and argue that the explicit Assyria-reference is the chapter's point: the cedar that fell most spectacularly within recent geopolitical memory is the typological precedent for Egypt's coming fall.

The fall of Nineveh in 612 BC is well-attested historically. The **Babylonian Chronicle BM 21901** (the "Fall of Nineveh Chronicle," published by **Cyril Gadd** in 1923 and re-edited in **A. K. Grayson's** *Assyrian and Babylonian Chronicles*, 1975) records the alliance of the Babylonian Nabopolassar with the Median Cyaxares and the destruction of Nineveh after a three-month siege. The archaeological record at **Nineveh** (Kuyunjik mound, excavated since the 1840s by **Layard**, **Rassam**, and the British Museum's later expeditions) confirms the burning destruction layer; the **Great Library of Ashurbanipal** with its tens of thousands of cuneiform tablets was sealed by the fire that destroyed the city. Egypt under Pharaoh Necho II had attempted to prop up the rump Assyrian state at Carchemish in 605 BC and lost catastrophically to Nebuchadnezzar (cf. Jer 46:2). The Egyptian-Assyrian connection that Ezek 31 deploys is, therefore, a recent and bitter geopolitical memory: Egypt had bet on Assyria and lost; Egypt was now betting on itself.

The **world-tree** imagery of the chapter draws on Ancient Near Eastern iconographic conventions. The *Sacred Tree* motif appears across Mesopotamian art: **Assyrian palace reliefs at Khorsabad** (Sargon II's palace, excavated by **Botta** in the 1840s and now distributed across the Louvre, the British Museum, and the Iraq Museum) depict winged genii flanking a stylized tree. The *kudurru* boundary stones of Kassite Babylon include tree-iconography that **Othmar Keel** (*The Symbolism of the Biblical World*, 1972, ET 1978) catalogs in detail. **Simo Parpola's** controversial 1993 *Journal of Near Eastern Studies* article *The Assyrian Tree of Life* argued for a sophisticated theological substrate behind the Assyrian Sacred Tree; the argument has been disputed (cf. **Mariana Giovino's** monograph *The Assyrian Sacred Tree*, 2007, which is more cautious) but the general iconographic pattern is well-established.

The chapter's *garden of God* (גַּן-אֱלֹהִים, *gan ʾelōhîm*) draws on the Eden tradition of Gen 2–3 and on the *cosmic-mountain* tradition that **Mark Smith** (*The Origins of Biblical Monotheism*, 2001) and **Jon Levenson** (*Sinai and Zion*, 1985) have documented. The Mesopotamian *abzu* (cosmic primeval water-source), the Egyptian *Nun* (the primordial waters), and the Ugaritic *mbk nhrm* (source of the rivers) all share the imaginative geography of a divinely-located cosmic well-source under a cosmic mountain on which a cosmic tree grows. **Michael Heiser's** more popular *The Unseen Realm* (2015) catalogs the OT's deployments of the *cosmic-mountain* tradition, though its more speculative claims should be read against the more cautious mainstream represented by Smith, Levenson, and **Richard Clifford** (*The Cosmic Mountain in Canaan and the Old Testament*, 1972).

Hebrew vocabulary worth flagging: *ʾerez* (אֶרֶז, "cedar," specifically the Lebanon cedar *Cedrus libani*); *gan ʾelōhîm* (גַּן-אֱלֹהִים, "garden of God," cf. 28:13 and Gen 2:8); *təhôm* (תְּהוֹם, "the deep," v. 4 and v. 15, the cosmic primeval water that nourished the cedar); *šəʾôl* (שְׁאוֹל, "Sheol," the underworld of the dead); *bôr* (בּוֹר, "pit," underworld synonym for Sheol).

## Lens 2 — Literature

Ezekiel 31 contains the **most extended *world-tree* allegory in the Old Testament**. The chapter divides into three movements: the cedar's growth and surpassing greatness (vv. 2–9), the cedar's fall and reason for falling (vv. 10–14), and the cedar's descent to Sheol with the cosmic mourning that accompanies it (vv. 15–18). The literary structure is allegorical and explicitly so: Pharaoh is *like* (v. 2 *whom are you like in your greatness?*) the cedar, and the comparison runs the full length of the chapter before being unsealed in the final verse: *this is Pharaoh and all his multitude* (v. 18).

The cedar comparison is layered. The cedar is **Assyria** historically (v. 3); it is also the **cosmic world-tree** mythologically (v. 8 *the cedars in the garden of God could not rival it*); and it is **Pharaoh-Egypt** typologically (v. 2 and v. 18). **Greenberg** notes that the layering is not confused but deliberate: the chapter teaches that all imperial pretension is *the same tree* under different historical guises, and the prophet is conscripting the most extended cosmological imagery available to make this single point.

The **Eden-allegory** of vv. 8–9 is striking. Verses 8–9 say the trees in *the garden of God* envied this cedar — a remarkable mythological claim that places the historical cedar (Assyria, by extension Pharaoh) inside the primordial garden imagery of Gen 2–3. **Block** treats this as Ezekiel's deliberate parallel to ch. 28:13–14, where the king of Tyre is placed *in Eden, the garden of God*. The Eden tradition is being deployed not as a historical-temporal Garden but as a cosmic-mythological category: the would-be Eden-tree is the imperial pretension that mistakes itself for the cosmic source.

The **fall-by-pride** trope of vv. 10–14 echoes the wisdom-tradition warning *pride goes before destruction* (Prov 16:18). The cedar's height is at first explicitly attributed to YHWH (v. 4 *the deep made it grow tall*; v. 9 *I made it beautiful*) and then re-attributed to the cedar's own self-elevation (v. 10 *because it towered high and set its top among the clouds, and its heart was proud of its height*). The literary device is the *narrative of self-deception*: the empire's strength was always granted, but the empire forgot the granting, and the forgetting itself becomes the warrant for the felling.

The **cosmic mourning** of vv. 15–18 is unusual. *I caused mourning* (v. 15) — the deep itself mourned, Lebanon was clothed in gloom, *all the trees of the field fainted because of it*. The cosmic-mourning trope appears elsewhere in the OAN (cf. Isa 14:8's lament of the cedars at the king of Babylon's fall; the *daughters of the nations chant* lament of Ezek 32:16). The literary effect is to scale the imperial fall to cosmological proportions: when Assyria-Egypt falls, the cosmos itself registers the event.

A textual variant: 31:3's MT reads *ʾaššûr ʾerez ba-ləḇānôn* (*Assyria, a cedar in Lebanon*). LXX reads *kyparissos* (cypress) and reorganizes the line. **Cooke** and **Eichrodt** prefer LXX-based emendation to *təʾaššûr* (a tree species); **Greenberg** and **Block** defend MT. The interpretive stakes are real: if the verse names Assyria explicitly, the chapter is a typological warning grounded in recent history; if it names only a tree species, the chapter is more mythologically free-floating. Most modern critical commentaries side with MT.

## Lens 3 — Larger Story

In Jewish reception, Ezekiel 31 became a primary text for the rabbinic doctrine of imperial pride and divine humbling. **Rashi** identifies the cedar straightforwardly as Assyria as type for Egypt; **Radak** develops the *garden of God* as a metaphor for the world's nations under divine providence. **Maimonides** (*Guide* III.46) treats the chapter as part of the prophetic critique of created beings claiming the prerogatives of the Creator. The Talmudic tractate **Sanhedrin 92b** discusses the cosmic-mourning trope in connection with the principle that *the righteous and the wicked together suffer in the fall of nations*. **Targum Jonathan** paraphrases the *garden of God* as *gintā de-ʿaden* (Garden of Eden), making the cosmic-Eden link explicit.

Christian reception has read Ezekiel 31 in close conversation with **Daniel 4** (Nebuchadnezzar's tree-dream), with which it shares the world-tree imagery and the fall-by-pride moral. **Origen's** lost commentary on Ezekiel survives in fragments preserved by Jerome; the typological reading of the cedar as the empire that exalts itself against God runs through **Jerome's** *Commentariorum in Ezechielem libri XIV* and into **Gregory the Great's** *Moralia*. **Augustine's** *De Civitate Dei* — though structured around Rome — develops the patristic critique of imperial self-divinization in language that draws explicitly on Ezekiel 31 and Daniel 4: every empire that mistakes itself for the *civitas Dei* is felled. **Calvin's** *Praelectiones in Ezechielem* treats the chapter as a sustained meditation on the *common-providence* doctrine: the empires rise and fall by divine grant, and the empires' forgetting of the grant is the moral occasion of the felling. **Pope Francis's** *Laudato Si'* (2015), in its extended discussion of human pride and ecological humility, draws indirectly on the Ezekiel-Daniel world-tree tradition.

The **chaoskampf-cosmic-mountain** scholarly tradition has been the focus of major treatments. **John Day's** *God's Conflict with the Dragon and the Sea* (1985), **Richard Clifford's** *The Cosmic Mountain in Canaan and the Old Testament* (1972), **Mark Smith's** *The Origins of Biblical Monotheism* (2001), and **Jon Levenson's** *Sinai and Zion* (1985) and *Creation and the Persistence of Evil* (1988) are the standard treatments. The argument is that the OT preserves and theologically transforms ANE cosmic-mountain imagery: the divine mountain is no longer a polytheistic locus but the singular *gan ʾelōhîm* of the one God, and the cosmic tree is no longer a polytheistic axis-mundi but a moral-theological emblem of imperial pretension.

The implications for biblical cosmology versus Christian metaphysics are significant. The OT's spatial-mythological imagination (cosmic mountain, primordial deep, world-tree, divine garden) sits uneasily with the more abstract metaphysical categories of post-Augustinian Christian theology (immateriality, infinity, atemporality). **Heiser's** *The Unseen Realm*, however speculative in places, raises a real exegetical question: how seriously should Christian theology take the OT's spatial-mythological imagery as ontologically descriptive rather than merely poetic? The mainstream Christian answer (Calvin, Aquinas, modern evangelical theology represented by **Bavinck's** *Reformed Dogmatics*) treats the imagery as accommodated and figurative; the more imaginative answer (Heiser, certain patristic and mystical traditions) treats the imagery as preserving real cosmological information that the metaphysical tradition has tended to flatten.

## Lens 4 — Theological Intent

**Tension 1 — Eden-as-divine-mountain and biblical cosmology.** The chapter places the cedar *in Eden, the garden of God* (vv. 8–9) and treats Eden as a locus where cosmic trees grow and watch the cedar's surpassing greatness with envy. The theological tension is between a flat-earth, cosmic-mountain, three-tiered-cosmology reading of OT spatial imagery (Heiser, the older work of **Luis Stadelmann** *The Hebrew Conception of the World*, 1970) and a fully-figurative reading that treats the imagery as poetic accommodation to ANE imagination (mainstream Christian interpretation). The most rigorous treatment is **Mark Smith's**: the OT preserves the cosmic-mountain tradition not because the biblical writers were primitive cosmologists but because the *theological deployment* of the tradition required real spatial categories — the divine garden is not nowhere, and the cosmic mountain is not metaphorical without remainder. Levenson's *Sinai and Zion* makes the parallel point: the biblical theology of Zion as cosmic mountain is theologically essential, not merely poetic. The unresolved question for Christian readers is how to preserve the cosmic-mountain theology while not committing to a literal flat-earth cosmology — a question that **Augustine's** *De Genesi ad litteram* already wrestled with, recognizing that Genesis's spatial-cosmological language must be read with care that preserves its theological substance without forcing literal claims about physical cosmology.

**Tension 2 — Imperial hubris and the *tall trees fall* trope.** The chapter teaches that the cedar's pride was not in its height but in its *forgetting that the height was given*. The unresolved theological question is whether this is a universal moral law (every tall thing eventually falls) or a contingent providential pattern (God may humble whom God wills, when God wills). The wisdom-tradition reading (Prov 16:18 *pride goes before destruction*) treats it as a universal moral law; the prophetic-providential reading (Calvin) treats it as contingent. **Augustine's** treatment in *De Civitate Dei* moves between the two: the *libido dominandi* is a universal moral disease, but the timing of any particular empire's fall is providentially determined. **Pope Francis's** *Laudato Si'* extends the trope to ecological hubris — the human empire's mistake of treating creation as its own *gan ʾelōhîm* to plunder and own. The Ezekiel-Daniel world-tree tradition is one of the most fertile OT resources for contemporary ecological theology, with **Norman Habel's** *The Earth Bible* series developing the theme.

**Unresolved question.** *Whom are you thus like in glory and in greatness among the trees of Eden?* (v. 18). The chapter declines to specify whether the trees-of-Eden category includes only fallen empires (Assyria, Egypt-to-be) or whether it includes any imperial-spiritual pretension to cosmic-tree status — including, by typological extension, ecclesial structures that imitate imperial form. The patristic tradition resisted any application of the cedar-trope to the church; the Reformation tradition (Calvin, Luther) freely applied it to the Roman papacy; modern liberation theology applies it to all imperial structures regardless of religious or secular form.

## The Thread

Four non-obvious cross-references. (1) **Daniel 4** — Nebuchadnezzar's tree-dream, where the cosmic-tree trope is deployed against Babylon (the very empire being conscripted in Ezek 30 to fell Egypt). The Daniel-Ezekiel pairing teaches that the world-tree warning is universal: the felling-tool itself eventually becomes the felled tree. (2) **Genesis 2–3** — the original Eden tradition that Ezek 31 (and 28) deploy. The chapter implicitly reads Eden not just as humanity's primordial home but as the cosmological location of all imperial-spiritual pretension; Pharaoh, the king of Tyre, and Adam himself are versions of the tree that mistook itself for the source. (3) **Isaiah 14** — the *Day Star, son of Dawn* taunt against the king of Babylon, with its parallel descent to Sheol and its parallel cosmic mourning of the cedars (Isa 14:8 *the cypresses rejoice at you, the cedars of Lebanon, saying, since you were laid low, no woodcutter comes up against us*). The parallel is so close that **Greenberg** treats Isa 14 and Ezek 31 as drawing on a shared OAN-cedar tradition. (4) **Revelation 22:1–2** — the eschatological tree of life, *yielding its fruit each month, and the leaves of the tree were for the healing of the nations*. The world-tree trope, deployed negatively in Ezek 31 against imperial pretension, returns positively at the canon's end as the redemptive cosmic tree that heals what the imperial trees broke. The forward arc from Ezek 31 to Rev 22 is one of the canon's most theologically rich.
