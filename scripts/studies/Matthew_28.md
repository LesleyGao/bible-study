# Matthew 28: Resurrection, Authority, and Emmanuel at the End

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

The chapter opens on the first day of the week — Aramaic-influenced Greek **εἰς μίαν σαββάτων** (*eis mian sabbatōn*, "toward the first of the week," 28:1) — at dawn, after the Sabbath has ended at sundown the night before. The two Marys travel to a tomb cut into a limestone outcrop in a quarry-garden outside Jerusalem's wall. The most likely location, on archaeological grounds, sits beneath the present Church of the Holy Sepulchre, whose Edicule encloses a much-modified rolling-stone tomb of first-century type. Magen Broshi, Hillel Geva, Shimon Gibson, Joan Taylor, and Jodi Magness have converged on this identification: the site lay outside the Second Wall in 30 CE, the rock outcrop is a quarry residual, and a *kokh*-tomb of the period was found in the complex. The 2016-2017 conservation project led by Antonia Moropoulou exposed the original limestone burial bench inside the Edicule for the first time in centuries, with mortar test results from the marble cladding dating to the Constantinian period (ca. 326-335 CE) — confirming the church's claim to continuous pilgrim memory back at least to the early fourth century.

A first-century Jerusalem rolling-stone tomb (a Greek *kokh* or Hebrew *kokhim* tomb, with a *golel* sealing stone) typically had a low entrance one had to stoop through (cf. John 20:5, 11), an antechamber, and a burial bench or shaft for the body. The stone was often disc-shaped, set in a track; archaeologists have catalogued only four genuine first-century rolling-stone tombs in the Jerusalem area (Amos Kloner, Boaz Zissu, *The Necropolis of Jerusalem in the Second Temple Period*, 2007), all from the wealthy class — consistent with Joseph of Arimathea's status (27:57). Most first-century tombs had stoppered square stones, not rolling discs.

The Galilee setting (28:7, 10, 16) for the climactic resurrection appearance is geographically distinctive. Luke 24 keeps the appearances in and around Jerusalem; Matthew (with Mark's implied trajectory in 16:7) sends the disciples back to Galilee, a journey of some 90 miles north. The "mountain" of 28:16 is unspecified — Davies and Allison treat it as a Matthean theological setting (the Mount of Beatitudes, the Mount of Transfiguration, the Mount of the Great Commission form a Matthean mountain-typology echoing Sinai and Zion) rather than a geographically identifiable peak. The traditional location at the Mount of Beatitudes (Mount of the Sermon) above Tabgha is unverified.

Three Greek terms anchor the chapter. **ἐξουσία** (*exousia*, "authority," 28:18) carries the freight of Daniel 7:13-14 LXX, where the Son of Man receives **ἐξουσίαν** ("authority") and **βασιλείαν** ("kingdom") that "shall not pass away." Matthew is making the claim explicit: the Son of Man enthroned in 26:64 is the Son of Man with universal *exousia* in 28:18. **πάντα τὰ ἔθνη** (*panta ta ethnē*, "all the nations/Gentiles," 28:19) takes its weight from Genesis 12:3 (the Abrahamic blessing to all nations) and Isaiah 49:6 (the Servant as a light to the nations). And the baptismal formula **εἰς τὸ ὄνομα τοῦ πατρὸς καὶ τοῦ υἱοῦ καὶ τοῦ ἁγίου πνεύματος** (*eis to onoma tou patros kai tou huiou kai tou hagiou pneumatos*, "into the name of the Father and of the Son and of the Holy Spirit," 28:19) uses the singular **ὄνομα** (*onoma*, "name") not the plural — the Trinitarian formula emerges in singular form, the linguistic seed of the *homoousion* dogma the church will articulate at Nicaea three centuries later.

## Lens 2 — The Literature

Matthew 28 is the shortest passion-resurrection narrative among the Synoptics and the most theologically compressed. It contains five scenes: the women at the tomb (28:1-8), Jesus's appearance to the women (28:9-10), the guards' bribe (28:11-15), the Galilean mountain commission (28:16-20). Three of these five scenes are uniquely Matthean.

The **earthquake and angel descent** (28:2-4) is uniquely Matthean. Mark 16:5 has a young man already inside the tomb when the women arrive; Matthew narrativizes the angelic action as cosmic-eschatological event. The earthquake bookends 27:51-54 (the earthquake at the cross), forming a Matthean seismic frame around the death-resurrection event. The angel "rolled back the stone and sat on it" — a gesture of triumphant authority. The guards posted in 27:62-66 become "like dead men" (28:4) — the only resurrection in this scene, ironically, is a swap: the guards drop dead-like, the dead one rises.

The **Christophany to the women** (28:9-10) is uniquely Matthean. Mark 16:8 ends (in the earliest manuscripts — Sinaiticus and Vaticanus end at v. 8) with the women fleeing in fear and silence. Matthew adds Jesus's direct encounter, a brief greeting (**χαίρετε**, *chairete*, "rejoice/greetings"), worship at his feet (the only worship-touching scene in the resurrection accounts in this form), and the commission to "tell my brothers to go to Galilee." The women are the first witnesses — a fact ancient and uncomfortable enough that 1 Corinthians 15:3-7's earlier creedal list omits them entirely. Davies and Allison, Luz, and Levine treat the historicity-criterion this way: the embarrassment of women as primary witnesses (women's testimony was disqualified in many first-century Jewish-legal contexts; see *m. Yebamoth* 16:7 for the exception, and Josephus *Antiquities* 4.219 for the rule) is itself an argument for the tradition's authenticity. Bauckham's *Jesus and the Eyewitnesses* extends this argument.

The **guards' bribe** (28:11-15) is uniquely Matthean. The setup in 27:62-66 is paid off here: the soldiers report what happened, the priests bribe them to spread the "stolen body" story, and the editorial coda — "this story has been spread among the Jews to this day" (28:15) — discloses Matthew's polemical context. The community of Matthew is contending, in the late first century, with a Jewish counter-narrative that Jesus's body was stolen. The medieval Jewish *Toledot Yeshu* tradition preserves a developed form of this counter-narrative (Peter Schäfer, *Jesus in the Talmud*, 2007). Matthew is responding to it in nascent form already in the 80s.

The **Galilean mountain commission** (28:16-20) is the climax. Matthew's distinctive details: "the eleven disciples" (Judas's vacancy not yet filled — that comes in Acts 1); the mountain "to which Jesus had directed them"; "when they saw him they worshiped him, **but some doubted**" (**οἱ δὲ ἐδίστασαν**, *hoi de edistasan*, 28:17). The doubt is striking and uniquely Matthean. Davies and Allison and Allison's later *Resurrecting Jesus* (2005) treat this as historiographically significant: a Matthean community polishing its resurrection apologetic does not invent residual disciple doubt; the detail bears the marks of preserved tradition. Allison's argument — that the diversity and disagreements within the resurrection accounts are themselves evidence of independent tradition rather than fabrication — is foundational.

Then come the four sentences that have shaped Christian missions, sacramental theology, and Trinitarian doctrine more than any other paragraph in the New Testament: "All authority in heaven and on earth has been given to me. Go therefore and make disciples of all nations, baptizing them into the name of the Father and of the Son and of the Holy Spirit, teaching them to observe all that I have commanded you. And behold, I am with you always, to the end of the age" (28:18-20).

The closing line — **ἐγὼ μεθ' ὑμῶν εἰμι πάσας τὰς ἡμέρας** (*egō meth' hymōn eimi pasas tas hēmeras*, "I with you am all the days") — forms a Matthean *inclusio* with 1:23, where Matthew quotes Isaiah 7:14 (LXX): "his name shall be called **Ἐμμανουήλ**, which means God with us." The Gospel that opens with God-with-us closes with Christ-with-us. France, Davies/Allison, and Luz all treat this inclusio as the structural-theological signature of the Gospel's design.

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

Read along the canonical arc, Matthew 28 is the resurrection event around which both Old Testament hope and New Testament mission rotate. The Son of Man's reception of universal authority (28:18) is Daniel 7:13-14 enacted; the commission to all nations (28:19) is Genesis 12:3 (the Abrahamic blessing) and Isaiah 49:6 (the Servant's mission to the Gentiles) enacted; and the abiding presence (28:20) is Exodus 33:14 ("my presence will go with you") and Haggai 1:13 ("I am with you, says the LORD") enacted in the risen Christ.

**Jewish interpretation** approaches this chapter with particular interest in 28:11-15 and the early Jewish-Christian polemic. Amy-Jill Levine and Marc Brettler (*The Jewish Annotated New Testament*) note that the "stolen body" charge survives in *Toledot Yeshu* and in Celsus's *True Discourse* (preserved in Origen's *Contra Celsum*). Geza Vermes (*The Resurrection*, 2008) treated the resurrection traditions as a complex layering of vision-experience reports that resist both simple naturalistic explanation and confessional acceptance, with Matthew's apologetic additions (guards, bribe) showing the late-first-century polemical context rather than the originating event. Daniel Boyarin (*The Jewish Gospels*) reads the Son of Man Christology Matthew deploys here within Jewish messianic possibilities of the period; the Trinitarian formula of 28:19, by contrast, exceeds those possibilities and represents a Christian-particular development.

**Christian interpretation** of 28:18-20 has a triple weight: Trinitarian doctrine, sacramental theology of baptism, and missionary theology. *Trinitarianly:* the formula was the seedbed of the *homoousion*. The Cappadocians — Basil of Caesarea (*On the Holy Spirit*, where the baptismal formula is the central textual warrant for the Spirit's full divinity), Gregory of Nazianzus (*Theological Orations*), and Gregory of Nyssa (*Against Eunomius*) — built their pro-Nicene Trinitarian theology on this verse. Athanasius's *Letters to Serapion* invokes 28:19 explicitly against pneumatomachian denial of the Spirit's deity. The Council of Nicaea (325 CE) and Constantinople (381 CE) canonized the *homoousion* of all three persons; Augustine's *De Trinitate* gave the West its enduring formulation; the Cappadocian-Augustinian synthesis culminated in the medieval Western *Filioque* and in the Eastern triadic monarchy of the Father. The textual question — whether 28:19 is an authentic Jesus-saying or a Matthean-redactional formula reflecting the baptismal practice of the late-first-century church — is contested. Eusebius of Caesarea, in citations of Matthew before Nicaea, sometimes preserves a shorter form ("go, make disciples of all nations in my name"); Conybeare argued in 1901 that this was the original. The current critical consensus (Metzger, *A Textual Commentary on the Greek New Testament*; Bart Ehrman, *Misquoting Jesus*; the UBS/NA28 apparatus) overwhelmingly affirms the Trinitarian form as original — every Greek manuscript that contains 28:19 has it; Eusebius's shorter quotations are best read as paraphrastic abbreviation rather than textual variant. The redactional question (does Matthew preserve the *ipsissima verba* of Jesus or formulate a saying reflecting his community's baptismal practice?) remains open even among confessional scholars.

*Sacramentally:* the formula has become the universal trinitarian baptismal invocation across Catholic, Orthodox, and most Protestant churches. The *Didache* (ca. 90-110 CE) instructs baptism "into the name of the Father and of the Son and of the Holy Spirit" using running water if possible, three pourings if not — the formula was operative within a generation of Matthew's composition. Calvin's *Institutes* 4.15-16 and Luther's *Large Catechism* both anchor baptismal theology in 28:19. The "Jesus-only" baptismal practice of certain modern Pentecostal traditions (Oneness Pentecostalism), citing Acts 2:38 ("baptized in the name of Jesus Christ"), is a minority dissent.

*Missiologically:* William Carey's *An Enquiry Into the Obligations of Christians to Use Means for the Conversion of the Heathens* (1792) made 28:18-20 the Magna Carta of the modern Protestant missionary movement. David Bosch's *Transforming Mission* (1991) provides the foundational late-twentieth-century mission-theological synthesis, reading Matthean mission as inseparable from discipleship, baptism, and ethical formation ("teaching them to observe all that I have commanded you"). Lesslie Newbigin's *The Gospel in a Pluralist Society* (1989) and *The Open Secret* (1978) developed a post-colonial missiology of the church-as-sent in dialogue with this verse. Andrew Walls's *The Missionary Movement in Christian History* (1996) traced the verse's transformation across cultural contexts — the Gospel becoming, in Walls's framework, simultaneously a translation principle and a pilgrim principle as it crosses each new cultural boundary.

The major commentaries on the resurrection narrative are Davies and Allison's *ICC* volume 3 (the redactional and tradition-historical apparatus is foundational) and Ulrich Luz's *Hermeneia* volume 4 (with reception-historical depth). N. T. Wright's *The Resurrection of the Son of God* (2003) is the single most ambitious historical-theological defense of bodily resurrection in modern scholarship — over 800 pages arguing that no first-century Jewish or Greco-Roman conceptual world produced "resurrection" as the early Christians used it; the term required a new event to occasion its new use. Dale Allison's *Resurrecting Jesus: The Earthly Christ and the Risen Christ* (2005) takes a more cautious position — he affirms that something happened that the disciples interpreted as resurrection, while remaining agnostic about whether the event was bodily-physical in the Wright sense. Markus Bockmuehl's *Simon Peter in Scripture and Memory* (2010) examines Petrine memory transmission. Gerd Lüdemann's *The Resurrection of Jesus* (1994) supplies the standard skeptical reading: the resurrection appearances were collective grief-induced visionary experiences, religiously valid but historically non-objective. Raymond Brown's *Death of the Messiah* limits itself to passion; *The Virginal Conception and Bodily Resurrection of Jesus* (1973) extends his analysis to the resurrection accounts.

For the Trinitarian theological tradition that builds from 28:19, Karl Barth's *Church Dogmatics* I/1 and the doctrine of the Trinity it develops, Catherine Mowry LaCugna's *God for Us: The Trinity and Christian Life* (1991), Robert Jenson's *Systematic Theology* volume 1 (1997), and Kevin Vanhoozer's *Remythologizing Theology* (2010) form the contemporary roster.

## Lens 4 — The Theological Intent

**Tension 1: The bodily resurrection — what kind of event is 28:6, "He is not here, for he has risen"?** Three positions structure modern theological debate. *Bodily-physical realist* (N. T. Wright, *Resurrection of the Son of God*; Richard Swinburne, *The Resurrection of God Incarnate*; the historic Catholic-Orthodox-Protestant confessional consensus): the tomb was empty, the body of Jesus was raised transformed, the appearances were of a tangible-yet-glorified body (the touching in 28:9; the eating in Luke 24:42-43; the wounds in John 20:27). The empty tomb is a non-negotiable fact; the appearances are not visions but encounters with the risen Christ in transformed embodiment. *Subjective-visionary* (Lüdemann, the historical-critical heirs of Bultmann, partially Crossan): the disciples experienced grief-induced visionary phenomena (well-documented in bereavement psychology) that they interpreted within their apocalyptic-eschatological framework as resurrection. The "empty tomb" tradition is a later legendary development. *Objectively-real but mystery-veiled* (Barth, *Church Dogmatics* IV/1 §59, IV/2 §64; Karl Rahner; Dale Allison's nuanced position in *Resurrecting Jesus*): something objectively happened to Jesus that exceeds the categories of physics and psychology alike; the event is "objective" in that it was not invented and "real" in that it grounds the faith of the church, but its precise mode of historical reality remains epistemically opaque. The Gospels themselves give it as encounter rather than as scientifically describable event. **Where this leaves us:** Wright's defense of the bodily-physical reading remains the dominant confessional position; Lüdemann's subjective reading remains the dominant skeptical alternative; Barth-Rahner-Allison occupies the theologically thoughtful middle. Matthew himself is in the bodily camp — the women take hold of his feet (28:9), the empty tomb is presupposed, the guard's bribe is meaningful only if the body's absence is real. **Unresolved:** what is the ontological structure of "transformed bodily existence"? Paul wrestles with this in 1 Corinthians 15:35-50 ("flesh and blood cannot inherit the kingdom... we shall all be changed"), and the question continues to divide theological imagination.

**Tension 2: The Great Commission and the question of religious pluralism — what does "make disciples of all nations" mean in a pluralist age?** Three positions structure contemporary theology. *Exclusivist* (the dominant historic position; J. I. Packer, R. C. Sproul, much of the evangelical tradition): salvation is in Christ alone, accessible only through explicit faith in him in this life, and the Great Commission is the church's marching order to bring all peoples to that explicit faith. The church's mission is the eternally consequential offer of salvation. *Inclusivist* (Karl Rahner's "anonymous Christians"; the Catholic *Lumen Gentium* 16; some evangelicals like Clark Pinnock, John Sanders): salvation is in Christ alone, but the saving work of Christ may reach those who, through no fault of their own, never hear the gospel — through their response to the light they have. The Great Commission remains, but its motive is the fullness of Christ rather than the rescue from inevitable damnation. *Pluralist* (John Hick, *An Interpretation of Religion*; Paul Knitter, *No Other Name?*): the religions are different cultural-historical responses to the one ultimate Real; Christianity is a particular trajectory among others, and the Great Commission must be reframed as witness-without-conquest. Lesslie Newbigin's mediating position in *Gospel in a Pluralist Society* steelmans against pluralism while resisting the cultural-imperialist tones of older exclusivism: the church is sent to bear witness to the *truth*, but in a posture of service and humility, not domination. **Where this leaves us:** Matthew 28:18-20 itself does not adjudicate among these positions. It asserts universal authority, universal mission, sacramental induction, and ethical formation. What it requires is that the church *go* — what it leaves theologically open is the precise relationship between the sent witness and the ultimate destiny of those who never receive it. **Unresolved:** the relationship between the missionary movement Carey began on 28:18-20 and the colonial complicities that movement sometimes carried (Andrew Walls, Lamin Sanneh, *Translating the Message*) — how does the church inhabit Matthew's commission honestly in a post-colonial world?

## The Thread

Matthew 28 closes the loop on the Gospel's structural inclusio: 1:23 (**Ἐμμανουήλ**, "God with us," the prophetic name given the unborn child) is fulfilled in 28:20 (**ἐγὼ μεθ' ὑμῶν εἰμι**, "I am with you," the closing word of the risen Lord). The Son of Man enthroned in 26:64 receives his universal *exousia* in 28:18, fulfilling Daniel 7:13-14. The blood "for the forgiveness of sins" of 26:28 is now applied to the nations in the baptismal triadic formula of 28:19. The earthquake of 27:51-54 is mirrored by the earthquake of 28:2 — the seismic bracket around the death-resurrection event Matthew alone constructs. Watch the early church's response: the Trinitarian formula of 28:19 surfaces in the *Didache* (ca. 90-110 CE) as established baptismal practice; the Son of Man's universal *exousia* of 28:18 surfaces in Philippians 2:9-11 and Revelation 11:15. And read into the Acts narrative: the eleven of 28:16 become the twelve of Acts 1, and the commission of 28:19 unfolds across Pentecost, Antioch, and Rome — Luke's whole second volume is, in effect, the church's enacted obedience to Matthew's last word.
