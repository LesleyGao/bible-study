# Psalm 35 — Go Deeper

## Lens 1: The Historical World

The superscription is bare: "Of David" (*le-David*, לְדָוִד). No narrative anchor like Ps 34's Abimelech-Achish escape, no genre marker like Ps 32's *maskil*. The poem is left to stand on its own as an unattached Davidic prayer, which means the redactor is signaling that this prayer travels — it is meant for any sufferer hounded by malicious enemies.

Form-critically, Hermann Gunkel classified Psalm 35 as a *Klagelied des Einzelnen* — an **individual lament** — and specifically as one of the great **imprecatory laments** of the Psalter. The imprecatory lament names a particular subgroup within the lament category: a lament that does not merely complain to God about enemies but **actively prays for their downfall**. The classical group of imprecatory psalms in Christian and Jewish reception is Pss 35, 58, 59, 69, 79, 83, 109, 137, and parts of others; among these, **Psalms 35, 69, 109, and 137 are typically named the most concentrated imprecations**. Psalm 35 is among the longest and most sustained.

The form-critical analysis identifies a triple movement, with the lament arc occurring **three times**:

- **First cycle (vv. 1–10):** invocation, petition, imprecation, vow of praise.
- **Second cycle (vv. 11–18):** complaint about false witnesses, recollection of speaker's prior kindness to enemies, plea for rescue, vow of praise.
- **Third cycle (vv. 19–28):** plea against enemies' triumph, imprecation, vow of praise.

Hossfeld and Zenger have argued this triple structure is deliberate — the poem performs the resilience of prayer under prolonged assault. The speaker does not break through to release; he prays the same arc three times, refusing to give up.

The opening Hebrew is martial and direct: *rivah Yahweh et-yerivai, lecham et-lochamai* (רִיבָה יְהוָה אֶת־יְרִיבַי לְחַם אֶת־לֹחֲמָי, "Contend, O LORD, with those who contend with me; fight against those who fight against me," v. 1). The verb *riv* (רִיב) is forensic — to bring a lawsuit, to press a legal case — while *lacham* (לָחַם) is military, to wage war. The two verbs together name the speaker's plight: he is **simultaneously sued and attacked**, facing a coordinated legal-and-physical assault. The petition is that God act as both his **defense attorney** (countering the *riv*) and his **commander** (countering the *lacham*).

The imagery cluster of vv. 2–3 is stark warrior-divine imagery: *magen* (מָגֵן, "shield"), *tzinnah* (צִנָּה, "buckler/large shield"), *chanit* (חֲנִית, "spear"), *segor* (סְגֹר, often translated "javelin" or "battle-axe"). This is YHWH as warrior — armored, weaponed, mobilized. The image is Israelite holy-war language (cf. Exod 15:3, "the LORD is a man of war") deployed in defense of an individual sufferer.

The hunting imagery in vv. 7–8 (the **net** and the **pit**) is the standard Psalter vocabulary for malicious entrapment (cf. Pss 7:15, 9:15, 31:4, 57:6). The *boomerang justice* of v. 8 — let the net the enemy hid catch the enemy himself — is the principle of *measure-for-measure* (*middah ke-neged middah*) that runs through biblical justice texts: the punishment fits the crime in form as well as severity.

The phrase "without cause" (*chinam*, חִנָּם) appears repeatedly (vv. 7, 19) — the speaker insists his enemies hate him *gratuitously*, not in response to provocation. This will be the verse John's Gospel picks up at Jn 15:25 ("they hated me without a cause," *emisēsan me dōrean*), applying the language to the world's hatred of Jesus.

The DSS preserves Psalm 35 in 4QPs-a and 11QPs-a fragments without significant variation from the MT. The Septuagint (Ps 34 in Greek numbering) translates the imprecations directly, without softening — a sign that the early translators were comfortable preserving the imprecatory force.

ANE parallels for the imprecatory lament are abundant. Mesopotamian *šuilla* prayers, Egyptian execration texts (curses written on figurines and broken), and Hittite ritual texts all include curses against enemies. What is distinctive in Psalm 35 is that **the speaker does not act on the curse**. He hands the rage to God and waits. The execration text smashes the figurine; the psalm prays the prayer and walks away.

## Lens 2: The Literature

Lowth's parallelism dominates. Synonymous in v. 1 ("Contend with those who contend with me // fight against those who fight against me"), antithetic in v. 12 ("they repay me evil for good // my soul is bereft"), synthetic in v. 5 ("let them be like chaff before the wind // with the angel of the LORD driving them away").

The strophic structure tracks the triple-cycle analysis:

- **Strophe 1 (vv. 1–3):** Petition — God as warrior.
- **Strophe 2 (vv. 4–8):** First imprecation — shame, chaff, dark paths, the boomerang net.
- **Strophe 3 (vv. 9–10):** First vow of praise — "all my bones shall say."
- **Strophe 4 (vv. 11–14):** Complaint about false witnesses; the speaker's recollected kindness.
- **Strophe 5 (vv. 15–17):** Renewed lament — "How long, O Lord, will you look on?"
- **Strophe 6 (v. 18):** Second vow of praise — "in the great congregation."
- **Strophe 7 (vv. 19–25):** Renewed imprecation — "let them not say, 'We have swallowed him up.'"
- **Strophe 8 (v. 26):** Third imprecation — "let them be clothed with shame."
- **Strophe 9 (vv. 27–28):** Third vow of praise — "my tongue shall tell of your righteousness all the day long."

The repetition of the **vow-of-praise** at three points (vv. 9–10, 18, 27–28) is the structural backbone. Walter Brueggemann has argued that the vows are not afterthoughts but the **load-bearing theological move**: the speaker resolves to praise even as he prays imprecation, holding the two together as the integrity of honest prayer.

A literary feature worth noting: the **animal imagery**. The enemies are *gnashing with teeth* like wild dogs (v. 16), and the speaker's "precious life" is among **lions** (v. 17). The "lion" is the Psalter's standard image for a powerful adversary (Pss 7:2, 10:9, 17:12, 22:13, 21). The speaker is not personifying the enemy as predator metaphorically; he is naming the predatory dynamic — they are hunting him as a lion hunts.

The phrase *Aha, Aha!* (*he'ach he'ach*, הֶאָח הֶאָח, vv. 21, 25) is an onomatopoetic exclamation of malicious satisfaction — the equivalent of "Got him!" or "Take that!" in modern English. The speaker hears this gloating in his enemies' mouths and prays God to silence it.

The closing line, *u-leshoni tehgeh tzidkecha kol-hayyom tehillatekha* (v. 28), uses the verb *hagah* (הָגָה) — to mutter, to meditate, to reflect aloud. The same verb opens the Psalter at Ps 1:2 ("on his law he meditates day and night"). The lament-imprecation closes with the same word that opens the entire collection: the righteous *mutter*, day and night, on God's righteousness — even when the muttering takes the form of imprecation.

There is no *Selah* in Psalm 35 and no acrostic — the form is straight sustained lament.

## Lens 3: The Larger Story

**In the canonical Psalter.** Psalm 35 sits in the late Davidic cluster of Book I. After the wisdom-thanksgiving acrostic of Ps 34, the Psalter plunges back into raw lament with Ps 35. The juxtaposition is theological: **the alphabet of praise (Ps 34) is followed immediately by the alphabet of complaint (Ps 35)**. The Psalter refuses to leave the reader in resolution. Hossfeld and Zenger note that Pss 35, 38, 39, and 40 form a sub-collection of laments that prepares the reader for the great lament-cluster of Pss 41–43 closing Book I.

**The Jewish trajectory.** *Midrash Tehillim* on Ps 35 reads it as Davidic and locates it in the Saul persecution narrative — the false witnesses are Doeg and the Ziphites, the gloating enemies are Saul's court. Rashi follows this line. Ibn Ezra reads more cautiously, noting that the psalm's enemies could be from any period of David's life. Radak treats the imprecations as petitions for **divine justice**, not personal vengeance — the speaker is asking God to act, not threatening to act himself. Nachmanides extends Radak: the imprecation is **honest grief made public to God** rather than concealed and acted upon privately.

In modern Jewish thought, Buber on prayer reads the lament as the I-Thou relationship under stress — the speaker insists on speaking the worst aloud rather than retreating into silence. Heschel on prophetic anger treats the imprecation as participation in God's own **pathos** — God is also angry at gratuitous hatred, and the speaker prays into that divine anger. Levenson on suffering and the persistence of evil reads Ps 35 as part of the Hebrew Bible's refusal to pretend the world is morally simple. Sarna emphasizes the legal vocabulary (*riv*) and the courtroom framing. **Erich Zornberg** on imprecation argues that the curse is the speaker's way of **handing the violence to God** so the speaker need not act on it — the imprecation is a transfer, not an assault.

In Jewish liturgy, Ps 35 is recited in some traditions at *Tachanun* (penitential supplication) and on fast days; the Sephardi *selichot* tradition includes portions of it.

**The Christian trajectory.** Augustine's *Enarrationes in Psalmos* on Ps 35 gives the most influential Western reading. Augustine **spiritualizes** the enemies — they are not human persons but **sin, the devil, the old self**. The imprecation against enemies becomes the believer's prayer against indwelling sin and the spiritual powers behind temptation. This reading allowed the Western church to pray Ps 35 in the Divine Office without endorsing personal vengeance. Augustine also reads vv. 11–14 (the speaker's prior kindness to enemies) as Christ's voice — Christ wept and prayed for those who would crucify him.

Athanasius lists Ps 35 among the psalms to pray when slandered. Cassiodorus reads it as the church's prayer against persecutors with explicit warning that the imprecation must not become personal vengeance. Aquinas in his Psalms commentary follows Augustine's spiritualization. Bonaventure mystically reads the warrior imagery as the soul's spiritual armor.

Luther's lectures on Ps 35 hold the imprecation more directly — he reads it as honest prayer against real opponents of the gospel, with the proviso that God, not the believer, executes the judgment. Calvin's commentary reads the imprecations as **legitimate prayers for divine justice**, distinguishing them sharply from personal vengeance: "the faithful do not pray that God may avenge their own wrongs, but that he may vindicate his own righteousness." This Reformed reading remains influential.

**C. S. Lewis** in *Reflections on the Psalms* (1958) gives the most famous modern Christian wrestling with the imprecations. Lewis is initially troubled — calls the imprecations "devilish" — but moves toward reading them as **honest indignation against systemic evil** that the modern Christian, who has lost capacity for righteous anger, can hardly fathom. He warns that the failure to feel imprecation may be a sign of moral exhaustion rather than moral progress.

**Walter Brueggemann** in *The Costly Loss of Lament* (1986) and *The Message of the Psalms* makes the most influential case for **recovering the imprecatory psalms**. Brueggemann argues that the modern church's selective pruning of the Psalter — removing the imprecations from public worship — is a theological loss: it teaches Christians that **honest rage is unwelcome before God**, with the result that the rage festers in private rather than being handed over in prayer. Brueggemann's reading has been adopted across mainline Protestant homiletics.

**Erich Zenger** in *A God of Vengeance? Understanding the Psalms of Divine Wrath* (German 1994; English 1996) is the most careful pastoral-theological treatment. Zenger argues that the imprecation is a prayer of **the powerless against violence**, and that handing rage to God is the speaker's way of refusing to *become* the violence. The psalm is anti-vengeance because it ensures that the speaker himself does not enact what he asks God to do. The imprecation, for Zenger, is the pacifist's prayer.

Among other modern critical interpreters: Gunkel (paradigmatic individual lament), Mowinckel (cultic, possibly royal), Westermann (lament with vows of praise), Hans-Joachim Kraus (Davidic individual lament with strong wisdom undercurrent), Hossfeld and Zenger (triple-cycle structure, anti-violence pacifist reading), John Goldingay (cautious about over-spiritualizing; honest imprecation), Marvin Tate, Gordon Wenham (canonical-ethical reading: the imprecation is licit only as transfer to God). Patrick D. Miller on lament. William P. Brown on the warrior metaphor. Beth Tanner reads the psalm pastorally, defending its place in worship.

**Selective pruning in liturgy.** The Anglican Book of Common Prayer (especially the 1979 American revision) and the Catholic Liturgy of the Hours (post-Vatican II) **abridged the imprecatory psalms**, removing or bracketing the harshest verses. Pss 58 and 109 were dropped from the 1971 Liturgy of the Hours entirely; portions of Pss 35, 69, 137 were bracketed or made optional. This decision has been controversial. Defenders argue that public liturgy should not place violent prayers on Christians' lips; critics (Brueggemann, Zenger, Tanner) argue the abridgement teaches dishonest prayer and prevents the laity from learning how to bring rage to God.

**NT use.** The phrase "they hated me without a cause" (*chinam*) is quoted in **John 15:25** — Jesus applies Ps 35:19 (also Ps 69:4) to the world's gratuitous hatred of him. Ps 35:11's malicious witnesses are echoed in Mt 26:59 (the Sanhedrin trial) and Mk 14:55–56 (where the false-witness pattern of Ps 35 is fulfilled at the trial of Jesus). The wider New Testament reception of imprecatory prayer continues in Rev 6:10 (the martyrs under the altar crying *"How long, O Lord, holy and true, before you will judge?"*) — an echo of Ps 35:17 ("How long, O Lord, will you look on?").

## Lens 4: Theological Intent — Imprecation, the Pacifist's Prayer, and the Church's Selective Pruning

**Tension 1: How can a Christian pray imprecation alongside Jesus's command to love enemies (Mt 5:44)?**

- **Position A (Augustinian spiritualization):** Augustine, the medieval Western tradition, much Catholic spiritual reading. The enemies in Ps 35 are not human persons but sin, the devil, the old self, the spiritual powers behind opposition. The imprecation is licit as prayer against indwelling sin. Steelman: the New Testament's transformation of enemy-language requires the church to read the Old Testament's enemy-language anew. *Spiritualization is not evasion; it is canonical reading.*
- **Position B (Brueggemann / Zenger pacifist transfer):** Brueggemann, Zenger, and modern recovery readings. The imprecation is the powerless person's prayer of rage **handed over to God** so that the speaker need not act on it. The speaker who prays Ps 35 is precisely the speaker who refuses to take revenge — the imprecation is the alternative to violence, not its rehearsal. Steelman: pretending we don't have rage at injustice is dishonest before God; refusing to hand the rage over breeds violence in private. The imprecatory psalm is a pacifist tool.
- **Position C (Calvin / classical Reformed):** Calvin, Reformed orthodoxy. The imprecation is a prayer for **God's righteous judgment**, not personal vengeance. The faithful pray that God will vindicate his own righteousness against the wicked, leaving the executing of judgment entirely in God's hand. Steelman: the imprecation is doxological — it confesses that God is just and that the wicked will not finally prevail. To pray the imprecation is to side with divine justice against evil.
- **Position D (Cautious or critical):** Some pacifist Christian traditions, much modern liberal Christianity. The imprecations are best abridged or quietly retired; they belong to a pre-Christian moral horizon. Steelman: Jesus tells us to love our enemies and pray for those who persecute us. The imprecations cannot be reconciled with this command and should be read as scripture-we-do-not-pray.

**Tension 2: The selective pruning of the imprecatory psalms in modern liturgy.**

- **Position A (Pruning is right):** Post-Vatican II Catholic liturgical reform, much of mainline Protestant practice. Public worship should not place violent prayers on Christians' lips; the imprecations are appropriate for private wrestling with scripture but not for corporate worship.
- **Position B (Pruning is wrong):** Brueggemann, Zenger, Tanner, much evangelical biblical theology. The pruning teaches the laity that honest rage is unwelcome before God, suppresses the prophetic voice, and produces a Psalter sanitized into uselessness for sufferers. Steelman: the church's reluctance to pray the imprecations correlates with the church's loss of capacity to feel imprecation against real injustice — and the laity then carry their rage privately, untransformed by prayer.
- **Position C (Catechized inclusion):** Wright, Wenham, much careful pastoral theology. The imprecations should be prayed but with catechesis — teaching the laity how the imprecation is a transfer to God, how it is read through Christ's love of enemies, and how it functions as protest against violence rather than rehearsal of it. Steelman: Scripture is inspired entire; the church's job is to teach the difficult parts, not to remove them.

**Where this leaves us:** Psalm 35 is not a comfortable prayer. It is a prayer of someone who is being lied about, hunted, gloated over, and surrounded by gnashing enemies, and who refuses both silence and violence. The genius of the psalm is that it teaches the sufferer to **speak the worst aloud to God** — to name the rage, name the enemies, name the hoped-for downfall — while waiting on God to act. The speaker does not lift a hand. He prays. And in praying, he hands the violence to God so that he need not become the violence himself. This is what Erich Zenger means by calling imprecation the pacifist's prayer: the curse is the alternative to action, not its prelude.

The selective pruning of these psalms in modern liturgy reflects a real pastoral concern (don't put violent prayers in the laity's mouth) but at a real cost (the laity then has nowhere to bring their rage and learns implicitly that rage is unwelcome before God). The recovery of the imprecatory psalms — read with Augustine's spiritualization, Calvin's divine-justice frame, and Zenger's pacifist transfer all available as hermeneutical lenses — is part of what it means to pray the whole Psalter rather than the curated Psalter.

**Closing question:** Is there a person, a system, or an evil that you are trying *not* to feel rage about because you have been told rage is unspiritual? What would it cost — and what would it free — to hand the rage to God in the form Psalm 35 teaches?

## The Thread

- Psalm 35:19 ("they hated me without a cause," *chinam*) is quoted by Jesus in John 15:25 (with Ps 69:4) — the world's gratuitous hatred of Jesus is read as fulfillment of the Davidic sufferer's cry.
- Psalm 35:11's false witnesses pattern is echoed in the trial of Jesus (Mt 26:59, Mk 14:55–56) and in the trial of Stephen (Acts 6:13) — the imprecatory psalm becomes Christological.
- The "How long, O Lord?" of v. 17 echoes through the prophetic literature (Hab 1:2) and culminates in Rev 6:10, where the martyrs under the altar cry the same question to the same God — connecting Davidic lament to the eschatological cry of the persecuted church.
- The Augustinian spiritualization, the Calvinian divine-justice reading, the Brueggemann / Zenger pacifist-transfer reading, and the Lewis honest-but-troubled reading have all become available to the contemporary church as ways to pray Ps 35 without becoming its enemies.
