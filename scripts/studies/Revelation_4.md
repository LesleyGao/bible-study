# Revelation 4: The Door That Opens Behind the Empire

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

The Apocalypse is, by mainstream consensus, the work of John of Patmos, written near the end of the reign of Domitian, around AD 95. The earliest external witness, Irenaeus (*Against Heresies* 5.30.3), places the vision "almost in our own day, towards the end of the reign of Domitian," and modern scholarship — David Aune's three-volume Word commentary, G.K. Beale's massive NIGTC volume, Craig Koester's Anchor Yale commentary, and Richard Bauckham's *Theology of the Book of Revelation* — has overwhelmingly endorsed this dating. A confident minority, especially John A.T. Robinson and the Reformed preterist Kenneth Gentry, argues for a Neronian dating in the 60s, reading Revelation as a book aimed at the Jewish War and the destruction of the Temple in AD 70. The Domitianic reading, however, frames the book the way Irenaeus did: as a response to imperial cult intensification in the province of Asia. Steven Friesen's *Imperial Cults and the Apocalypse of John* documents the proliferation of municipal cult sites in cities like Ephesus, Smyrna, and Pergamum during exactly this period, while Leonard Thompson, in *The Book of Revelation: Apocalypse and Empire*, urges caution against assuming systematic state persecution under Domitian — Thompson's view is that John writes to a community in *anxious accommodation*, not to one already martyred at scale. Adela Yarbro Collins, in *Crisis and Catharsis*, mediates: the perceived crisis is partly real and partly constructed by John himself in order to mobilize a complacent church.

Chapter 4 turns the camera. The seven letters of chapters 2-3 have addressed local communities; now "a door stood open in heaven" (θύρα ἠνεῳγμένη ἐν τῷ οὐρανῷ, 4:1) and John is summoned upward. The throne (θρόνος) is the chapter's gravitational center; the noun appears fourteen times in twenty-one verses, the highest concentration in the New Testament. Aune notes that this throne-density is rhetorically deliberate: a Roman provincial reader knew exactly one throne — the emperor's, in Rome — and John's chapter is engineered to relocate that imagination. The visual catalog of jasper, carnelian, and an emerald-rainbow (4:3) draws directly on Ezekiel's chariot vision, where "above the likeness of the throne was a likeness with a human appearance" surrounded by brightness "like the appearance of the bow that is in the cloud on the day of rain" (Ezek 1:26-28). John strips the anthropomorphic figure away: there is *one seated on the throne*, but no description of his form. The refusal of a face is itself a Jewish theological move.

The twenty-four elders (πρεσβύτεροι, 4:4) are among the chapter's most contested entities. Three identifications dominate. The first reads them as the redeemed people of God (twelve patriarchs plus twelve apostles), favored by R.H. Charles in his ICC commentary, by George Caird, and by Robert Mounce; Beale held this view in his earlier work before nuancing it. The second reads them as a heavenly council of angelic beings analogous to the Canaanite-Israelite divine council of Psalm 82 and 1 Kings 22. The third — increasingly the dominant scholarly position — reads them as the heavenly counterpart of the *twenty-four priestly courses* of 1 Chronicles 24, who served by rotation in the Jerusalem Temple. Aune marshals the strongest case for this reading; Bauckham concurs; Larry Hurtado, in *At the Origins of Christian Worship*, treats it as the most contextually persuasive option, since the elders' function in the chapter is exactly priestly — falling down, offering thanks, casting crowns. The detail that they wear "white garments" and "golden crowns" (στεφάνους χρυσοῦς) is consistent with priestly investiture as much as with royal honor.

The four living creatures (ζῷα, 4:6-8) fuse two prophetic streams. From Ezekiel 1 comes the four-faced quartet: lion, ox, man, eagle, though John redistributes one face per creature rather than four faces per creature. From Isaiah 6 come the six wings and the *trisagion*: "Holy, holy, holy, is the Lord God Almighty" (ἅγιος, ἅγιος, ἅγιος Κύριος ὁ θεὸς ὁ παντοκράτωρ, 4:8). Ralph Brucker, in his work on early Christian hymnody, and Larry Hurtado, in *One God, One Lord*, treat this verse as the most obvious New Testament evidence of an early Christian liturgical *Sanctus* — a hymn that, by the fifth century, would be embedded in the Roman canon of the Mass and would never leave it. The Jewish *Kedushah* of the synagogue service descends from the same Isaianic source; Reuven Kimelman has shown that the Christian and Jewish liturgies developed in parallel from a shared apocalyptic-temple matrix, not by borrowing in either direction.

The "sea of glass, like crystal" (θάλασσα ὑαλίνη, 4:6) carries doubled significance. It is the heavenly counterpart to the bronze laver that stood before Solomon's temple (1 Kings 7:23-26), where it was called "the sea." It is also the apocalyptic transformation of the Genesis 1 chaos-sea: in Revelation 21:1 there will be "no more sea," but here the chaotic deep has already been pacified into glass. The eschatological tension — chaos defeated yet still present — runs through the whole book.

Greek terms worth holding: *thronos* (θρόνος), the chapter's hinge; *zōa* (ζῷα, "living ones," not the lesser "beasts" some translations imply); *presbyteroi* (πρεσβύτεροι, "elders"); *axios* (ἄξιος, "worthy"), the liturgical acclamation that returns three times in the doxology of 4:11 and again in 5:9, 5:12; *pantokratōr* (παντοκράτωρ, "Almighty"), Septuagintal translation of YHWH Sabaoth.

## Lens 2 — The Literature

After the seven letters of chapters 2-3, John shifts genres without warning. The seven letters are oracular epistolary, formally similar to the prophetic *messenger formula* combined with first-century imperial decree. Chapter 4 jumps into high apocalyptic vision-report. The transitional formula — "After this I looked, and behold" (μετὰ ταῦτα εἶδον, 4:1) — is John's standard scene-marker, repeated at 7:1, 7:9, 15:5, 18:1, and 19:1. It does not necessarily mean chronological succession; it means narrative succession in the visionary sequence.

The chapter's literary template is the Jewish ascent-vision — what scholars now call the Hekhalot or Merkavah tradition. Christopher Rowland's *The Open Heaven* and James Davila's *Descenders to the Chariot* place Revelation 4 squarely within this literature alongside 1 Enoch 14, Daniel 7:9-10, Ezekiel 1, Isaiah 6, the *Apocalypse of Abraham* 17-18, the *Songs of the Sabbath Sacrifice* from Qumran, and the later Hekhalot Rabbati. The standard features are: an opened heaven, a throne, brilliant gemstone imagery, attendant angelic beings, a fiery river or sea, and a worship-acclamation. John reproduces every standard element. His distinct contribution is the radical *abstention* from describing the figure on the throne — a restraint shared with Ezekiel and 1 Enoch but practiced more strictly than in either.

The chapter's literary architecture is concentric. Outside is the open door (4:1) and the closing doxology (4:11). Inside that frame stand the elders (4:4) and their crown-casting (4:10-11). Inside that frame stand the four living creatures (4:6-8). At the center stands the throne and the one seated on it (4:2-3). Bauckham, in *The Climax of Prophecy*, reads this concentric structure as the chapter's primary theological argument: every layer of created reality, in concentric rings outward from the throne, exists for and orbits around the act of worship. Revelation will repeat this geometry in chapter 5 with the Lamb in the center, in chapter 7 with the multitude before the throne, and in chapters 21-22 with the New Jerusalem.

The chapter is also a hymn-book. Two hymns are quoted in full: the *trisagion* of 4:8 ("Holy, holy, holy") and the worthiness-hymn of 4:11 ("Worthy are you, our Lord and God"). John Christopher Thomas, in his Pentecostal commentary, observes that Revelation contains more direct quotation of liturgical material than any other New Testament book, and chapter 4 is its first dense hymnal cluster. The hymns are not decorative; they carry the theological argument. The trisagion declares God's holiness in the present and across time ("who was and is and is to come" — ὁ ἦν καὶ ὁ ὢν καὶ ὁ ἐρχόμενος, 4:8); the worthiness-hymn grounds that holiness in creation ("for you created all things, and by your will they existed and were created," 4:11).

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

Within the New Testament canon, Revelation 4 is the chapter that anchors the whole book in the worship of the Creator. The doxology of 4:11 is functionally a creation hymn — closer in form to Psalm 104 or Job 38-41 than to anything in Paul. It is the throne-room counterpart to Hebrews 12:22-24, where the church gathers in the heavenly Jerusalem.

**Patristic** readings treat Revelation 4 as the locus classicus of heavenly worship. Justin Martyr cites the trisagion as evidence that Christian liturgy participates in heavenly liturgy. Irenaeus reads the four living creatures, by the late second century, as figures of the four Gospels — lion (Mark, in his ordering), ox (Luke), man (Matthew), eagle (John); the assignment shifted across patristic writers, but the hermeneutic stuck and shaped Christian iconography permanently. Origen, characteristically, allegorizes: the throne is the soul of the just; the elders are the perfected. Augustine, in *City of God*, reads chapter 4 as proof that the city of God has its citizenry already in heaven, distinct from but related to the earthly church.

**Medieval** interpretation deepens the iconographic harvest. Joachim of Fiore reads the seven torches as the seven ages of the church, the four creatures as the four major Old Testament covenants. Bonaventure organizes his theology of orders of angelic ministry partly from chapter 4. The Sanctus of the Roman Mass — sung at every Eucharist since the fifth century — quotes 4:8 directly, fusing it with Isaiah 6 and Matthew 21:9. The four-creature symbolism becomes the dominant programmatic decoration of medieval Gospel-books and tympana, from the Book of Kells to Chartres.

**Reformation** readers, particularly Luther in his late preface to Revelation, accept the chapter's heavenly worship as straightforward but resist the Joachimite historicizing. Calvin, who never wrote a commentary on Revelation, leaves no extended treatment, but his successors in Geneva read 4:11's "you created all things" as one of the strongest scriptural buttresses of the doctrine of creation *ex nihilo* against any Origenist eternalism.

**Critical** scholarship since the late twentieth century has restored the chapter's pulse. Bauckham's *Theology of the Book of Revelation* opens with chapters 4-5 as the book's theological center. Adela Yarbro Collins reads the imperial-cult counter-narrative directly: Domitian had himself acclaimed *dominus et deus* — "lord and god" — in the very province from which John writes; chapter 4's "our Lord and God" (ὁ Κύριος καὶ ὁ θεὸς ἡμῶν, 4:11) is a deliberate cuckoo-egg laid in imperial vocabulary. Brian Blount, in *Can I Get a Witness?*, reads the chapter through an Africentric lens as the rhetorical re-throning of the marginalized God against the visible thrones of empire. Allan Boesak, writing under apartheid in *Comfort and Protest*, reads the open door as a political act: the heavens open, and from above one can see what the regime would prefer remained invisible.

**Contemporary** Jewish scholarship has recognized the chapter as a New Testament window into Second Temple Jewish piety. Daniel Boyarin's *Border Lines* argues that the high Christology that will erupt in chapter 5 develops here on a fully Jewish substrate — Memra, Logos, divine Wisdom traditions provide the binitarian-monotheistic grammar that makes Revelation 4-5 imaginable. Christopher Rowland and James Davila place chapter 4 inside the Hekhalot tradition rather than against it; Bruce Chilton has paired the chapter with the targumic traditions of the heavenly throne. The Sanctus and the Kedushah, sung at synagogue and at Mass on the same Sabbath morning, share an ancestor in this chapter and in Isaiah 6.

The four interpretive schools each find purchase on chapter 4, though it resists the most aggressive historicizings.

- **Preterist**: chapter 4 sets the heavenly stage for judgments that will fall on first-century Jerusalem (Gentry) or first-century Rome (Aune); the open door is the unveiling of God's throne *now*, in opposition to imperial throne-claims being made in Asia at this very hour.
- **Historicist**: especially in Reformation-era exposition, chapter 4 was read as the heavenly parallel to the church on earth across the ages; the elders represented the totality of redeemed humanity worshipping continuously throughout history.
- **Futurist** (dispensational): the dispensational tradition of John Walvoord and Charles Ryrie reads 4:1 — "come up here" — as the hidden moment of the rapture; the church, having vanished from the earthly scene at the end of chapter 3, is now seen worshiping in heaven through chapters 4-19.
- **Idealist**: the chapter depicts a permanent reality — God's throne, occupied; creation's worship, continuous; the elders' praise, unceasing. The chapter is not a vision *of* a coming event but a vision *behind* every event.

## Lens 4 — The Theological Intent

What God is doing in Revelation 4: the curtain pulls back. John is shown what lies behind the visible architecture of empire. There is a throne, and someone is on it, and his identity is not Caesar. The proper response of every created thing — angelic, ecclesial, cosmic — is worship; and that worship is grounded not in the church's redemption (that will come in chapter 5) but, more fundamentally, in creation itself: "for you created all things." The chapter is an act of theological reorientation aimed at small Christian communities in Asia who are being told daily, by the visible structures around them, that the throne belongs to someone else.

### Tension One: The Identity of the Twenty-Four Elders

Three positions, and each shapes the chapter's ecclesiology.

The first reads the elders as *the redeemed people of God*: twelve patriarchs plus twelve apostles, or the totality of Old- and New-covenant saints. R.H. Charles, George Caird, and Robert Mounce favor this. The strength of this view is that the elders are crowned and white-robed (4:4) — language Revelation later applies explicitly to the redeemed (3:5; 6:11; 7:9). Its weakness is that the elders appear *distinct* from the redeemed in 5:9, where they sing about those whom Christ "ransomed for God."

The second reads the elders as a *heavenly council* of high angelic beings, in the lineage of 1 Kings 22 and Psalm 82. The strength is the chapter's persistent angelic atmosphere; the weakness is the absence of any explicit textual signal that these are angels (Revelation does name angels elsewhere: 5:11, 7:11).

The third — the position to steelman, since it is the least intuitive to modern Protestant readers — is that the elders are the *heavenly counterpart of the twenty-four priestly courses* of 1 Chronicles 24, instituted by David to serve in rotation at the Temple. Aune, Bauckham, and Hurtado make the case. The number is not random — twenty-four is otherwise unmotivated in Jewish tradition *except* for the priestly courses, which were a structural feature of Second Temple worship every literate first-century Jewish reader would have known. The elders' actions in chapters 4-5 are precisely priestly: they fall down, they offer incense (5:8), they sing the Sanctus-style hymns. On this reading, the chapter teaches that the church's worship on earth is not merely commanded but *participates*: the priestly courses of the heavenly Temple are doing this all the time; what the church does on Sunday is a finite participation in the infinite reality.

**Where this leaves us**: the chapter does not require resolution of the elders' identity to make its theological point — that the proper inhabitant of the throne-room is Jewish in idiom, priestly in posture, and creator-focused in confession. But the priestly-courses reading carries the most freight, because it makes the chapter not just a vision of heaven but a vision of *liturgy* — and Revelation will turn out to be a sustained polemic about which liturgy claims the universe.

### Tension Two: Universal Worship and the Question of Universal Salvation

The doxology of 4:11 — "you created all things, and by your will they existed and were created" — combines with 5:13's later expansion ("every creature in heaven and on earth and under the earth and in the sea") to raise the question: does the universal worship of created reality imply, or anticipate, universal salvation?

Three positions.

The first, *universalist recapitulation*, finds in 4:11 and 5:13 a textual basis for the hope that every created thing will eventually be brought into the worship of God. Origen's *apokatastasis* tradition, revived by David Bentley Hart in *That All Shall Be Saved*, by Robin Parry in *The Evangelical Universalist*, and pre-figured by the Cappadocian Gregory of Nyssa, reads chapter 4's creation-doxology as a prolepsis: if God created all things, and if all things are oriented toward worship, then the eschaton must, at minimum, complete the creational arc. The strength of this view is its grounding in creation rather than in voluntarist soteriology.

The second, *universal acknowledgment under judgment*, is the Augustinian-Calvinist mainstream. All things will indeed acknowledge God's worthiness — but acknowledgment is not the same as participation in the beatific vision. Some confess from below the throne, others from before it. Augustine, Calvin, and, in their wake, the bulk of magisterial Catholic and Reformed eschatology distinguish ontological acknowledgment from soteriological inclusion.

The third — the position to steelman, because it is rarely articulated cleanly — is *cosmic recapitulation without anthropological universalism*. The Pauline language of *anakephalaiōsis* in Ephesians 1:10 ("to unite all things in him, things in heaven and things on earth") does not demand human universalism; it demands that creation as a whole be reconciled. Revelation 4-5's creational doxology can be read this way: the cosmos is restored to its proper orientation; the moral question of which humans are part of that restored cosmos remains open and contested. Karl Barth in *Church Dogmatics* IV/3 hovered around this position without committing fully; Hans Urs von Balthasar's *Dare We Hope* gave it Catholic articulation. The chapter, on this reading, refuses to settle the question and refuses to let the question disappear.

**Where this leaves us**: chapter 4 is creation hymn before it is judgment scene. Whatever Revelation will later say about the unrepentant, it has first established that the throne is a creator's throne — and the relationship between creator and creation is not symmetrical to the relationship between judge and defendant. The chapter forces the question; it does not answer it.

**Closing question**: if the worship described in chapter 4 is happening *now*, and the door has stood open since John's vision, what would change in our reading of empire — ancient or modern — if we were to assume, as the chapter does, that the loudest throne in the room is not the one that humans built?

## The Thread

- **Ezekiel 1:4-28** — the chariot-throne vision; jasper, sapphire, rainbow-brightness, and four living creatures provide John's primary visual vocabulary, though John redistributes the creatures' faces and amplifies the wings.
- **Isaiah 6:1-3** — the trisagion; John's hymn of 4:8 is the direct echo, with the eschatological "who was and is and is to come" added to the seraphic original.
- **1 Enoch 14:8-25** — the closest formal Jewish-apocalyptic counterpart, with an ascent through gates, throne of crystal, and surrounding angelic worship; demonstrates that John writes within a recognizable genre.
- **Hebrews 12:22-24** — the parallel New Testament heavenly assembly, where the church on earth is said to have already come "to Mount Zion and to the city of the living God"; chapter 4 supplies the visual the Hebrews passage describes liturgically.
