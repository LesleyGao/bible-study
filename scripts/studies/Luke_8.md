# Luke 8: Women Patrons, Parables, and the Storm Across the Lake

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

Luke 8 opens with one of the most historically arresting verses in the Synoptic tradition: a list of named women who travel with Jesus and the Twelve, providing financial support "out of their means" (***ek tōn hyparchontōn autais***, ἐκ τῶν ὑπαρχόντων αὐταῖς, 8:3). Three women are named — Mary called Magdalene, Joanna the wife of **Chuza**, **Herod's** household manager (***epitropos***, ἐπίτροπος, 8:3), and Susanna — followed by "many others." Each name lights up first-century social geography in a different way.

**Mary called Magdalene** ("of Magdala") locates her in the most economically dynamic Galilean lakeside town. Magdala (Hebrew *Migdal Nunaya*, "tower of the fishes"; Greek *Tarichaea*, "the place of salting") was the regional center of the fish-salting industry that exported preserved Galilee fish across the Mediterranean. The first-century synagogue uncovered at Magdala in 2009 by **Dina Avshalom-Gor** and the Israel Antiquities Authority, alongside the broader Magdala Project led by **Stefano De Luca** and **Marcela Zapata-Meza**, has revealed a town with paved streets, a ritual bath complex, mosaic floors, and the famed **Magdala Stone** (carved with menorah and what appears to be a representation of the Jerusalem Temple). This was not a backwater but a wealthy commercial town — exactly the kind of place a woman of independent means might emerge. The "seven demons" tradition of Mary's healing (8:2) provides her with a back-story of profound deliverance, though it does not, in any version of Luke's narrative, identify her with the sinful woman of 7:36-50. That conflation is the work of **Pope Gregory the Great's** Homily 33 (591 CE), which fused Mary of Bethany, Mary Magdalene, and the unnamed sinner into a single composite figure — a fusion the Eastern Orthodox tradition never accepted and the Catholic Church formally undid in the 1969 calendar reform.

**Joanna** is even more remarkable: wife of **Chuza**, **Herod Antipas's** ***epitropos***, the steward managing the tetrarch's household and estates. This is not peripheral but Herodian-court connection. Antipas's court at Tiberias and Sepphoris employed substantial administrative staff; archaeological work at Tiberias (founded c. 19 CE on the lake's western shore) has documented the Herodian palace complex. Joanna's presence among Jesus's followers means the Jesus movement had penetrated, however obliquely, into Herod's own household — a fact Luke 9:7-9 will narratively dramatize when Herod's "perplexity" about Jesus is reported. **Richard Bauckham's** *Gospel Women* devotes an extensive chapter to Joanna, arguing that she may be the **Junia** of Romans 16:7 ("outstanding among the apostles"), with Joanna as her Hebrew name and Junia as her Roman one.

**Susanna**, otherwise unknown, has the Hebrew name *Shoshannah* ("lily"), the name of the heroine of the apocryphal Susanna-and-the-elders narrative. Her presence in this list, named alongside Mary and Joanna, marks her as a person of sufficient economic independence to fund traveling ministry.

The economic detail is significant. **Carolyn Osiek** and **Margaret MacDonald's** *A Woman's Place: House Churches in Earliest Christianity* and **Tal Ilan's** *Jewish Women in Greco-Roman Palestine* have documented that women in the first century could and did own property, receive inheritance, and conduct independent economic activity, especially widows and women whose husbands' professional status (like Chuza's) provided them resource-channels.

The chapter then shifts geography across the lake. The "country of the Gerasenes" (8:26-39) presents a textual-geographical puzzle. The town of Gerasa lay over thirty miles from the lake, in the Decapolis, making the swine-rushing-down-the-steep-bank narrative impossible. Manuscript variants offer Gadara (still some miles inland) and Gergesa, a smaller village whose location at modern **Kursi** on the eastern lakeshore matches the steep bank topography. **Joel Green** and **Fitzmyer** treat Gerasa as Luke's preferred reading, with the geographical strain accepted as part of the territory's broader Greco-Roman pagan-region designation. Archaeological work at Kursi by **Vassilios Tzaferis** has identified a Byzantine pilgrimage church marking the traditional site of the exorcism, with associated structures interpreted as commemorating the cave-tombs of the demoniac.

The presence of pigs (8:32) is itself a marker: this is Gentile territory. Jewish farmers did not raise pigs (Lev 11:7); a herd of two thousand (Mark's number, which Luke does not repeat) implies a substantial Gentile pastoral economy across the lake from Jewish Galilee.

Three Greek terms anchor the chapter. ***Diakoneō*** (διακονέω, 8:3) — "they provided" — Luke uses this verb for the women's patronage-service, the same root that becomes "deacon" in early church polity. ***Sōzō*** (σώζω, 8:48, 8:50) — "made well / saved" — the chapter's theological key-word, with deliberate ambiguity between physical healing and salvation. ***Kraspedon*** (κράσπεδον, 8:44) — "fringe" — the *tzitzit* tassels that observant Jewish men wore on their garments per Numbers 15:38; the woman with the discharge touches Jesus's *tzitzit*, a detail that grounds the scene firmly in Jewish ritual practice.

## Lens 2 — The Literature

Luke 8 is structured as five linked units with carefully orchestrated thematic resonances: the women patrons (8:1-3), the parable of the sower with its interpretation (8:4-15), the lamp and family-of-Jesus pericope (8:16-21), the calming of the storm (8:22-25), the Gerasene demoniac (8:26-39), and the intercalated Jairus's daughter / hemorrhaging woman (8:40-56). The sequence moves geographically (lakeshore — across the lake — back to Galilee), thematically (word, faith, fear, healing, raising), and Christologically (parabolic mystery, sovereign over creation, sovereign over demons, sovereign over death).

The **women patrons opening** (8:1-3) is uniquely Lukan and has no Synoptic parallel. **Turid Karlsen Seim's** *The Double Message* devotes substantial analysis to this list, arguing that Luke's gendered narrative-structure simultaneously honors and constrains the women: they are named and present as patrons, but their patronage is structurally subordinate to the male apostolic mission. **Schüssler Fiorenza** in *In Memory of Her* recovers the women's patronage as constitutive of the early Jesus movement and resists the subordinating reading. **Beverly Gaventa** and **Carolyn Osiek** have developed the theological implications of the women's economic agency.

The **parable of the sower** and its interpretation (8:4-15) follows Mark 4:1-20 closely, with Lukan distinctives. **Joel Green** notes that Luke's interpretation of the seed-on-good-soil emphasizes "**hold fast in an honest and good heart**" (***en kardia kalē kai agathē***, ἐν καρδίᾳ καλῇ καὶ ἀγαθῇ, 8:15) — Hellenistic moral-psychology vocabulary, ***kalos kai agathos*** being the classical Greek ideal of the "noble and good" person. **Bovon** (Hermeneia) reads this as Luke deliberately translating the parable into Hellenistic ethical categories accessible to his Greco-Roman audience. **Bock** (BECNT) sees the addition as Luke's pastoral emphasis on perseverance.

The "secret of the kingdom" saying (8:10) preserves the Markan paradox: parables both reveal and conceal, with the citation of **Isaiah 6:9-10** ("seeing they may not see, and hearing they may not understand"). Luke softens Mark's harshest version (Mark 4:12 has *hina*, "in order that," suggesting purpose; Luke retains this but does not develop it). **Joachim Jeremias's** *Parables of Jesus* argued that the original parable was a positive, non-allegorical proclamation of harvest; the allegorical interpretation in 8:11-15 represents early-church catechetical development. Most modern commentators retain the interpretation as Lukan-redactional but historically rooted.

The **family-of-Jesus pericope** (8:19-21) is significantly Lukan in its softer treatment. Mark 3:31-35 places the scene in narrative tension: Jesus's family thinks he is "out of his mind" (Mark 3:21) and comes to seize him; Jesus distances himself from them. Luke removes the negative framing entirely; his version is a positive saying about the family-of-discipleship, with no implicit critique of Mary. **Beverly Gaventa** in *Mary: Glimpses of the Mother of Jesus* sees this as Luke's positive Marian theology — the same Mary who "treasured all these things in her heart" (2:19, 51) is presumptively included among "those who hear the word of God and do it."

The **storm-stilling** (8:22-25) follows Mark 4:35-41 with Lukan compressions. The disciples' "Master, Master, we are perishing!" (***Epistata, epistata***, ἐπιστάτα, ἐπιστάτα) uses Luke's distinctive title of address (Luke alone among the Synoptics uses this term, six times). Jesus's "where is your faith?" replaces Mark's harsher "have you no faith yet?" The Lukan softening pattern continues.

The **Gerasene demoniac** (8:26-39) is the longest exorcism narrative in the Synoptic tradition. **Conzelmann** and others have noted the political-allegorical resonance of the demons named "Legion" — a Roman military unit of 5,000–6,000 soldiers; the demons drowning in the lake echoes Pharaoh's army drowning in the Red Sea. **Richard Horsley** in *Jesus and the Spiral of Violence* reads the political-allegorical dimension explicitly: the exorcism dramatizes liberation from imperial occupation. Luke retains Mark's politically-loaded language while moderating Mark's wilder details (Mark's two thousand pigs becomes Luke's anonymous "large herd").

The **intercalated Jairus / hemorrhaging woman** narrative (8:40-56) preserves Mark's classic sandwich-structure (Mark 5:21-43): the outer story of Jairus's daughter is interrupted by the inner story of the woman with the twelve-year discharge, with the "twelve years" detail linking the two (Jairus's daughter is "about twelve years of age," 8:42). The narrative pairing places a synagogue-ruler's named daughter and an unnamed ritually-impure woman in deliberate juxtaposition. Both are healed; both are called "daughter" (8:48, 8:54); both narratives function as faith-and-power pericopes.

Luke's distinctive details: where Mark says the woman "had spent all that she had on physicians and was no better but rather grew worse," Luke (the physician, by ancient tradition) softens the medical critique to "though she had spent all her living on physicians, she could not be healed by anyone" (8:43, with some manuscripts omitting the physicians-clause). **Loveday Alexander's** work on Lukan medical-vocabulary maintains the traditional connection of Luke with Hellenistic medicine, while critical scholarship has been more skeptical that the medical vocabulary requires a physician-author.

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

The **Jewish interpretive trajectory** for Luke 8 engages multiple intersecting traditions. **Tal Ilan's** *Jewish Women in Greco-Roman Palestine*, **Levine's** *The Misunderstood Jew*, and **Susan Marks's** essays on women patrons in the *Jewish Annotated New Testament* have collectively dismantled the older Christian polemical claim that first-century Judaism was uniquely oppressive to women. Women in Jewish communities of the period could own property, conduct business, and patronize religious institutions; the **Aphrodisias inscription** documents a similar phenomenon in the synagogue of that city. Mary, Joanna, and Susanna's economic patronage of the Jesus movement is intelligible within first-century Jewish women's documented agency.

The hemorrhaging woman scene engages Jewish ritual-impurity law (Leviticus 15:25-30), where a woman with prolonged menstrual or other genital discharge was *zavah*, ritually impure for the duration plus seven days after cessation. **Mishnah Niddah** and rabbinic tractates develop the casuistry. The woman's twelve-year duration would have made her chronically impure, transmitting impurity to whatever she touched. Her touching Jesus's *tzitzit* in the press of the crowd would have, on a strict reading of the law, transmitted impurity to him — but Luke's narrative reverses the flow: power goes out from Jesus to her. **E.P. Sanders** notes that the impurity laws did not in fact carry the social-stigmatic weight that older Christian polemics assigned them; they were practical regulations, not occasions for ostracism. **Levine** stresses that the woman's faith in Jesus's healing power is an entirely Jewish theological move — she is acting within a recognizable Jewish prophetic-healing framework.

The Gerasene exorcism raises Jewish questions about Gentile territory and impurity — pigs, tombs, demons, all classic Jewish-impurity markers. **Sanders** and **Vermes** read Jesus's willingness to enter Gentile territory as continuous with prophetic-eschatological expectation that the Gentiles would be brought to YHWH at the consummation. The demoniac's restoration ("clothed and in his right mind") and his return to "his own home" to declare what God had done is, in **Daniel Boyarin's** reading, a programmatic Jewish-eschatological scene: even Gentile territory is touched by the in-breaking kingdom.

The **Christian interpretive trajectory** is dense for this chapter. **Origen's** *Homilies on Luke* read the parable of the sower allegorically, with the four soils as the four temperaments of human souls. **Ambrose's** *Expositio in Lucam* developed the Gerasene exorcism as the conversion of the Gentile world; the demoniac's return to his home as the Gentile mission. **Augustine's** *Sermons* on Mark and Matthew (Luke's parallels) developed the family-of-Jesus pericope as a teaching about the priority of faith over biological kinship. **Cyril of Alexandria** and **Bede** synthesized.

**Calvin** in his *Harmony* read the women patrons-list as evidence that the Jesus movement was not, contrary to ancient slanders, a band of disreputable men: the presence of women of standing legitimates the mission. On the parable of the sower: the four soils as four kinds of hearers, a thoroughly anti-allegorical reading focused on the parable's catechetical use. On the storm: a Christology demonstration; on the Gerasene: a Christology demonstration; on Jairus: a Christology demonstration. Calvin is consistently christocentric where the Fathers were allegorical.

**Luther** preached extensively on the women in the gospel, finding in Mary Magdalene the type of justification by faith — her former demonic possession the depths of human bondage; Christ's deliverance the gratuity of grace.

Twentieth-century: **Conzelmann's** *Theology of St. Luke* read the chapter within his salvation-historical scheme; **Tannehill's** *Narrative Unity* read it as a coherent demonstration of Jesus's authority across registers (word, creation, demons, disease, death); **Fitzmyer** AB, **Joel Green** NICNT, **Nolland** WBC, **Bock** BECNT, **Marshall** NIGTC, **Johnson** Sacra Pagina, **Bovon** Hermeneia all treat the chapter extensively. **Bovon's** reception-history is unmatched on Mary Magdalene's reception across Christian tradition; he documents the slow recovery of her independent identity from the medieval composite figure.

**Feminist scholarship** has revolutionized readings of Luke 8. **Schüssler Fiorenza** *In Memory of Her* recovered the women patrons as constitutive of the Jesus movement against the "great men" historiography. **Seim** *The Double Message* developed the more ambivalent reading: Luke is unusually attentive to women but structures their presence in subordinate-patronage modes. **Beverly Gaventa** *Mary: Glimpses of the Mother of Jesus* explored the positive Lukan Mariology. **Carolyn Osiek**, **Margaret MacDonald**, and **Lynn Cohick** have placed the women patrons within the broader social history of women's religious patronage in the Greco-Roman world.

**Liberation and post-colonial readings** find Luke 8 fertile. **Justo González** *Luke (Belief)* foregrounds the demoniac's deliverance as paradigmatic of liberation from systemic-violent forces. **Richard Horsley** *Jesus and the Spiral of Violence* reads "Legion" as transparent political allegory: the colonized people's psychological possession by the imperial occupier, dramatized and resolved through exorcism. **Kenneth Bailey** *Jesus Through Middle Eastern Eyes* offers extensive cultural-context readings of the family-of-Jesus pericope and the Jairus/woman intercalation.

## Lens 4 — The Theological Intent

**Tension One: What is the theological meaning of women's patronage of Jesus's mission (8:1-3)?** Three positions. The **structural-egalitarian reading** (Schüssler Fiorenza, much feminist scholarship) holds that Luke 8:1-3 reveals an egalitarian inner-circle structure of the Jesus movement that the later church suppressed: women were not merely beneficiaries of Jesus's ministry but co-equal participants and patrons, with economic and missional agency that prefigures full ecclesial equality. Steelman: the women's named-and-public role, their economic agency, and their resurrection-witness in Luke 24 collectively make egalitarian reading textually warranted; the subsequent subordination is reception-history, not Lukan substance. The **structural-hierarchical-but-honoring reading** (Seim, Gaventa) holds that Luke is genuinely innovative in honoring women's presence and contribution but maintains the structural priority of the male apostolic Twelve; the women provide *out of their means* but do not preach, teach, or hold ecclesial office. Steelman: the chapter explicitly distinguishes "the twelve" who travel with him from the women who provide; Luke's careful gender-structuring across the gospel suggests deliberate ordering, not incidental hierarchy. The **prophetic-fulfillment reading** (Bauckham, Joel Green) holds that the women's patronage fulfills Hebrew prophetic patterns where women appear at decisive salvation-historical moments (Miriam, Deborah, Hannah, Judith), making Luke's gendered narrative a Hebrew-prophetic recapitulation rather than either egalitarian innovation or hierarchical maintenance. Steelman: the Lukan infancy narrative (Mary, Elizabeth, Anna) and resurrection narrative (the women at the tomb) frame the gospel with prominent women; the prophetic-fulfillment frame integrates these. **Where this leaves us:** the prophetic-fulfillment reading honors the textual evidence without forcing modern egalitarian or anti-egalitarian categories onto the text. Luke is presenting women's agency in the Jesus movement as fulfilling Hebrew patterns; how that agency relates to subsequent ecclesial structures is a separate (and contested) question.

**Tension Two: What does it mean that the demons named "Legion" are sent into pigs that drown in the lake (8:32-33)?** Three positions. The **historicist reading** (most patristic and Reformation tradition, much modern evangelical commentary) holds that the narrative records a historical exorcism, with the demons' transfer to the pigs and the herd's destruction as documented event. The pigs' drowning demonstrates the demons' destructiveness and the totality of the man's deliverance. Steelman: Luke claims to be writing historical narrative (1:1-4); the Synoptic agreement on the basic event-shape supports historicity; Jesus performed exorcisms throughout his ministry. The **political-allegorical reading** (Horsley, **Ched Myers** in *Binding the Strong Man*) holds that "Legion" is transparently the Roman military legion (specifically the Legio X Fretensis stationed in the region after 70 CE, whose emblem was a wild boar); the demons drowning in the lake recapitulate Pharaoh's army drowning in the Red Sea. The narrative is not anti-historical but politically-resistant theological-narrative, dramatizing Jesus's authority over imperial demonic-systemic powers. Steelman: Mark/Luke's first audiences would have heard "Legion" with unmistakable imperial reference; the boar emblem and the lake-drowning are too pointed to be coincidental; resistance-allegory is well-documented in Second Temple Jewish literature (Daniel, the Animal Apocalypse). The **dialectical reading** (Joel Green, much mainstream commentary) holds that the historical and the political-allegorical are not exclusive: the historical exorcism is narratively shaped to bear political-theological resonance, with both registers operative simultaneously. Steelman: Lukan narrative-craft consistently layers historical event with theological-political meaning; reducing the scene to either pole impoverishes its work. **Where this leaves us:** the dialectical reading is most adequate to the Lukan text. The narrative makes a Christological claim (Jesus has authority over demons), a soteriological claim (the destroyed-man is restored), and a political-theological claim (Jesus's kingdom confronts imperial-demonic systems) — and these claims are mutually reinforcing.

**Unresolved question:** Why does Jesus tell the Gerasene demoniac to *return home and proclaim*, when he has elsewhere commanded silence (the leper of 5:14, the parents of Jairus's daughter at 8:56)? The scholarly tradition's "messianic secret" theme (Wrede, classic in Markan studies) has been imported into Lukan readings, but Luke's pattern is distinctive: he tells the Gentile to proclaim and the Jewish recipients to remain silent. Is this a programmatic Gentile-mission anticipation? An eschatological-timing distinction? The text does not specify, and the question opens onto Lukan theology of mission and revelation that resists easy systematization.

## The Thread

Three cross-references mark this chapter's web. **Isaiah 6:9-10** — "seeing they may not see" — is cited at 8:10 and stands at the center of Lukan theology of revelation-and-hardening; it will be cited again at the climax of Acts (Acts 28:26-27), making the parable-of-the-sower scene a structural anchor between the gospel and its sequel. **Numbers 15:38-39** — the *tzitzit* commanded for Israelite garments — stands behind the woman's touching Jesus's "fringe" (8:44); the Jewish ritual-practice anchor grounds Jesus's healing power within Torah-observance, not against it. **Acts 13:1** — among the prophets and teachers at Antioch is "**Manaen** a member of the court of Herod the tetrarch," another Herodian-court Jesus-follower, suggesting that the Lukan thread that begins with Joanna in 8:3 continues into the Antiochene mission. Watch also for **Luke 24:1-12** — the women named at 8:2-3 (Mary Magdalene, Joanna) reappear at the empty tomb as the first witnesses of the resurrection; the Lukan women's narrative arc spans the gospel.
