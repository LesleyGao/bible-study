# 1 Chronicles 28 — Going Deeper

## Lens 1: The Historical World

Chapter 28 is the Chronicler's most distinctive composition in the entire Davidic narrative. It has *no parallel in Samuel-Kings*. Where 1 Kings 1 narrates a tense, contested, late-night succession in which the dying David is roused by Bathsheba and Nathan to declare Solomon king before Adonijah's faction can seize the throne, the Chronicler stages something completely different: a public, daylight, kingdom-wide assembly in which David, on his feet, transfers both the throne and the temple plans to Solomon in front of "all the officials of Israel" (verse 1). The contrast is the most important single fact about this chapter, and the Chronicler chose it deliberately.

David assembles "the officials of the tribes, the officers of the divisions that served the king, the commanders of thousands, the commanders of hundreds, the stewards of all the property and livestock of the king and his sons, together with the palace officials, the mighty men and all the seasoned warriors" (verse 1). The list is essentially the cast of chapter 27 reassembled in Jerusalem. This is a constitutional moment: the entire administrative spine of the kingdom witnesses the succession and receives the temple-building charge.

David rises and addresses them. The speech follows a recognizable Hebrew Bible form — the *farewell address* — used by Moses (Deuteronomy 31–33), Joshua (Joshua 23–24), and Samuel (1 Samuel 12). Sara Japhet, Hugh Williamson, and Mark Boda all note that the Chronicler is consciously placing David in this prophetic-leader-succession lineage.

The speech opens with the temple-disqualification: *I had it in my heart to build a house of rest for the ark of the covenant of the LORD... but God said to me, "You may not build a house for my name, for you are a man of war and have shed blood"* (verses 2–3). The Hebrew phrase *ish milchamot ve-damim shafakhta* (אִישׁ מִלְחָמוֹת וְדָמִים שָׁפָכְתָּ, "a man of wars, and you have shed blood") echoes the more developed disqualification speech of 1 Chronicles 22:8. The Chronicler is willing to put a permanent moral cost on David's military career — the very career chapter 27 just celebrated administratively. Roddy Braun observes the calibration: David is the founder, but he is also blood-stained, and the temple's purity requires a successor who has not yet had to bleed for the kingdom.

Solomon's election is then framed in a striking sequence of nested choices. Hebrew *bachar* (בָּחַר, "chose") appears repeatedly: God chose Judah as leader (verse 4), then within Judah chose David's father's house, then chose David among his brothers, then has now chosen Solomon among David's many sons (verse 5). The verb is the same one used of Israel's election (Deuteronomy 7:6–7) and of Zion's election (Psalm 132:13). The Chronicler is mapping Solomon's accession onto the entire pattern of divine election that runs through the Hebrew Bible. Gary Knoppers calls this the Chronicler's "concentric election" — God's choice narrows from a tribe to a house to a man to a son.

Verse 5 contains a phrase the Chronicler is fond of: Solomon will sit "on the throne of the kingdom of the LORD over Israel" (*kisseh malkhut Yahweh al Yisrael*, כִּסֵּא מַלְכוּת יְהוָה עַל יִשְׂרָאֵל). The Davidic throne is Yahweh's throne. The king is regent, not proprietor. This theology becomes one of the Chronicler's signature claims (1 Chronicles 29:23 will repeat it).

Verses 6–7 introduce the conditional element. God has said of Solomon, *I have chosen him to be my son, and I will be his father* — quoting and extending the dynastic promise of 2 Samuel 7:14 and 1 Chronicles 17:13 — *I will establish his kingdom forever if he continues strong in keeping my commandments and my rules, as he is today*. Steven McKenzie notes the editorial work the Chronicler is doing here. In 2 Samuel 7 the promise to David is mostly unconditional. In 1 Chronicles 28, applied specifically to Solomon, it is hedged with a Deuteronomistic-style conditional. Solomon's own dynasty depends on his obedience; the Davidic line as a whole does not.

The charge to Solomon in verse 9 is one of the most exalted statements of divine knowledge in the Hebrew Bible: *the LORD searches all hearts and understands every plan and thought*. Hebrew *Yahweh doresh kol levavot ve-khol yetzer machshavot mevin* (יְהוָה דּוֹרֵשׁ כָּל לְבָבוֹת וְכָל יֵצֶר מַחֲשָׁבוֹת מֵבִין). The verb *doresh* — "searches, examines, requires" — and the noun *yetzer* — "inclination, frame, formation" — together produce a doctrine of divine omniscience that goes beyond external observation to internal disposition. Then comes the conditional: *if you seek him, he will be found by you, but if you forsake him, he will cast you off forever*.

Verses 11–19 then transition to the temple plans. The Hebrew is *tavnit* (תַּבְנִית, "pattern, design, blueprint") — exactly the word used in Exodus 25:9, 40 for the tabernacle pattern shown to Moses on the mountain. David hands Solomon the tavnit of the vestibule, the houses, the treasuries, the upper rooms, the inner chambers, the room for the mercy seat (Hebrew *bayt ha-kapporet*), the courts, the chambers, the priestly and Levitical divisions, the vessels with their gold and silver weights, the lampstands, the showbread tables, the forks and basins and cups, the altar of incense, and "his plan for the golden chariot of the cherubim that spread their wings and covered the ark of the covenant of the LORD" (verse 18). The list is detailed enough to be visualized.

Verse 19 is decisive: *All this he made clear to me in writing from the hand of the LORD, all the work to be done according to the plan*. Hebrew *ha-kol bi-ktav mi-yad Yahweh alai hiskil* (הַכֹּל בִּכְתָב מִיַּד יְהוָה עָלַי הִשְׂכִּיל). The Chronicler is making a remarkable claim: David received the temple plan in writing, directly from Yahweh's hand. Williamson and Japhet flag this as a Mosaic typology — David is to the temple what Moses was to the tabernacle. Both received a heavenly pattern, both transmitted it to a builder-successor, both stood at the threshold of a project they would not personally execute.

Verse 20 closes with the Joshua-formula: *Be strong and courageous* — Hebrew *chazaq ve-ematz* (חֲזַק וֶאֱמָץ). This is the same charge Moses gave to Joshua in Deuteronomy 31:7, 23, and that Yahweh repeated to Joshua at his commissioning (Joshua 1:6, 7, 9). The Chronicler is drawing a line: as Moses to Joshua, so David to Solomon. The temple is to the kingdom what the conquest was to the wilderness — the project that completes a generation's preparation.

## Lens 2: The Literature

The chapter is composed as a single dramatic scene with three speeches inside it: the public address to Israel (verses 2–8), the personal charge to Solomon (verses 9–10), and the second personal charge after the transfer of plans (verse 20–21). Between the second and third speech sits the most distinctive literary unit — a long descriptive enumeration of the *tavnit* (verses 11–19), which mirrors the tabernacle-instruction passages of Exodus 25–31 in genre and rhythm. The chapter therefore inhabits two literary modes simultaneously: farewell address (Mosaic-Joshuanic genre) and tabernacle-instruction (Exodus genre). The Chronicler has fused them into a single succession scene.

## Lens 3: The Larger Story

The Chronicler's omission of 1 Kings 1 is the most consequential literary choice he makes in this part of the book. In Kings, Solomon's accession is the product of a palace coup orchestrated by Nathan and Bathsheba while Adonijah is already feasting with his supporters. David, near death, has to be prompted by his wife to remember his earlier promise. The narrative is morally ambiguous. The Chronicler eliminates this entirely. There is no Adonijah, no Bathsheba intervention, no Nathan whispering in the king's ear, no rival faction. The succession is public, divinely chosen, and unchallenged. McKenzie reads this as theological housekeeping; Williamson reads it as the Chronicler's intentional construction of a *legitimating* narrative for the post-exilic temple community. The Yehud reader needs to know that the temple stands on a clean transfer of authority, not on the residue of a coup.

The Jewish trajectory takes the chapter as a charter for the temple itself. The detail of the *tavnit* — including the "golden chariot of the cherubim" (verse 18, Hebrew *merkavat ha-keruvim*, מֶרְכָּבַת הַכְּרוּבִים) — became one of the seedbeds of later Jewish merkavah mysticism, the contemplative tradition that focused on Ezekiel's chariot vision and on the heavenly pattern behind earthly worship. Maimonides treats David's reception of the plan as evidence that the temple is not a human invention but a revealed institution.

The Christian trajectory inherits two threads. First, the typological reading of Solomon as a Christ-figure, mediated through Hebrews 4:8–11, where Joshua's failure to give true rest opens the way for an eternal Solomon — a son of David who builds a true house. The "Be strong and courageous" charge, the temple plans received from heaven, and the public divine election all become Christological types in patristic and medieval exegesis. Second, the explicit citation of Exodus 25:40 ("See that you make them after the pattern shown you on the mountain") in Hebrews 8:5, applied to Christ's heavenly priesthood, draws the *tavnit* tradition forward: the earthly sanctuary is a copy and shadow of a heavenly reality, and Christ ministers in the original. 1 Chronicles 28 is the bridge between the Mosaic *tavnit* and the Hebrews-letter Christology, even though Hebrews quotes Exodus rather than Chronicles directly.

## Lens 4: The Theological Intent

The Chronicler's agenda in this chapter is to present Solomon's accession as a public, divinely-elected, unambiguously legitimate succession, and to anchor the temple project in a heavenly pattern that David transmits in writing. Every Samuel-Kings ambiguity is resolved upward into theological clarity. That clarity is itself the chapter's most interesting theological feature, because it prompts a series of questions the Chronicler will not let his reader avoid.

**The first tension** is between the Chronicler's idealized public election of Solomon and the messy political reality preserved in 1 Kings 1. Both texts are canonical scripture. They cannot be harmonized at the level of historical event without violence to one or the other. The reader has to ask: what does the Chronicler's reframing *mean*?

**Position 1: Devotional harmonization.** A long tradition of pre-critical exegesis — Jerome, the medieval glossators, much pre-modern Jewish and Christian commentary — treats the two accounts as supplementary rather than competing. 1 Kings 1 records the inner palace events; 1 Chronicles 28 records a subsequent public ratification. On this reading, both happened. The Chronicler simply foregrounds the public moment because his theological purpose is institutional, not biographical.

**Position 2: Theological-historiographical reframing.** The mainstream historical-critical reading — Williamson, Japhet, Knoppers, McKenzie, Roddy Braun — argues that the Chronicler is doing something more deliberate. Writing for a post-exilic Yehud community whose temple is a Persian-protected institution, the Chronicler needs Solomon's accession to be theologically clean. So he constructs a parallel narrative in which Solomon's election is public, divine, and uncontested. This is not deception. It is theological historiography: the Chronicler is asking what the succession *means* and dramatizing the meaning, even at the cost of narrative fidelity to Samuel-Kings. The two accounts are doing different work for different communities.

**Position 3: Canonical-conversation reading.** Brevard Childs, in his canonical approach, argues that the church and synagogue receive both texts and are meant to read them together. The Chronicler's reframing does not erase 1 Kings 1; it stands beside it. Together they teach something neither text could teach alone: divine election is real (Chronicles), and it works through human politics that are often messy and morally compromised (Kings). To read only Chronicles is to lose the realism. To read only Kings is to lose the theological depth. The canonical shape demands both.

**The second tension** is the *tavnit*-in-writing claim of verse 19. David says he received the temple plan "in writing from the hand of the LORD." This is a strong claim about written revelation that goes well beyond what the Mosaic tradition typically asserts. Even the tabernacle pattern in Exodus 25 is *shown* to Moses, not given to him in writing.

**Position 1.** Traditional Jewish and Christian interpretation has read verse 19 as parallel to the tablets of the law — a unique moment of direct divine writing, suitable to the unique status of the central sanctuary.

**Position 2.** Williamson and Japhet read it as the Chronicler's polemical move against any later attempt to revise the temple's design. By rooting the *tavnit* in a written document delivered from God to David, the Chronicler is making the temple immune to architectural innovation. The post-exilic community rebuilding its second temple needs the original plan to be heaven-given, not improvisable.

**Position 3.** Mark Boda reads it as participating in the Hebrew Bible's broader theology of *written* revelation: Moses' law, the prophetic books, the wisdom traditions, all stabilize religious life by reducing it to text. The Chronicler is extending this principle to architecture itself.

**Steelman of Position 3 on the first tension.** The strongest case for the canonical-conversation reading is that the Chronicler clearly knows Samuel-Kings. He is not writing in ignorance. He has read the messy account and chosen to write a different one. That is not a corruption of memory; it is an act of interpretation. To insist on choosing one account as "what really happened" is to flatten the canon's own dialogical structure. The Chronicler is in conversation with Samuel-Kings, and the reader's job is to listen to both voices.

**Where this leaves us.** The Chronicler is showing his reader a succession that *should* have been clean — a public divine election, a written heavenly plan, a son ready to be father-and-sonned by Yahweh, an Israel assembled to witness the transfer. He is not pretending the actual transfer was that clean. He is asking what the transfer was *for*. And the answer is: a temple, built according to a heavenly pattern, by a chosen son, in a kingdom that belongs to Yahweh, on a throne that is Yahweh's. Every layer of that vision is making a claim against the post-exilic community's smaller and shabbier reality, and against any Christian reader's smaller and shabbier ecclesial life.

**Closing question.** When you transmit something important to the next person — a body of work, a household, a tradition, a charge — what do you do with the parts of your own story that disqualify you, the *blood on your hands* that David names openly in verse 3? Do you suppress them, or do you name them so the successor can build on cleaner ground? The Chronicler has David do the second. Most of us do the first.

## The Thread

David, on his feet before all Israel, hands Solomon a written heavenly plan and the Joshua-charge. The Chronicler will not let the succession be a palace whisper; he stages it as a public election under God. The blood-stained founder names his disqualification, transmits the pattern, and steps aside.
