# Leviticus 2: Bread on the Altar

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

Leviticus is known in Hebrew as *Wayyiqrāʾ* (וַיִּקְרָא, "and he called"), named after its opening word in 1:1. The book continues directly from the close of Exodus: the tabernacle has just been completed (Ex 40), the cloud has descended, and now the narrative pivots from construction to function. YHWH calls Moses from the newly pitched tent and instructs Israel how to approach a holy God. Chapter 2, the grain offering (*minḥâ*), sits within the opening worshipper-manual (Lev 1-5) before the priest-manual that follows in chapters 6-7.

The narrative setting is Sinai, c. thirteenth-century BCE on traditional chronology. The critical consensus since Wellhausen's *Prolegomena* (1878) has placed the P source (Priestly) and its sacrificial legislation in the post-exilic period, understanding Leviticus as a retrojection of Second Temple priestly practice onto the Mosaic past. Yehezkel Kaufmann (*The Religion of Israel*) and Menahem Haran (*Temples and Temple-Service in Ancient Israel*) mounted the major counter-case, arguing for pre-exilic composition on linguistic and institutional grounds. Jacob Milgrom's three-volume *Anchor Bible Leviticus* (1991-2000) — definitive in the field — argued the core of P is demonstrably pre-exilic, noting its ignorance of features central to Second Temple practice (Levitical singers, no Sabbath sin-offering, no Yom Kippur liturgy of confession). Israel Knohl's *Sanctuary of Silence* (1995) introduced the now-standard distinction between P proper and the Holiness School (H, Lev 17-26), arguing H is a later priestly reform that reframes the older ritual legislation ethically.

The material culture of Lev 2 is agrarian Bronze- and Iron-Age Levantine. Fine flour (*sōlet*, סֹלֶת) denotes wheat finely milled and sifted, a premium product; ordinary households used coarse meal (*qemaḥ*). *Sōlet* appears in Ugaritic economic texts as a temple commodity. Olive oil (*šemen*) and frankincense (*lĕbōnâ*, לְבוֹנָה) complete the offering. Frankincense was not local; it came from *Boswellia sacra* groves in the Hadramawt and Dhofar regions of South Arabia, reaching the Levant via the Arabian incense road. The presence of frankincense in a sacrificial code presupposes long-distance trade networks operative by at least the tenth century BCE, with carbonized frankincense now attested at Iron II Tel Arad.

Three grain-offering forms are specified: raw flour (vv. 1-3), oven-baked unleavened loaves or wafers (vv. 4-10), and griddle or pan-cooked (vv. 5-7). A separate firstfruits grain offering (*rēʾšît*, רֵאשִׁית) of fresh roasted ears closes the chapter (vv. 14-16). Each receives a token *ʾazkārâ* ("memorial portion") — a handful — burned on the altar, with the remainder going to Aaron's sons as "most holy" priestly food. This is a casuistic priestly torah, graded to a worshipper's economic reach: Milgrom argues persuasively that *minḥâ* functions as a substitute for those who cannot afford animal offerings (cf. Lev 5:11, where a poor man's sin offering becomes flour).

Three prohibitions shape the theology: no leaven (*ḥāmēṣ*), no honey (*dĕbaš*), and required salt (*melaḥ*). The first two are striking because they cut against the grain of ANE practice. Mesopotamian temple offerings regularly included both leavened bread and date-honey; the Ugaritic sacrificial tariff KTU 1.39 specifies *lḥm* (bread) without a leaven restriction. The Punic Marseille tariff (CIS I 165, c. fourth-century BCE Carthage) lists grain and fruit offerings without such exclusions. Israel's code is deliberately contrastive. Salt, by contrast, is shared across the region: the "covenant of salt" (*bĕrît melaḥ*, Num 18:19; 2 Chr 13:5) reflects ANE covenant-meal practice, where sharing salt bound parties irrevocably. Hittite treaties and Arab tribal custom both preserve the idea.

Key terms to carry:

- *Minḥâ* (מִנְחָה) — tribute, gift-offering. Used in Gen 32:13 of Jacob's gift to Esau and 1 Kgs 4:21 of vassal tribute. In priestly register it narrows to grain offering.
- *ʾAzkārâ* (אַזְכָּרָה) — memorial portion, from the root *zkr* (remember). The token makes the whole offering representative.
- *Sōlet* (סֹלֶת) — fine semolina flour, a luxury.
- *Rêaḥ nîḥōaḥ* (רֵיחַ נִיחֹחַ) — "pleasing aroma," the stock refrain threading Lev 1-3.
- *Bĕrît melaḥ* (בְּרִית מֶלַח) — covenant of salt.

Manuscript evidence for Lev 2 is strong. 4QLev-Levb (4Q24) preserves portions of the chapter with only minor orthographic variation from MT. The LXX renders *minḥâ* as *thysia* or *dōron*, and *ʾazkārâ* as *mnēmosynon* — the lexical choice Paul inherits in Eph 5:2 and which Origen will exploit allegorically. The Samaritan Pentateuch and Peshitta align closely with MT.

## Lens 2 — The Literature

The genre is priestly ritual jurisprudence in casuistic form — Milgrom's "casuistic torah." Conditional protases (*kî* / *ʾim*, "when / if anyone brings") introduce each case, followed by prescriptive apodoses. The chapter is not narrative; it is a procedural manual.

Literary architecture is shaped by graded options. The three preparation methods (raw, oven, griddle/pan) are economically descending: raw flour requires no fuel, oven baking requires more fuel and equipment than griddle cooking, and so on — though here the grading is horizontal rather than vertical, offering multiple equivalent paths to the same ritual outcome. The same democratizing logic governs the whole worshipper-manual: wealthy Israelites bring bulls; poorer households lambs, goats, or pigeons; the poorest, flour. Sacrifice is carefully scaled so that access to the sanctuary is not a function of wealth.

Two refrains structure the passage. The phrase *ʾiššēh rêaḥ nîḥōaḥ laYHWH* ("a food offering with a pleasing aroma to the LORD") recurs at vv. 2, 9, and 16 — binding the grain offering to the animal offerings of Lev 1 and 3, where it also appears. The second refrain, *qōdeš qodāšîm* ("most holy," vv. 3, 10), marks the priestly portion as belonging to the highest grade of sanctity, to be eaten only by male priests in a holy place (elaborated in 6:14-18). The "most holy" designation distinguishes *minḥâ* from peace offerings, whose meat is "holy" but shared more broadly.

Repetition with variation is characteristic. The prescription "he shall pour oil on it and put frankincense on it" recurs three times (vv. 1, 15, and by implication in v. 4), with subtle procedural differences. Mary Douglas in *Leviticus as Literature* (1999) reads this repetition as a deliberate ring-composition technique, though not all accept her structural proposals for the book as a whole.

Two editorial layers are visible. Verses 1-10 treat prepared offerings; vv. 11-13 give the leaven/honey/salt rules as a general principle; v. 12 flags firstfruits as an exception (they may be brought as firstfruits but not burned on the altar); vv. 14-16 return to a specific case — firstfruits that ARE burned, with *qālî* (roasted ears) and *geres* (crushed new grain). Milgrom treats vv. 14-16 as a supplementary unit that resolves an ambiguity in v. 12, and Wenham (NICOT) agrees that the chapter shows editorial layering without inconsistency.

Textual variants are minor. 4QLevb preserves most of the chapter; the LXX occasionally expands, e.g. rendering the terse "food offering" (*ʾiššeh*) as *thysia kyriou* ("sacrifice of the Lord"). The Samaritan Pentateuch reads *laYHWH* for MT's *yhwh* at the opening of v. 1 with no theological consequence.

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

**Jewish Interpretive Trajectory.** In Second Temple literature, *minḥâ* receives sustained attention. Philo in *Special Laws* 1.271-275 reads the grain offering symbolically: fine flour as the purified, well-sifted soul, oil as the illuminating power of wisdom, frankincense as the prayer that ascends. Josephus (*Antiquities* 3.233-236) gives a procedural account close to the biblical text, adding details about quantities in Second Temple practice. The Temple Scroll (11Q19) at Qumran expands *minḥâ* regulations considerably, particularly around festival grain offerings. Jubilees 6:14 and 7:5 embed grain offerings in the Noah narrative, connecting them to covenant renewal.

Rabbinic interpretation is encyclopedic. Mishnah Menachot — the dedicated tractate — works through every *minḥâ* variety, the precise amount of flour (*ʿissarôn*, roughly 2.3 liters per offering), the volumes of oil and frankincense, and the *qemîṣâ* (priest's handful) that constitutes the *ʾazkārâ*. The Bavli on Menachot 110a preserves the famous dictum "whoever studies the laws of the sin offering is as if he offered a sin offering, and whoever studies the laws of the guilt offering is as if he offered a guilt offering" — a rabbinic response to the destruction of the Temple that relocates the efficacy of sacrifice into textual study. The Sifra, the halakhic midrash on Leviticus, provides a verse-by-verse expansion, deriving from the smallest terminological choice in Lev 2 a raft of procedural distinctions.

Rashi (on 2:1) asks why, uniquely for the *minḥâ*, the text says "a person" (*nefeš*) rather than "a man" (*ʾiš*) — and answers, citing the Talmud, that the *minḥâ* is typically the offering of the poor, and therefore the text uses the language of soul: "God regards it as though he had offered his very soul." Ibn Ezra focuses on the natural-philosophy of the three ingredients. Ramban (Nachmanides) reads *minḥâ* as a gift of lower status to higher, emphasizing that the Hebrew root in non-priestly contexts always means a tribute-gift (Gen 43:11, Judg 3:15). Maimonides, in *Guide of the Perplexed* 3.32, gives the concessive-pedagogy reading — God permitted sacrifice as an accommodation to Israel's ANE religious imagination and redirected it toward himself — and in *Hilchot Maaseh HaKorbanot* 12-14 codifies *minḥâ* practice for the restored Temple. Bahya ben Asher and Sforno add mystical and moral readings respectively. In modern Jewish commentary, Nahum Sarna (JPS Exodus-Leviticus tradition), Baruch Levine (*JPS Leviticus*), Nehama Leibowitz (*Studies in Vayikra*), and Jonathan Sacks (*Covenant and Conversation: Leviticus*) all emphasize the democratizing logic — Sacks in particular reads the grain offering as Leviticus's quiet argument that nearness to God is not a commodity purchased by the wealthy.

**Christian Interpretive Trajectory.** Origen devoted one of his sixteen *Homilies on Leviticus* to the grain offering (Hom. Lev. 13), and his allegorical reading set the pattern for patristic and much medieval exegesis. Fine flour becomes the purified soul; the absence of leaven signifies the absence of malice (echoing Paul's use of leaven in 1 Cor 5:7-8); the oil is the Spirit's anointing; the frankincense is prayer (cf. Rev 5:8). Cyril of Alexandria in *Glaphyra on Leviticus* extends the typology to Christ, reading the grain offering as a figure of the incarnate body. Augustine, though not a sustained commentator on Leviticus, touches the grain offering at *City of God* 10.5-6, where he develops his theology of the true sacrifice as the soul's devotion. Chrysostom and Theodoret follow similar allegorical lines.

Medieval interpretation inherited Origen. Aquinas, *Summa Theologiae* I-II q. 102 a. 3 ad 8-10, treats the grain offering as a figure of Christ's humanity — bread being proper to human food — and as anticipating the Eucharistic species. The Glossa Ordinaria compiles the patristic readings; Hugh of St. Victor in *De Sacramentis* draws the Eucharistic connection explicitly.

Reformation exegesis shifted register. Luther, typically impatient with allegory, nonetheless finds in Lev 2 a straightforward picture of thanksgiving worship dependent on God's provision. Calvin's *Commentary on the Last Four Books of Moses* (1563) treats Lev 2 at length, insisting that the grain offering teaches that "all the benefits which we receive proceed from God's fatherly liberality" — every meal is sanctified only through offering it back. Bullinger's *Decades* make similar use. The Reformed tradition generally resists Eucharistic-sacrificial extensions.

Critical scholarship since Wellhausen has placed Lev 2 within P's reconstructed post-exilic priestly system; de Vaux's *Studies in Old Testament Sacrifice* (1964), von Rad's *Old Testament Theology*, Childs, Milgrom, and Knohl constitute the modern critical spine. Among contemporary theological commentators, Ephraim Radner's Brazos volume (*Leviticus*, 2008) reads the grain offering Christologically through a figural-Thomist lens; L. Michael Morales (*Who Shall Ascend the Mountain of the Lord?*, 2015) places Lev 2 in the cultic-ascent narrative of Pentateuchal theology; Wenham (NICOT), Sklar (NIVAC), and Hartley (WBC) work the historical-grammatical middle.

**Where they converge and diverge.** Both traditions recognize that *minḥâ* is the offering of the ordinary Israelite and that it carries a theology of thanksgiving and dependence. Both traditions read it as speaking of soul-offering, not merely food. The divergence lies downstream. For Jewish interpretation the grain offering is suspended with the Temple, transmuted into Torah study (Menachot 110a) and liturgical prayer (Berakhot 32b, "prayer is greater than sacrifices"), awaiting eschatological restoration. For Christian interpretation the *minḥâ* is either typologically fulfilled in Christ's body (Eucharistic readings, Catholic/Orthodox) or pedagogically superseded by thanksgiving and Christian liturgy (Reformed readings). Both traditions tell the story of an offering that ends and is transfigured; they disagree on what the new form is.

## Lens 4 — The Theological Intent

**Tension 1: Why is the grain offering burned if God does not eat?** The text says the priest burns the *ʾazkārâ* as "a food offering with a pleasing aroma to the LORD" (vv. 2, 9, 16) — the same refrain that attends the burnt and peace offerings. But Israelite theology insists God is not fed; Ps 50:12-13 mocks the idea. So why the anthropomorphic register?

The Jewish critical reading, pressed by Milgrom and Levine (Anchor Bible / JPS), is that *rêaḥ nîḥōaḥ* is a frozen ANE idiom, inherited from a wider ANE feeding-the-god theology and retained for its ritual weight even as Israelite theology repudiates its literal content. Compare the Atra-hasis flood epic, where the gods "gather like flies over the sacrifice." Israel keeps the aroma; it drops the feeding. Maimonides (*Guide* 3.32) develops this concessively: anthropomorphic sacrificial language is a pedagogical accommodation. Rashi (on 1:9) reads *nîḥōaḥ* etymologically as *naḥat rûaḥ* — "satisfaction of spirit" — not literal nourishment. Nachmanides presses further: the aroma signifies God's acceptance of the worshipper's consecrated intention.

A Catholic reading through Ratzinger (*Spirit of the Liturgy*) construes the burning as the offerer's surrender embodied — ascending smoke as the soul's desire rising. Aquinas (ST I-II q. 102) relates the burning to "the consumption of the offerer's self in the fire of divine love." Orthodox theology through Schmemann (*For the Life of the World*) reads sacrificial consumption as the transfiguration of creation — bread offered, taken up, and in the Eucharist returned. Reformed theology (Calvin, Owen) reads the burning more austerely: the food language teaches Israel that what is given to God is not useful to God; it is wholly relinquished. Lutheran theology with its *theologia crucis* (Forde, Bayer) similarly insists the burning signifies the creature's nothingness before God.

The steelman is Mary Douglas's reading in *Leviticus as Literature*: the "pleasing aroma" is not a theological claim about God's metabolism but a ritual-aesthetic category — sacrifice is beautiful before God; beauty, not consumption, is the point. The Israelite does not feed God; the Israelite makes something beautiful and loses it. Douglas's anthropology here is disciplined and not often enough engaged.

**Where this leaves us:** the grain offering's "food" language is not Israel slipping back into ANE religion. It is a deliberately appropriated idiom whose literal content has been emptied, preserved because the ritual grammar requires it. God receives the offering; God does not eat it.

**Tension 2: Why is leaven banned but salt required, when both were common ANE ritual materials?** This is not a trivial dietary rule; it is a deliberate cultural differentiation. Mesopotamian temple offerings used leavened bread and date-honey liberally; Israel's code excludes both from the altar.

The Jewish halakhic reading (Sifra, Rashi, Ramban) treats leaven and honey as symbols of fermentation and decay — both substances alter over time, and the altar must receive what is untainted. Ramban adds that leaven swells unnaturally, honey putrefies in heat; God receives what is stable. Milgrom's anthropological reading is functional: leaven and honey are fermentable; frankincense and salt are preservative. The altar's chemistry is the theology. Mary Douglas reads the exclusions as part of Leviticus's broader structural logic of wholeness and boundary — leaven blurs; salt defines.

A Catholic reading (Ratzinger, and the Latin unleavened-host tradition) connects the exclusion of leaven to Christ's sinless body, with 1 Cor 5:7-8 (Paul on leaven) as the NT warrant. The Orthodox tradition, which uses leavened bread in the Eucharist, responds that the prohibition was typological-ritual, now fulfilled: the risen Christ is the living leaven. Reformed theology (Calvin, and the Westminster tradition on the regulative principle) reads the prohibitions as sheerly positive law — binding because commanded, not because of intrinsic symbolism — and resists over-allegorizing. Wesleyan-Arminian readings (Wesley, Adam Clarke) tend to blend: leaven as corruption, salt as preservation and covenant fidelity.

The steelman, rarely voiced, is the ANE-contrast argument developed by Levine and Haran: the bans are anti-Canaanite, not metaphysical. Ugaritic and Phoenician cults used fermented offerings; Israel's code was deliberately contrastive to inscribe a different religious identity. On this reading the prohibition has little to do with Christ or with decay; it is boundary-work. This is less intuitive because it treats ritual rules as sociological rather than theological, but it fits the ANE evidence well.

**Where this leaves us:** the leaven-ban and salt-requirement are over-determined. The prohibitions bear real theological freight in Jewish and Christian reception; they also functioned as cultural markers distinguishing Israelite cult from its neighbors. Both readings can be true at once. The text invites reflection on decay and preservation without requiring one reading to exclude the others.

**Unresolved question:** if *minḥâ* is the offering of the poor — the one accessible without flocks — why does the text never foreground that democratizing logic, leaving it to be inferred from the graded economy of Lev 1-5 as a whole? The silence on poverty in a chapter about the poor person's offering is one of the stranger pastoral features of Leviticus.

## The Thread

Three non-obvious cross-references worth following:

- **Numbers 5:15** — the "jealousy offering" for a suspected adulteress is a *minḥâ* WITHOUT oil or frankincense, inverting the Lev 2 prescription. The same form, ritually defaced, marks an offering of suspicion rather than devotion. The inversion is deliberate and rewards close reading.
- **1 Kings 18:29** — the time of the *minḥâ* (afternoon offering) is the narrative pivot of Elijah on Carmel. The daily grain offering is the clock by which Israel's cult keeps time, and the Deuteronomistic historian uses that time to frame confrontation with Baal.
- **Malachi 1:11** — "in every place incense is offered to my name, and a pure *minḥâ*" — the post-exilic prophet projects the grain offering universally, a vision that Christian tradition (Didache 14, Justin's *Dialogue with Trypho* 41, Irenaeus *Adv. Haer.* 4.17) will seize on as a prophecy of the Eucharist and that Jewish tradition will read as the eschatological inclusion of the nations in Israel's worship.
