# John 20: The Empty Tomb, Mary Magdalene, and the Confession of Thomas

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

John 20 begins on Sunday morning at a rock-cut tomb in a garden adjacent to Golgotha. Tomb architecture in early-first-century Jerusalem is well documented: rock-cut chamber with one or more *kokhim* (loculus shafts) or *arcosolia* (arched benches), a small forecourt, and a closure stone. Two closure types are attested. Most common is the disk-shaped stopper plug; rarer and reserved for high-status burials is the rolling stone running in a stone groove. The Tombs of the Kings (Helena of Adiabene's complex) and the Herod family tomb west of the Old City both feature rolling stones. The Synoptics describe the Lukan rolling stone explicitly; John says only that the stone was "taken away" (*ērmenon*, perfect passive participle of *airō*, 20:1). The Holy Sepulchre rotunda preserves what tradition identifies as the *aedicula* enclosing the burial bench; the surrounding bedrock excavated in the twentieth-century campaigns under Charles Coüasnon and Virgilio Corbo confirms the area as a first-century quarry-and-tomb complex outside the second wall.

The garden setting (*kēpos*, κῆπος) at 20:15 matches what we know of upper-class burial grounds in Jerusalem. Joseph of Arimathea's family plot at the foot of Golgotha — a quarry edge converted into a private burial garden — fits the topography of the area beneath the Holy Sepulchre.

The first witness is Mary Magdalene (*Maria hē Magdalēnē*, Μαρία ἡ Μαγδαληνή). The epithet places her hometown at Magdala (Aramaic *Migdal Nunya*, "Tower of Fish") on the western shore of the Sea of Galilee. The 2009 excavation of Magdala Park exposed a remarkably preserved first-century synagogue, fishing harbor installations, and a *miqveh* complex — giving us the first documented look at a town where Mary actually lived and Jesus likely visited. Migdal's role as a fish-processing center supplied the salted fish trade that reached as far as Rome (the *taricheiai* of Magdala); the town's wealth shows in its decorated stone synagogue and well-built domestic quarters. Mary, in other words, came from a substantial Galilean town with active synagogue life — the social profile fits Luke 8:2–3, where she is named among women of independent means who supported Jesus's mission.

The locked-room scene (20:19, 20:26) takes place "where the disciples were" — likely a private house in Jerusalem, possibly the same upper room of John 13. The phrase "for fear of the Jews" should be read carefully: in Johannine usage, *Ioudaioi* in this context names the Jerusalem authorities, not Jewish people generally; Reinhartz's discipline applies. Eight days later (20:26) is Sunday-to-Sunday inclusive reckoning, the second appearance falling on the next first-day-of-the-week — the seedbed of Christian Sunday gathering.

Greek terms: *Maria*, *Rabbouni* (Ραββουνι, Aramaic with first-person possessive suffix preserved into Greek transliteration; "my master/teacher"); *mē mou haptou* (μή μου ἅπτου, "do not cling to me," 20:17 — the Vulgate's *noli me tangere*); *enephysēsen* (ἐνεφύσησεν, "he breathed into," 20:22, the same verb used in LXX Genesis 2:7 of God breathing into Adam); *ho kyrios mou kai ho theos mou* (ὁ κύριός μου καὶ ὁ θεός μου, "my Lord and my God," 20:28).

## Lens 2 — The Literature

John 20 is constructed as a series of paired recognitions. Mary at the tomb (20:1–18) is paired with the disciples in the locked room (20:19–23), and Thomas's absence-then-presence (20:24–29) inverts Mary's scene structurally. The whole chapter ends with a purpose statement (20:30–31) that reads as the original ending of the Gospel.

**Mary Magdalene alone at the tomb** (20:1–10) is a Johannine variant of a tradition the Synoptics carry. Mark 16, Matthew 28, and Luke 24 all have groups of women; John alone has Mary by herself at first. Brown reads this as Johannine literary compression of a tradition that did include other women (note the plural *oidamen*, "we do not know," at 20:2 — Mary's first-person plural verb implies the presence of at least one other woman, even though John names only her).

**The race to the tomb** (20:3–10) is uniquely Johannine. Peter and the Beloved Disciple run together; the Beloved Disciple outruns Peter; arrives first; does not enter; Peter enters first; the Beloved Disciple enters and "saw and believed." The narrative carefully distinguishes their roles: Peter as the institutional first; the Beloved Disciple as the first to faith. Bauckham, in *Jesus and the Eyewitnesses* and *The Testimony of the Beloved Disciple*, builds his case for the Beloved Disciple as the implicit author-witness on this kind of detail — the Beloved Disciple sees with eyes that have already understood.

**Mary Magdalene's encounter with the risen Jesus** (20:11–18) is uniquely Johannine — Matthew has a brief encounter with multiple women on the road, but the extended one-on-one Magdalene scene belongs to John alone. The recognition unfolds in stages: she does not know him; she mistakes him for the gardener; she only recognizes him when he speaks her name; then she answers in Aramaic — *Rabbouni*. Sandra Schneiders, in *Written That You May Believe*, reads the scene as a deliberate echo of Genesis 2–3: a garden, a man and a woman, a recognition, the new creation begun. The misidentification of Jesus as the gardener becomes a Johannine pun — he is, in a sense, the new gardener of the new Eden. The "do not cling to me" (*mē mou haptou*) at 20:17 is one of the most-debated lines in the Gospel: Brown, Köstenberger, and Carson read it as a corrective to a particular kind of tactile grasping (the present imperative *mē + present subjunctive* nuance: "stop clinging" rather than "do not touch"); the encounter with Thomas at 20:27 makes clear that touch is not categorically forbidden.

**Mary as apostle to the apostles**: 20:17–18 commissions Mary to go to "my brothers" with the message of the ascending Christ. Hippolytus of Rome (third century) and later medieval tradition gave her the title *apostola apostolorum*. Pope Francis in 2016 raised her June 22 (now July 22) commemoration from a memorial to a feast in the Roman calendar, with the title *apostola apostolorum* explicitly cited. Karen King's *The Gospel of Mary of Magdala* and Jane Schaberg's *The Resurrection of Mary Magdalene* trace the reception history; Schneiders's work in NTL is the major Catholic feminist reading.

**The locked-room appearance** (20:19–23) is uniquely Johannine in detail. Jesus's "Peace be with you" (*eirēnē hymin*) is repeated three times in the chapter (20:19, 20:21, 20:26), recalling Jesus's promise of peace at 14:27 ("Peace I leave with you, my peace I give to you"). The breathing — *enephysēsen* — is the same verb LXX uses for God's breathing into Adam (Genesis 2:7) and for the breath into the dry bones (Ezekiel 37:9). This is John's Pentecost — the giving of the Spirit on Easter evening, in clear tension with Luke-Acts's fifty-days-later schema. The major theological conclusion: John has compressed Easter and Pentecost into one resurrection event; Spirit-giving is part of the resurrection itself. The "If you forgive the sins of any, they are forgiven; if you withhold forgiveness from any, it is withheld" (20:23) is the binding-and-loosing logion in Johannine form.

**Doubting Thomas** (20:24–29) is uniquely Johannine. Thomas's name — *Thōmas ho legomenos Didymos*, "the Twin" — appears at 11:16 (where he proposes going with Jesus to Bethany "to die with him") and 14:5 ("Lord, we do not know where you are going"). The portrait builds across the Gospel: Thomas is the courageous-yet-uncomprehending disciple. His demand for tactile evidence is met head-on; Jesus invites the touch he had previously withheld from Mary. The scene is structured to climax at the confession.

**"My Lord and my God!"** (*ho kyrios mou kai ho theos mou*, 20:28) is the single most exalted Christological confession in the New Testament. The Greek nominative-with-article construction is unmistakably vocative direct address. Bauckham's *Jesus and the God of Israel* and Larry Hurtado's *Lord Jesus Christ* both treat this verse as the inclusio with the prologue — *kai theos ēn ho logos* (1:1) and *ho theos mou* (20:28). Boyarin's *Jewish Gospels* reads it within the Jewish tradition of high-Christology that he traces in Daniel 7 and the Parables of Enoch.

**Purpose statement** (20:30–31) reads as an original ending. The textual variant *pisteuēte* (present subjunctive, "may continue believing" — Sinaiticus, Vaticanus) versus *pisteusēte* (aorist subjunctive, "may come to believe" — Alexandrinus, the Byzantine tradition) — has been pressed to argue whether the Gospel is evangelistic (*pisteusēte*, written for unbelievers) or pastoral (*pisteuēte*, written for believers needing reinforcement). Both readings have strong textual support; Brown leans pastoral, Carson leans evangelistic. The chapter 21 epilogue, considered widely to be a later addition, will add a second ending at 21:24–25.

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

John 20 is the chapter where the new creation begins. The first day of the week — *tē mia tōn sabbatōn* (20:1) — is the eighth day, the first day of a new world. The garden setting recalls Eden; the recognition by name recalls God calling Adam in the garden; the breathing of the Spirit recalls Genesis 2:7 and Ezekiel 37; the locked doors and peace-greeting recall the world before the Fall, where the human heart was not afraid.

N.T. Wright's *Resurrection of the Son of God* (2003) is the foundational modern study. Wright's argument runs in two stages. First, the historical case: the empty tomb plus the appearances together constitute a sufficient explanation for the rise of Christian belief, and no naturalistic alternative (cognitive dissonance, mass hallucination, deliberate fraud, mythological development) accounts for the data. Second, the theological case: bodily resurrection is the launch of new creation, the inauguration of God's promised eschatological reality within history. Dale Allison's *Resurrecting Jesus* and *The Resurrection of Jesus: Apologetics, Polemics, History* take a more cautious historical position — the empty tomb is more probable than not, but the appearances admit of more interpretive ambiguity than Wright allows. Both books should be read together; they frame the contemporary critical conversation.

Markus Bockmuehl's *Simon Peter in Scripture and Memory* sets the Petrine portrait in 20:1–10 within the broader tradition of Peter; the Beloved Disciple/Peter narrative interplay belongs to a recurring Johannine pattern.

Mary Magdalene reception history is its own field. The Western conflation with Luke 7's anonymous "sinful woman" — codified by Pope Gregory I in his Homily 33 (591 CE) — gave us the medieval penitent-prostitute Magdalene of Mary Magdalene's basilica at Vézelay, the Magdalene of Caravaggio and Donatello, and the Magdalene of *The Da Vinci Code*. The Eastern church never made the conflation; for Eastern Orthodoxy Mary has always been *Myrrhophore* (myrrh-bearer) and *Isapostolos* (equal-to-the-apostles). The 1969 Roman calendar reform separated the Magdalene from Luke 7's anonymous woman; the 2016 promotion to feast cemented the shift. Schneiders, King, Schaberg, and Schüssler Fiorenza have all written extensively on the recovery of Mary's apostolic role.

Patristic reception of John 20: Origen's *Commentary on John* engages the Magdalene scene allegorically as the soul finding the risen Logos. Augustine's *Tractates* 121 reads the breathing of the Spirit as the foundation of the church's authority to forgive sins. Chrysostom's Homilies 86–87 develop Thomas's doubt as a pastoral case study — God permits the doubt for our sake. Cyril of Alexandria reads the *theos mou* of 20:28 as the climactic affirmation of Christ's full divinity. Aquinas's *Lectura* lays out the question of how Thomas could see and yet require touching, and answers with a careful distinction about the modes of human cognition.

Reformation reception: Calvin's *Commentary on John* on 20:23 forcefully rejects the priestly-absolution reading of binding-and-loosing — the church declares forgiveness, the priest does not effect it. Luther reads Thomas's confession as the model of evangelical faith.

Twentieth-century theology: Karl Barth's *Church Dogmatics* IV/2 §64 ("The Exaltation of the Son of Man") works centrally with John 20. Pannenberg's *Jesus — God and Man* makes the resurrection the historical-eschatological foundation of all theology. Moltmann's *Theology of Hope* and *The Way of Jesus Christ* both turn on the resurrection; the *eirēnē hymin* of 20:19 is the gift of the new creation. Catholic resurrection theology after Vatican II — Rahner, von Balthasar — works the same texts.

Major commentaries: Brown (Anchor Bible), Barrett (CGTC), Bultmann, Köstenberger (BECNT), Carson (PNTC), Keener, Beasley-Murray (WBC), Schnackenburg, Marianne Meye Thompson (NTL), Moloney (Sacra Pagina), Brant (Paideia), O'Day (NIB), Newbigin. For Mary Magdalene specifically: Sandra Schneiders, *Written That You May Believe*; Karen King, *The Gospel of Mary of Magdala*; Jane Schaberg, *The Resurrection of Mary Magdalene*. For the resurrection: N.T. Wright, *Resurrection of the Son of God*; Dale Allison, *Resurrecting Jesus*. For Peter: Bockmuehl, *Simon Peter in Scripture and Memory*. For eyewitness: Bauckham.

## Lens 4 — The Theological Intent

**Tension 1: What does *enephysēsen* and "Receive the Holy Spirit" (20:22) do, given Acts 2's Pentecost fifty days later?** Position one: harmonization through stages. Calvin and many Reformed readers: the Easter breathing is a partial or initial gift; Pentecost is the public empowerment for mission. Position two: literary independence. Brown, Bultmann, and many critical readers: John has compressed the entire post-resurrection gift of the Spirit into the resurrection event itself; the Lukan Pentecost is a different theological telling and the two should not be harmonized. Position three: sacramental ordination reading. Catholic tradition has often read 20:22–23 as the institution of the apostolic office and the sacrament of reconciliation, distinct from the public Pentecost gift to the church. Steelman the literary-independence reading: John's Gospel has no separate Pentecost narrative because, on the Johannine schema, Easter *is* Pentecost — the cross-resurrection-ascension-Spirit are one paschal event, not four chronological stages. **Where this leaves us:** the harmonization position makes sense pastorally but underplays what John is doing theologically. The Johannine compression is intentional; the Spirit is given as *the* fruit of the resurrection. This does not require denying the Lukan Pentecost; it requires letting John's schema breathe alongside Luke's rather than collapsing one into the other.

**Tension 2: Is 20:23 ("if you forgive the sins of any, they are forgiven") the institution of priestly absolution?** Position one: Catholic sacramental reading. *CCC* 1441–1445 reads 20:23 alongside Matthew 16 and Matthew 18 as the Scriptural basis for the sacrament of reconciliation; Trent's Session 14 (1551) made this dogma. Position two: Reformed proclamatory reading. Calvin: the apostles are given the authority to *announce* the forgiveness or non-forgiveness that God has already accomplished; the church's word is declarative, not constitutive. Position three: communal-discipline reading. Many modern commentaries: the saying is about the communal life of the believing community — discerning who belongs and who does not, parallel to Matthew 18's church-discipline language. Steelman the sacramental reading: the perfect-passive forms in the Greek (*aphēōntai*, "they have been forgiven") do strongly imply that an actual transaction has occurred when the church speaks; Calvin's strictly declarative reading may underplay the force of the verb forms. **Where this leaves us:** the sacramental and proclamatory readings are not as far apart as Reformation polemics made them; both agree that what the church does in this saying *matters* eternally. The disagreement is about *how* the church's word is related to God's prior action — does the word effect the forgiveness, declare it, or some sacramental conjunction of both. The Johannine context favors the relational-communal reading — this is the Spirit-empowered community speaking its risen Lord's forgiveness — without ruling out either of the older confessional readings.

One unresolved question: what does "do not cling to me, for I have not yet ascended to my Father" (20:17) actually mean, given that Thomas is invited to touch the very same body eight days later? Brown lists at least seven proposals — physical-resurrection ambiguity, ascension chronology, Mary's emotional grasping, Eucharistic typology, theological "stages of glorification" — and concludes that no proposal cleanly accounts for the data. The question remains genuinely open.

## The Thread

John 20 picks up the Genesis-Eden strand running through 1:1–18 (the prologue's *en archē*, "in the beginning") and 19:41 (the burial garden) — the new creation begins where the old creation began, in a garden, with a man and a woman, with breath given for life. The chapter picks up the *egō eimi* and Christological inclusio of 1:1 — *kai theos ēn ho logos* — and lands it at *ho theos mou* (20:28), bracketing the entire Gospel between the Word's divinity and the disciple's confession of it. The peace-greeting (*eirēnē hymin*) lands the promise of 14:27 in the body of the risen Christ. Flag forward: chapter 21, widely held to be a later epilogue, will pick up the Peter strand — the institutional first who comes second to the tomb — and bring Peter's threefold restoration to balance his threefold denial; whether that closes a gap John 20 leaves open or adds something the original ending did not need is a question this Gospel asks each reader to answer.
