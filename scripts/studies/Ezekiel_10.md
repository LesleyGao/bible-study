# Ezekiel 10: The Chariot Returns and the Glory Departs

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

**Political situation**: **Exilic period, dated to 592 BC** within the unified vision of chapters 8–11 (8:1: *the sixth year, the sixth month, on the fifth day* = September 17, 592). Ezekiel is in Tel-abib by the Chebar canal in the south-Babylonian Nippur region, with the elders of the exilic community before him; the visionary frame transports him to the Jerusalem temple. Chapter 10 occupies the architectural-theological center of the chapters-8–11 cycle: the *kavod*'s removal from the inner sanctuary to the threshold (begun in 9:3) advances here from the threshold to the cherubim, then from the cherubim to the east gate (10:18–19), and the destruction-by-fire is symbolically inaugurated by the man-in-linen scattering coals from between the cherubim over the city (10:2). The actual physical destruction of Jerusalem is six years away (586 BC); chapter 10 is the *theological cause* preceding the historical catastrophe — the divine departure makes the city's destruction not just possible but inevitable. **Greenberg (Anchor)** stresses that for Ezekiel, the temple's *fall* in 586 is *consequent on* the *kavod*'s prior departure; the human Babylonian army merely registers, in the world of effects, what has already happened in the world of cause. **Block (NICOT)** elaborates: the chapter is "the most theologically devastating moment in the Old Testament" — the divine self-eviction from the sanctuary that the entire Sinai-Tabernacle-Temple narrative had built toward.

**Material culture**: The temple architecture is presupposed throughout — the inner court (10:3), the threshold of the house (10:4, 18), the south side of the house (10:3), the east gate of the house of the LORD (10:19). The vocabulary matches what we know of the late-monarchic temple plan from 1 Kings 6, the parallel descriptions in Mishnah Middot (which preserves older traditions even in describing the Second Temple), and recent reconstructive work by **Hurowitz**, **Bloch-Smith**, and **Stager**. The cherubim themselves explicitly recall the **Mesopotamian *karibu* / *šēdu* / *lamassu*** — the winged composite-creature guardians attested at **Khorsabad** (Sargon II's palace, 8th c. BC), **Nineveh** (Sennacherib and Ashurbanipal palaces), and **Nimrud** (Ashurnasirpal II's palace). The Akkadian *kāribu* (from *karābu*, "to bless, intercede") is a near-identical cognate of the Hebrew *kerub* (כְּרוּב); both denote a winged composite intercessor-attendant of the divine throne. **Greenberg, Sommer (*The Bodies of God*), and Mettinger (*The Dethronement of Sabaoth*)** all draw the parallel explicitly. The **Khorsabad and Nineveh palace gates** had pairs of *lamassu* — bull or lion bodies, eagle wings, human faces — bracketing the entrance, exactly the iconographic schema of the temple cherubim atop the ark and of the four-faced cherubim of Ezekiel's chariot. The *whirling wheels* (*galgal*, גַּלְגַּל, 10:13) — whose *rims and spokes* are *full of eyes all around* (10:12) — have no exact ANE parallel; the eye-multiplied wheels are Ezekiel's own visionary innovation, picked up later by 1 Enoch and Revelation 4. The *burning coals from between the cherubim* (10:2, 6–7) recall the **Mesopotamian incense-and-coal cult-furnace** practice and the Levitical incense ritual of Lev 16:12 — the man-in-linen taking coals from before the divine throne is performing a *priestly* gesture even as he prepares the city for destruction.

**Authorship & dating**: Ezekiel ben Buzi, priest of the Zadokite line. **Greenberg (Anchor)** treats chapters 8–11 as a single unified vision-cycle authentically Ezekielian, dated to 592 BC. **Block (NICOT)** concurs. **Zimmerli (Hermeneia)** identifies a redactional layer in chapter 10 specifically — the explicit *cherub*-identification of 10:15, 20 he reads as a secondary clarification, prompted by the original chapter-1 vision's failure to name the four creatures. **Cooke (ICC 1936)**, **Eichrodt (OTL)**, and **Odell (Smyth & Helwys)** accept Ezekielian provenance with some redactional smoothing. The chapter has long been recognized as densely re-elaborating chapter 1 — about half its content is a retrospective re-description of the chariot-throne, now identified explicitly as cherubic.

**Archaeological & textual evidence**: **The Ishtar Gate, Esagila temple, and Etemenanki ziggurat** in Babylon — visible to the exilic community via captives and travelers — formed the visual horizon against which Ezekiel's chariot-vision deploys: a YHWH-throne *more mobile* and *more glorious* than the great Marduk-temple of the imperial capital. **Khorsabad and Nineveh *karibu* / *lamassu*** — visible in the Mosul-region ruins of 6th-c.-BC Mesopotamia — supply the sculpted iconography of the divine-attendant. **The Murashu and Al-Yahudu tablets** (Pearce & Wunsch 2014) anchor the exilic-Judean community in the documentary record. **4QEzek-a** preserves chapter 10 in fragmentary form; the LXX shows a slightly shorter text in places (notably 10:14 — the cherub-face is sometimes omitted in Greek manuscripts), but the chapter's theological structure is stable across textual witnesses.

**Key terms**: *kavod YHWH* (כְּבוֹד יְהוָה, "glory of the LORD" — the chapter's central word, used 5× in eleven verses; the *kavod* moves three times: from cherub to threshold (10:4), from threshold over the cherubim (10:18), and from over the cherubim out to the east gate (10:19)); *kerub* / *kerubim* (כְּרוּב / כְּרוּבִים, "cherub/cherubim" — explicitly identified with the *living creatures* of chapter 1 at 10:15 and 10:20: *I knew that they were cherubim*); *galgal* (גַּלְגַּל, "wheel" or *whirling thing*, 10:2, 6, 13 — the same root as the chariot's wheels); *ofan* (אוֹפַן, "wheel," 10:9 — paired with *galgal*; the *wheel-within-a-wheel* construction at 10:10); *terashish* (תַּרְשִׁישׁ, "beryl" or "chrysolite," 10:9 — the same gemstone-language as 1:16 and Dan 10:6); *anaḥ* (the absent-but-implied verb of *human-likeness* — the cherubim have *human hands under their wings*, 10:8, 21 — preserving the same hedging that ran through chapter 1).

## Lens 2 — The Literature

Genre: **prophetic vision-report with embedded ritual-symbolic action**, the third movement of the chapters-8–11 cycle. The chapter has a notable double structure: (a) the framing narrative — the *kavod*'s movement from cherub to threshold (10:4) and out to the east gate (10:18–19) — and (b) the embedded retrospective re-description of the chariot-throne itself (10:8–17, 20–22), explicitly cross-referencing chapter 1.

The literary architecture is:

- A. Throne above the cherubim; man-in-linen commissioned to take coals (10:1–2)
- B. Cherubim and cloud; *kavod* goes from cherub to threshold (10:3–4)
- C. Coals taken from between the cherubim (10:5–7)
- D. Re-description of the chariot: hands under wings, wheels, eyes (10:8–13)
- E. Faces of the cherubim (10:14)
- F. Ascent of the cherubim — *they were the living creatures from the Chebar* (10:15)
- D'. Re-description: wheels and spirit (10:16–17)
- A'. *Kavod* leaves threshold, stands over cherubim, ascends to east gate (10:18–19)
- E'. Identification: *I knew that they were cherubim* (10:20–22)

The chapter's central literary movement is the *retrospective identification*: chapter 1 had described *living creatures* (*ḥayyot*, חַיּוֹת) without naming them as cherubim. Chapter 10 closes the loop: *these were the living creatures that I saw underneath the God of Israel by the Chebar canal; and I knew that they were cherubim* (10:20). Greenberg reads this as the prophet's own learning curve — the visionary's understanding deepens between the inaugural call and the temple-departure vision. Zimmerli reads it as a redactional clarification. Either way, the literary effect is double-anchoring: the temple cherubim atop the ark, the cherubim of Ezekiel 1, and the cherubim of the temple-departure are now established as *the same*, and the *kavod*'s mobility is established as a property continuous with the temple's static cherub-iconography.

The **leitwort** technique is concentrated: *kavod* (5×), *cherub*/*cherubim* (15× in eleven verses — densest concentration in any biblical chapter), *wheels* (10×), *eyes* (3×), *fire* / *coals* (4×). The *eyes-on-the-wheels* repetition (10:12) is structurally the chapter's most striking visual datum: an all-seeing throne whose *very mobility* is many-eyed. The hedging language of chapter 1 — *demut*, *mar'eh*, *ke-* — is lighter here, because the chapter is identifying rather than first-disclosing.

**MT/LXX variants**: 10:14 lists four faces — *the face of the cherub*, *a human face*, *the face of a lion*, *the face of an eagle*. Chapter 1 had listed *human, lion, ox, eagle*. The *cherub* face here, in place of the *ox* face of chapter 1, is one of the chapter's textual puzzles. The LXX of 10:14 is shorter and omits the four-face list entirely in some manuscripts, leading **Cooke (ICC)** to argue the verse is secondary. **Greenberg (Anchor)** defends the MT reading and offers two solutions: either (a) the *cherub* face is itself bovine in iconographic shape (the Mesopotamian *kāribu* often has a bull body and human face) and the verse is consistent with chapter 1, or (b) the visionary perspective has shifted, and the formerly-ox face is now seen as *cherub* face because Ezekiel now has the explicit *cherub* identification. **Block (NICOT)** prefers the latter. Either way, the variant is theologically minor — what matters is that the *living creatures* of chapter 1 and the *cherubim* of chapter 10 are the same beings.

The chapter sits at the heart of **Ma'aseh Merkavah** (the rabbinic *work of the chariot* tradition), and the **Mishnah Hagigah 2:1 / Bavli Hagigah 11b–14a Pardes prohibitions** apply with full force to the public exposition of chapter 10 alongside chapter 1.

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

**Place in the canonical arc**: Ezekiel 10 is the central pivot of the *kavod*-departure arc. The *kavod*'s journey out of the temple is reported in three stages — from cherub to threshold (9:3, 10:4), from threshold over the cherubim (10:18), and from over the cherubim out to the east gate (10:19); the fourth and final stage is the ascent to the mountain east of the city (11:23). Chapter 43 closes the arc with the *kavod*'s return to the new temple from the east, completing the longest theological-architectural movement in Scripture.

**Jewish trajectory**: **Sirach 49:8** singles Ezekiel out for *the vision of glory* (*hazon kavod*) by the 2nd c. BC. **Mishnah Hagigah 2:1** prohibits the public exposition of *Ma'aseh Merkavah*; the **Bavli Hagigah 11b–14a Pardes story** (four entered the orchard; only Akiva exited unharmed) is the rabbinic frame for the chapter's interpretive danger. The **Bavli Megillah 29a** preserves the most theologically generous Jewish reading of the *kavod*'s departure: *in every place to which Israel was exiled, the Shekinah went with them*. This is a counter-reading to the surface text: where Ezekiel 10 narrates the *kavod departing the temple*, the rabbinic tradition reads the same text as *the Shekinah accompanying Israel into exile*. The reading has profound theological weight — exile is not abandonment, the divine presence is in transit *with* the people, the temple is not the only locus of divine accessibility. **Targum Jonathan** moderates the anthropomorphism by reading the *kavod*'s movement as *the glory of the Shekinah* moving. **Rashi** reads the chapter's identifications philologically and refuses speculation. **Radak** elaborates on the chariot's mechanical detail with characteristic philological care. **Maimonides** (*Guide* 3.1–7) integrates Ezekiel 10 with chapter 1 in his Aristotelian decoding: the cherubim are the separate intelligences, the wheels are the celestial spheres, the *kavod* is what perfected human contemplation may approach. **Hekhalot literature** (3rd–8th c. AD) — the *Hekhalot Rabbati*, *Hekhalot Zutarti*, *Sefer Hekhalot* / 3 Enoch — imagines mystical journeys through seven concentric heavenly halls toward the chariot-throne, and Ezekiel 10's eye-multiplied wheels are central to that imagery. **Pesikta Rabbati 33** preserves a striking aggadic tradition: the *kavod* did not depart at once but *lingered* over the threshold, the Mount of Olives, and the wilderness, hoping for repentance — *the Shekinah waited three and a half years, calling to Israel, before it departed*. Modern: Greenberg (Anchor); **Levenson, *Theology of the Program of Restoration* (1976)** — the recovery of Ezekiel's priestly-spatial theology, where *kavod*-mobility is a real-divine property; **Sommer, *The Bodies of God* (2009)** — the *fluid divine self* reading where YHWH genuinely travels.

**Christian trajectory**: Patristic — **Origen's *Homilies on Ezekiel*** (homily 2 specifically; the chariot homilies more broadly) reads the *kavod* departing the temple as the prefigurement of the divine glory's transition from Israel-temple to the church-as-new-temple. **Jerome's Commentary on Ezekiel** (414, written under the shock of the sack of Rome in 410) draws the explicit historical analogy: the *kavod* departed Jerusalem before the Babylonian destruction, just as the divine *gloria* departs Rome before its sack — and just as Christ wept over Jerusalem and prophesied its second destruction (Luke 19:41–44). **Gregory the Great's *Homilies on Ezekiel*** (twenty-two homilies, the most influential medieval treatment) reads the cherubim as the orders of preaching ministry and the chariot's mobility as the church's missional reach. **Pseudo-Dionysius's *Celestial Hierarchy*** (5th–6th c.) systematizes the *cherubim* as the second of the nine angelic orders — directly above the *thrones*, just below the *seraphim* — anchoring the Catholic and Orthodox angelological tradition. **Irenaeus's tetramorph** (*Adv. Haer.* 3.11.8) — the four faces as the four evangelists — receives further elaboration in Ezekiel 10's explicit cherub-identification. Medieval — Hugh of St. Victor, **Richard of St. Victor's *De Visione Ezechielis*** (12th c.) — extended contemplative reading; Aquinas (*ST* I.108) on the angelic orders. Reformation — **Calvin's *Lectures on Ezekiel* (1565, two volumes, incomplete)** insists on *accommodation*: the *kavod*'s departure is a visual depiction of God's *withdrawing his favor* rather than a literal-spatial transit. Critical — Cooke (ICC), **Zimmerli (Hermeneia)**, Eichrodt (OTL). Contemporary — **Greenberg (Anchor)**, **Block (NICOT)**, **Odell**, Wright, **Lapsley**, Wilson.

**Where they diverge**: the **Christian patristic-typological** reading takes the *kavod*'s departure as Christological prefigurement — the *Shekinah* leaving the Second Temple in 70 AD, paralleling Christ's death-and-departure, anticipating Christ's return to the new temple of his risen body (John 2:21) and to the eschatological New Jerusalem (Rev 21:22 — *I saw no temple in it, for the Lord God Almighty and the Lamb are its temple*). The **Jewish Shekinah-accompanies-into-exile** tradition (Bavli Megillah 29a; Pesikta Rabbati) reads the same text as the divine *traveling-with* Israel — exile is not abandonment, the Shekinah is in transit *with* the exilic community. The **Catholic and Orthodox sacramental** tradition reads the *kavod*'s mobility as preparing the way for the *real presence* of Christ in the Eucharist as the new locus of the Shekinah. The **Reformed Word-and-Sacrament** tradition reads the *kavod*'s mobility as preparing for the new economy in which divine presence is mediated through Word-preached and Sacrament-administered, not bound to a single architectural site. The **critical-historical** reading treats the *kavod*'s departure as Ezekiel's theological account of how the temple's destruction is *theologically* possible — YHWH has *already* left, so the Babylonians find an empty house.

## Lens 4 — The Theological Intent

**Tension 1: The *kavod* departing — does YHWH's glory genuinely *travel*, and what theology of *the holy place* survives the departure?** The chapter narrates a literal-spatial transit of the divine throne out of the temple. The theological question is what such transit *is*: real-divine movement, accommodated speech, withdrawal of favor, or relocation of the locus of holy place.

**Catholic and Orthodox sacramental theology** reads the *kavod*'s departure as preparing for the *real presence* of Christ in the Eucharist as the new locus of the Shekinah — the divine glory in transit toward a fuller mode of dwelling (CCC 1373–1381). **Reformed Word-and-Sacrament tradition** reads the same trajectory differently: the *kavod*'s mobility prepares for the new economy in which divine presence is mediated through preached Word and rightly-administered sacraments, *gathered* wherever two or three are gathered (Matt 18:20). **Catholic immutability** (Aquinas *ST* I.9) reads the departure as accommodation: God does not change in being; what *moves* is creation in its relation to God. **Reformed accommodation** (Calvin Institutes 1.13–14) follows similar lines. **Process theology** (Whitehead, Hartshorne, Cobb) takes the wheeled-chariot ontology seriously: God genuinely is *in process*, the *fellow-sufferer who understands*. **Jewish Shekinah-accompanies-into-exile** (Bavli Megillah 29a) inhabits a middle position: the divine presence really does *travel*, takes localized form, separates and rejoins, without compromising the unity of the divine. **Sommer's *The Bodies of God*** systematizes this as the *fluid divine self* of the Hebrew Bible.

The least intuitive position to the confessional Catholic or Reformed reader is **Sommer's fluid-divine-self / Bavli Megillah 29a Shekinah-traveling** reading. The steelman: the canonical text really does describe a wheeled throne climbing into the sky over Jerusalem and a wheeled throne returning over the new temple, and theological abstraction must begin with the data, not over it. If immutability cannot accommodate the chariot leaving and returning, immutability has been imported as a Greek-philosophical premise and the Hebrew text has been silenced. The Shekinah-tradition shows that even within strict monotheism a real divine *traveling-with* is articulable — the Shekinah is not a separate divine being, but a real mode of divine presence that is *mobile*, *localizable*, and *with-Israel*. The Catholic and Reformed traditions have valid concerns about polytheism by the back door, but the Hebrew text resists their abstraction.

Where this leaves us: Ezekiel 10 inaugurates a theology in which the deepest sign of divine sovereignty is not stasis but *pursuit* — the *kavod* will not be confined to one architectural locus, exile is not abandonment, and the question of *the holy place* is reframed from *which building* to *which mode of presence*. The unresolved question: if the *kavod* travels, what is the theological status of *holy ground* — Sinai, Zion, the Temple Mount, the Eucharistic altar, the church gathering? The chapter does not answer; it puts the question on the table.

**Tension 2: The cherubim — what is the theological category of *composite winged creature with four faces*, and what work do they do in the divine economy?** The chapter explicitly identifies the *living creatures* of chapter 1 as cherubim (10:15, 20), tying the chariot-throne directly to the cherubim atop the ark and the cherubim woven into the temple curtains.

**Catholic and Orthodox angelology** systematizes the cherubim as the second of nine angelic orders: **Pseudo-Dionysius's *Celestial Hierarchy*** ranks them below the seraphim and above the thrones; **Aquinas (*ST* I.108)** elaborates the metaphysics. The cherubim are *intellectual creatures* of the highest knowledge-rank, whose office is to bear the divine throne. **Reformed angelology** is more reserved: **Calvin Institutes 1.14** allows that the cherubim are creaturely beings of high rank but warns against speculative ranking. **Jewish *karubim* tradition** runs through the temple cherubim atop the ark and the cherubim woven into the *parokhet* (Ex 26:31) — the etymological link to Akkadian *karābu* (to bless, intercede) preserving the intercessor-office. The **Mesopotamian *karibu* / *šēdu* / *lamassu*** parallels (Khorsabad, Nineveh, Nimrud) supply the visual grammar Ezekiel inherits and subverts: the Mesopotamian throne-attendants belong to Marduk; the cherubim of Ezekiel 10 belong to YHWH, and the YHWH-throne is *more mobile, more glorious, more all-seeing* than anything Esagila can muster.

The least intuitive position to a modern Protestant reader is the **Catholic / Orthodox developed angelology** — the nine ranks, the Dionysian hierarchy, the cherubim as second-rank intellectual creatures. The steelman: the canonical text really does present the cherubim as *non-divine creaturely beings of distinctive office and constitution*, and refusing to systematize is a refusal to think with the data. The Dionysian system may overdetermine, but the underlying intuition — that the canonical text presents *layered* creaturely-supernatural beings with distinct offices — is exegetically defensible.

Where this leaves us: Ezekiel 10 provides the canonical foundation for cherubic ontology — the cherubim are creaturely (not divine), composite (not simple), all-seeing (eye-multiplied), mobile (wheel-borne), and bearer-of-throne (their office is to bear the *kavod*). The unresolved question: if the cherubim are creaturely intercessor-attendants, what is the theological relationship between cherubic mediation (the cherubim bearing the chariot) and the Christological mediation of Hebrews 1 (*the Son ... upholds the universe by the word of his power*)? The chapter does not pose the question, but the canonical trajectory cannot avoid it.

## The Thread

The eye-multiplied wheels and the four living creatures of Ezekiel 10 reappear directly in **Revelation 4:6–8** — *four living creatures, full of eyes in front and behind ... the first creature like a lion, the second creature like an ox, the third creature with the face of a man, and the fourth creature like a flying eagle* — and from Revelation 4 they enter the universal Christian iconographic tradition of the **tetramorph** (the four faces as the four evangelists, **Irenaeus *Adv. Haer.* 3.11.8**). The *kavod*'s departure-and-return arc that begins in chapter 10 is closed in **Ezekiel 43:1–5** (the *kavod* returns from the east) and reframed Christologically in **John 1:14** (*the Word became flesh and tabernacled* — *eskēnōsen* — *among us, and we beheld his glory*) and in **Rev 21:3, 21:22** (*the dwelling place of God is with man ... and I saw no temple in it, for the Lord God Almighty and the Lamb are its temple*). The man clothed in linen who scatters coals over the city (10:2, 6–7) reappears in Daniel 10 and 12 as the heavenly visitor at the Tigris, and in **Revelation 8:5** as the angel who takes fire from the altar and casts it on the earth. The chariot itself becomes the silent referent of the entire **Merkavah-and-Hekhalot mystical tradition** in Judaism and the **Christ-in-Majesty surrounded by the tetramorph** iconographic tradition in Christianity — the same vision routed in two directions.
