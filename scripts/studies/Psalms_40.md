# Psalm 40 — Go Deeper

## Lens 1: The Historical World

Psalm 40 carries the heading *lamnatzeach le-David mizmor* (לַמְנַצֵּחַ לְדָוִד מִזְמוֹר, "to the choirmaster. A psalm of David"). The psalm is one of the more structurally puzzling pieces in the Psalter. Form-critically, Hermann Gunkel and the form-critical school recognized it as a hybrid: vv. 1-10 are an **individual thanksgiving** (*Danklied des Einzelnen*) — the worshipper testifies to past rescue and offers public praise — while vv. 11-17 shift abruptly to **individual lament** (*Klagelied des Einzelnen*) with petition for fresh deliverance. The combination of *Danklied* and *Klagelied* in a single psalm is rare. Westermann (*Praise and Lament in the Psalms*) notes that thanksgiving and lament are formally opposite poles, and their fusion here demands explanation.

The most striking textual fact is that **vv. 13-17 are virtually identical to Psalm 70**. Six and a half verses appear twice in the Psalter, with only minor variations (Ps 40:13's *Yahweh* corresponds to Ps 70:1's *Elohim*, reflecting the Elohistic shift in Book II). This is one of the major **doublet phenomena** in the Psalter (alongside Ps 14 // Ps 53 and Ps 108 // Pss 57:7-11 + 60:5-12). Two readings have dominated. (1) **Composite-redaction reading** (Gunkel, Mowinckel, much critical scholarship): Psalm 40 is an editorial joining of two originally independent psalms — the thanksgiving of vv. 1-10 and the lament of vv. 11-17 (= Ps 70). (2) **Compositional-unity reading** (Hossfeld-Zenger, Goldingay, Wenham, with reservations): the psalm was composed as a deliberate hybrid, the lament-section recycled from or to Ps 70 for liturgical purposes. The doublet phenomenon itself is well-known — Mowinckel argued that liturgical-cultic reuse of psalmic material was normal in Israelite worship.

Dating is contested. The reference to "the scroll of the book" (*megillat sefer*, מְגִלַּת־סֵפֶר, v. 7) suggests a literary culture; the prophetic critique of sacrifice (v. 6) parallels Hos 6:6, Mic 6:6-8, Isa 1:11, Jer 7:21-23, Amos 5:21-24 — a strong prophetic-tradition resonance. Some scholars place Ps 40:6 within the same eighth-to-seventh-century theological milieu; others defend Davidic ascription. The Dead Sea Scrolls preserve fragments of Psalm 40 in 11QPsa and 4QPsa.

The Septuagint reading of v. 6 is the most consequential textual divergence in the entire Psalter for Christian theology. The Hebrew reads *oznayim karita li* (אָזְנַיִם כָּרִיתָ לִּי, "ears you have dug for me" — i.e., opened ears for obedience). The LXX reads *sōma de katērtisō moi* (σῶμα δὲ κατηρτίσω μοι, "but a body you have prepared for me"). The variation has produced enormous theological literature. Possible explanations include: a different Hebrew *Vorlage* before the LXX translator; a deliberate interpretive paraphrase reading "ears" as *pars pro toto* for the whole "body"; or a scribal corruption. **Hebrews 10:5-7 quotes the LXX reading** and applies it Christologically to Christ's incarnational obedience — making this one of the most-discussed examples of the New Testament's use of the LXX. Major studies include Susan Docherty's *The Use of the Old Testament in Hebrews* (2009) and Richard Bauckham's work on the climax of biblical Christology.

Hebrew terms structure the meaning. *Qaweh qiwiti Yahweh* (קַוֹּה קִוִּיתִי יְהוָה, v. 1) — the *infinitive absolute* construction emphasizes intensity: "I waited indeed for YHWH" or "I patiently waited" or "I waited and waited." The verb *qawah* and its noun *tiqvah* ("hope") are central to the wisdom-and-petition vocabulary. *Bor sha'on* (בּוֹר שָׁאוֹן, v. 2, "pit of destruction") combines *bor* (pit, well) with *sha'on* (tumult, roaring) — a vivid evocation of Sheol's chaos. *Tit ha-yawen* (טִיט הַיָּוֵן, v. 2, "miry bog") — the same vocabulary recurs in Jeremiah 38:6 of Jeremiah's actual cistern-imprisonment.

Ancient Near Eastern parallels deepen the imagery. The "pit-rescue" pattern is widespread — Mesopotamian prayers describe deliverance from the Underworld in similar terms. The "new song" (*shir chadash*, v. 3) is a refrain of the broader Psalter (Pss 33:3, 96:1, 98:1, 144:9, 149:1) and signals eschatological-renewal hope.

## Lens 2: The Literature

Robert Lowth's parallelism patterns operate throughout. Verse 2 builds a four-colon climactic parallelism:

- "He drew me up from the pit of destruction,
- out of the miry bog,
- and set my feet upon a rock,
- making my steps secure."

The vertical movement is dramatic: *pit / bog / rock / steps* — from chaos below to ordered standing above. The literary architecture of the verse enacts the theological rescue.

Strophic structure has been analyzed in multiple ways. The most common division is:

- **Strophe 1 (vv. 1-3):** the rescue-testimony — the three-fold action of YHWH (drew up, set, put a new song).
- **Strophe 2 (vv. 4-5):** the wisdom-beatitude and praise — *ashrei ha-gever asher sam Yahweh mivtacho*.
- **Strophe 3 (vv. 6-10):** the obedience-section — sacrifice critique, the scroll, the proclamation in the great congregation.
- **Strophe 4 (vv. 11-12):** the transition — petition and confession of new troubles.
- **Strophe 5 (vv. 13-17):** the urgent lament — petition, imprecation, closing self-description as *ani ve-evyon*.

The transition between vv. 10 and 11 is the structural crux. Verse 10 closes the thanksgiving with an emphatic four-fold confession-vocabulary: *tzidqatekha / emunatekha / teshu'atekha / chasdekha ve-amitekha* ("your righteousness / your faithfulness / your salvation / your steadfast love and your faithfulness"). Verse 11 then pivots: *attah Yahweh lo-tikhla rachamekha mi-meni* ("As for you, O LORD, you will not restrain your mercy from me"). The petition has been prepared by the testimony — the thanksgiving becomes the ground of the lament.

The closing self-description — *va-ani ani ve-evyon* (וַאֲנִי עָנִי וְאֶבְיוֹן, v. 17, "as for me, I am poor and needy") — is the same vocabulary that recurs across the Davidic Psalter (Pss 35:10, 37:14, 70:5, 109:22). The *ani / evyon* pair names the worshipper's social and theological status — humble, dependent, the proper object of YHWH's *hesed*.

There is no *Selah* in Psalm 40, contrary to several other Davidic compositions of similar length — its movement is unbroken, and the structural pivots happen at strophe boundaries rather than at liturgical pauses. Inclusio binds the psalm: the *Yahweh* address opens (v. 1) and closes (v. 17), with the closing *al-te'achar Elohai* ("do not delay, O my God") urgently echoing the opening *qaweh qiwiti Yahweh* — the same waiting, now under fresh pressure.

## Lens 3: The Larger Story

Psalm 40 sits near the end of Book I. Patrick Miller and Gerald Wilson read its placement as transitional — the Davidic individual-laments and thanksgivings of Pss 35-39 culminate in this hybrid before the closing doxology of Ps 41 ends Book I. The hybrid form anticipates the canonical pattern: thanksgiving and lament are not opposites but stages of a single covenantal life.

**Jewish trajectory.** *Midrash Tehillim* on Psalm 40 develops the "new song" of v. 3 as the song of redemption — sometimes connected to the song at the Sea (Exodus 15) and sometimes to the eschatological song of the world-to-come. Rashi reads the pit-and-bog of v. 2 as Egypt and the rock as Sinai; the psalm becomes a typological summary of redemption-history. Ibn Ezra reads v. 6's sacrifice critique as parallel to the prophets — YHWH desires the open ear, not the slaughtered animal. Radak harmonizes the LXX and MT readings of v. 6 with sober textual care, defending the MT *oznayim*. Nachmanides treats v. 6 as a profound theology of Torah-internalization: the law within the heart (v. 8) is the inner counterpart to outer sacrifice.

In modern Jewish scholarship: Nahum Sarna emphasizes the prophetic-priestly tension in v. 6 — sacrifice is not abolished but reordered under obedience. Jon Levenson (*Sinai and Zion*, *Resurrection and the Restoration of Israel*) places the rescue-from-the-pit imagery within Israel's broader theology of salvation from death. Buber, in *Right and Wrong*, attends to the structural arc — past rescue grounds present petition, the testimony of *qaweh qiwiti* becomes the courage to ask again. Heschel cites v. 8 — *la-asot retzonkha Elohai chafatzti ve-toratkha be-tokh me'ai* ("I delight to do your will, O my God; your law is within my heart") — as a paradigm of the prophetic-and-Hasidic interior covenant. Avivah Zornberg's literary readings would attend to the *megillat sefer* (scroll of the book) of v. 7 as a reflexive moment — the speaker recognizing his own life as inscribed.

Liturgically, Psalm 40 features in some Sephardic morning rites, and v. 8 is often quoted in modern Jewish ethics as a touchstone of internalized Torah.

**Christian trajectory.** The Christian reception is dominated by Hebrews 10:5-7's quotation of vv. 6-8. The author of Hebrews places vv. 6-8 (in the LXX form) on Christ's lips at his incarnation: *thysian kai prosphoran ouk ēthelēsas, sōma de katērtisō moi … idou hēkō … tou poiēsai, ho theos, to thelēma sou* ("sacrifice and offering you did not desire, but a body you have prepared for me; behold, I have come … to do your will, O God"). This is one of the most theologically significant Old-Testament-in-the-New uses, applying the psalm's obedience-section directly to Christ's incarnational and sacrificial obedience.

Augustine's *Enarrationes in Psalmos* (Latin Ps 39) reads the entire psalm Christologically: the pit-and-bog rescue is Christ's resurrection from death; the new song is the gospel; v. 7's *megillat sefer* is the Old Testament prophesying Christ. Athanasius recommends the psalm to those rescued from grave trouble. Cassiodorus structures his commentary around the dual *prosopon* — Christ speaks vv. 1-10, the church speaks vv. 11-17.

Aquinas (*Postilla*) treats vv. 6-8 with sustained attention, drawing on Hebrews 10 to develop his Christology of obedience. The *Summa Theologiae* on the satisfaction-theology of the cross (III, q. 48) draws on the same tradition. Bonaventure's *Lignum Vitae* echoes the obedience-language. Luther's *Operationes in Psalmos* on Psalm 40 develops the *coram Deo* theology: the believer's obedience is not the sum of sacrifices but the surrender of the will. Calvin's 1557 commentary engages the LXX/MT divergence honestly, defending the MT reading while acknowledging the legitimate theological direction Hebrews takes.

Among modern critical interpreters: Gunkel and Mowinckel saw the composite character; Westermann's lament-thanksgiving categories illuminate the structure; Kraus, Hossfeld-Zenger, Goldingay, Tate, Wenham (*The Psalter Reclaimed*), Brueggemann (*The Message of the Psalms*), Patrick Miller, William P. Brown (*Seeing the Psalms*), Beth Tanner, and Susan Gillingham (*Psalms Through the Centuries*) all engage the structural and theological complexity. Richard Bauckham's *The Climax of Prophecy* and Susan Docherty's *The Use of the Old Testament in Hebrews* (2009) are essential for Hebrews 10's use of Ps 40.

**NT use.** Hebrews 10:5-10 is the major NT citation. The author quotes Ps 40:6-8 (LXX) and applies it to Christ's incarnation: the *body prepared* is the body of Christ offered once for all. The argument turns on the LXX reading; the Hebrew "ears you have dug for me" would not have served the Hebrews argument as directly. This raises the major hermeneutical question.

## Lens 4: The Theological Intent

**Tension 1: The LXX-Hebrew divergence in v. 6 — what does *sōma katērtisō moi* mean?** This is one of the canonical hermeneutical-theological knots. (a) **LXX-canonical reading (most patristic and medieval Christian, Hebrews):** the LXX is canonical scripture for the New Testament writers; *sōma* is the divinely-inspired clarification of *oznayim*; the Christological application is exegetically legitimate. (b) **MT-priority reading (much modern critical scholarship, some Reformed):** the Hebrew "ears you have dug" is the original; "open ears for obedience" makes excellent sense as prophetic-Torah piety; the LXX is a paraphrase or corruption; Hebrews is doing creative typology rather than strict exegesis. (c) **Both-canonical reading (Bauckham, Docherty, much contemporary biblical theology):** the LXX is itself a stream of canonical reception; the New Testament writers worked from the LXX as their scripture; what the LXX rendered as *sōma* captures something true about the Hebrew (*pars pro toto* — ears for the whole obedient body). (d) **Sensus plenior reading (Catholic and some evangelical):** the Hebrew text means what it says, and the LXX surfaced a *fuller sense* the Hebrew text bore providentially even before its Christological clarification.

The strongest steelman is the both-canonical reading. The Hebrew *oznayim karita li* — "ears you have dug for me" — already employs *pars pro toto*: the ears stand for the whole obedient body, the body that hears and acts. The LXX *sōma katērtisō moi* makes the implicit explicit. This is not strict word-for-word translation, but it is faithful translation in the sense of disclosing what the Hebrew text bears. The Christian writer of Hebrews then takes this LXX form and applies it Christologically — Christ is the body prepared for the perfect doing of YHWH's will, the obedience the sacrificial system anticipated. Whether the Hebrew text *constrains* this application is a question of canonical theology: if the LXX is canonical scripture (which is the position of much of Eastern Christianity to this day), the Christological reading is exegetically authorized; if the Hebrew text alone is canonical, the Christological reading is typological extension.

**Tension 2: Sacrifice-critique and the prophetic tradition.** Verse 6 — "in sacrifice and offering you have not delighted" — joins a chorus of prophetic critiques (Hos 6:6, Mic 6:6-8, Isa 1:11, Amos 5:21-24, Jer 7:21-23). (a) **Anti-cultic reading (some Wellhausen-school readings):** the prophets and this psalm reject the sacrificial cult as such, in favor of ethical religion. (b) **Cult-critical-but-not-anti-cultic reading (most modern scholarship):** the texts criticize sacrifice *as a substitute for obedience*, not sacrifice as such; the cult remains valid when integrated with obedience. (c) **Eschatological-fulfillment reading (Hebrews, much Christian tradition):** the sacrifice-system was always provisional, awaiting the *one body* prepared for perfect obedience; Christ fulfills what the cult could only enact in shadow. (d) **Internalization reading (Heschel, modern Jewish ethics):** the verse calls for sacrifice integrated with the open ear, the law within the heart — outer ritual in service of inner alignment.

The strongest steelman is the cult-critical-but-not-anti-cultic reading: Psalm 40 does not abolish sacrifice but subordinates it. The "open ear" of v. 6 and the "law within the heart" of v. 8 specify the *condition* under which sacrifice is acceptable — the obedient and attentive worshipper. The Christian eschatological reading then sees Christ as the one in whom that condition is fulfilled completely. Hebrews 10 is not anti-cultic but cult-fulfilled: the system that pointed forward has reached what it pointed at.

Where this leaves us: Psalm 40 is the prayer of the rescued who needs rescue again. The thanksgiving of vv. 1-10 is real; the lament of vv. 11-17 is also real. The two are not in contradiction. The worshipper *has* been drawn from the pit; he is *also* surrounded by fresh evils. The pattern is not failure — it is the structure of a covenantal life. The psalm refuses the false consolation that yesterday's rescue exempts us from today's danger. It also refuses the despair that says rescue was illusion. The way through is the way the psalm walks: thanksgiving as the ground of fresh petition.

**Closing question:** When Hebrews 10 puts the LXX form of Ps 40:6-8 on the lips of Christ at the incarnation, what does that imply about the relationship between the Old Testament's own voice and the New Testament's reception of it — and how do we honor both as scripture without collapsing either?

## The Thread

- **Psalm 1:** the *ashrei* opening that Ps 40:4 echoes (*ashrei ha-gever asher sam Yahweh mivtacho*); the wisdom-beatitude embedded in thanksgiving.
- **Psalm 70:** the doublet of Ps 40:13-17 — the lament that floats free as its own psalm in Book II.
- **Hosea 6:6 / Micah 6:6-8 / 1 Samuel 15:22:** the prophetic chorus on sacrifice-and-obedience that Ps 40:6 joins.
- **Hebrews 10:5-10:** the canonical-Christological application of Ps 40:6-8 (LXX) to Christ's incarnational obedience.
