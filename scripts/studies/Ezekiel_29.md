# Ezekiel 29: The Great Dragon in His Streams and the Wages of Tyre

## Lens 1 — Historical World

Ezekiel 29 opens the **Egypt cluster** (chs. 29–32) within the larger Oracles Against the Nations (OAN) block of chs. 25–32. The chapter is unusual in that it carries two precisely separated dates that span a decade and a half. The first oracle (29:1) is dated *the tenth year, in the tenth month, on the twelfth day*: by the standard Babylonian-reckoning chronology used in Ezekiel and defended in the Anchor Yale commentary by **Moshe Greenberg**, this is **January 7, 587 BC**, in the months when the Babylonian siege of Jerusalem was already underway and Pharaoh Hophra's army had just made its abortive intervention (cf. Jer 37:5–11). The second oracle (29:17) is dated *the twenty-seventh year, in the first month, on the first day*, which corresponds to **April 26, 571 BC** — **the latest dated oracle in the entire book of Ezekiel**, written some sixteen years after Jerusalem's fall. **Daniel Block** (NICOT) treats this dating asymmetry as theologically deliberate: Ezekiel preserves a prophecy from the desperate days of 587 BC and then, sixteen years later, returns to amend its outcome.

The Egyptian context is the late Twenty-Sixth (Saite) Dynasty. **Pharaoh Hophra** — Greek *Apries*, Egyptian *Wahibre Haaibre* — ruled from 589 to 570 BC. He is the pharaoh of Jer 44:30 and the unnamed pharaoh of Ezek 29 in its first oracle. Herodotus (*Histories* 2.161–169) preserves the tradition that Hophra was overthrown in a civil war by his general **Amasis II** (*Ahmose II*, 570–526 BC) following a disastrous Egyptian intervention against the Greek colony of Cyrene around 570 BC; the stelae of Amasis preserved at the Cairo Museum (notably the so-called **Elephantine Stela**) corroborate the broad outline. Standard Egyptological treatments — **Anthony Spalinger's** monograph on Late Period Egypt, **Karol Mysliwiec's** *The Twilight of Ancient Egypt*, and **Alan Lloyd's** commentary on Herodotus Book II — set the stage for Ezekiel's references. Saite-Dynasty material remains are abundant: Sais (Tell el-Fara'in), Memphis (the *Hwt-ka-Ptah* sanctuary), and the *Apis* bull cult that flourished during the period; **Tahpanhes** (Tel Defenneh, identified by **Flinders Petrie** in his 1886 excavations and described in *Tanis II: Nebesheh and Defenneh*) was the eastern Delta fortress where Jeremiah was forcibly taken (Jer 43:7) and where, by his testimony, Nebuchadnezzar's tent would be pitched.

The 29:17–20 oracle responds to a striking historical fact: **Nebuchadnezzar's thirteen-year siege of Tyre (586–573 BC) ended without the comprehensive plunder Ezekiel had earlier predicted** (cf. ch. 26). Josephus (*Against Apion* 1.21) preserves the Tyrian king-list from **Menander of Ephesus** that brackets the siege. Tyre evidently negotiated a settlement; the city remained on its island fortress, its wealth unplundered, until Alexander's mole in 332 BC. Ezekiel's response is theologically and historically remarkable. Where one might expect rationalization, the prophet receives a fresh oracle that simply re-aims the divine intent: *because they worked for me*, **Egypt** is given to Nebuchadnezzar as wages. The Babylonian invasion of Egypt is attested in the fragmentary cuneiform text **Babylonian Chronicle BM 33041**, dated to Nebuchadnezzar's thirty-seventh regnal year (568/567 BC), which preserves enough of the campaign-list to confirm a Babylonian military action against Egypt during Amasis's early reign — though, as **D. J. Wiseman** notes in *Nebuchadrezzar and Babylon* (Schweich Lectures, 1985), the chronicle is too fragmentary to establish that Egypt was conquered or that its wealth was carried off.

The geographic register of the chapter is precise. *From Migdol to Syene* (29:10) — Migdol, the eastern Delta fortress mentioned alongside Tahpanhes; Syene, the southern frontier town at modern **Aswan**, the ancient *Swenet*, where the **Elephantine papyri** would later document a Judean garrison. *Pathros* (29:14) is Upper Egypt, the *p3-t3-rsy* of hieroglyphic texts. **Cush** is Nubia south of Aswan. **Walther Zimmerli's** Hermeneia commentary, **Greenberg's** Anchor Yale volume, and **Block's** NICOT all treat the chapter's geography as authentic Saite-period administrative knowledge consistent with the Judean diaspora's exposure to Egypt during and after the fall of Jerusalem (cf. Jer 44).

Hebrew vocabulary worth flagging: *tannin* (תַּנִּין, "dragon, sea-monster, serpent") in 29:3 — the term that elsewhere designates the cosmic-sea-creature defeated by YHWH at creation (Isa 27:1; 51:9; Job 7:12; Ps 74:13; Ps 148:7); the divinatory phrasing of *ya'or* (יְאֹר, "the Nile/the canal"), which in Egyptian self-understanding is the divine river-god *Hapi*; and the recognition formula *wəyaḏəʿû kî-ʾănî YHWH* ("and they shall know that I am YHWH"), which closes the chapter's oracular units.

## Lens 2 — Literature

The chapter divides cleanly into three oracles. The first (vv. 1–16) opens the OAN-Egypt cluster with a *tannin* image, a *qina*-tinged judgment, and a forty-year exile-and-return prediction. The second (vv. 17–20) is the **prophecy-amendment** dated 571 BC. The third (v. 21) is a brief eschatological coda (*on that day I will cause a horn to spring up for the house of Israel*) that gestures toward the Davidic *qeren* tradition (cf. Ps 132:17; Lk 1:69).

The dominant literary device is the **theomachy taunt-song** built on the *tannin* trope. Egypt is portrayed as the **great dragon lying in the midst of his streams** (29:3), claiming *the Nile is mine, and I made it for myself*. **Greenberg** notes that the trope draws on the chaoskampf inheritance of the Ancient Near East: the cosmic sea-monster of the *Enuma Elish* (Tiamat, defeated by Marduk), the Ugaritic Yam-and-Lotan tradition (KTU 1.5 — *Litan, the fleeing serpent, the twisting serpent*), and the Egyptian *Apophis*-serpent who threatens Re's solar barque each night. Egypt the empire is depicted in the very imagery the Egyptians used for the **enemies of Re**. The taunt is doubly subversive: it identifies Egypt with chaos rather than order, and it casts YHWH in the Marduk-role, the dragon-slayer who imposes cosmos on chaos. The hook-and-fishing imagery of vv. 4–5 is a deliberate inversion of the Egyptian self-image: Pharaoh boasts of mastering the Nile and its fish, but YHWH will pull *Pharaoh* out of his own river by hooks like a hauled-in crocodile, with the fish (his subjects) sticking to his scales.

The divine self-claim of 29:3 (*the Nile is mine, and I made it for myself*) is the literary mirror of 28:2's Tyrian *I am a god, I sit in the seat of the gods*. **Block** notes that the Egypt-Tyre pairing treats imperial self-divinization as the same theological sin in two cultural registers: maritime-mercantile (Tyre) and riverine-agricultural (Egypt). The forty-year desolation prediction (29:11–13) deliberately echoes the wilderness-generation forty (Num 14:33), now applied in reverse: Egypt itself will undergo a wilderness exile and a *partial* restoration as a *lowly kingdom* (29:14–15), never again the geopolitical reliance that drew Israel away from YHWH.

The second oracle (vv. 17–20) is unusual. **Zimmerli** classifies it as a *Schwertreaktion* — a prophetic re-application — and the literary form is essentially a divine compensation-decree: Nebuchadnezzar worked, did not get paid, and the deity transfers a different revenue source to settle the account. The economic vocabulary is striking: *śāḵār* (wages) and *pəʿullâ* (labor's reward) are wage-and-labor terms. Cooke (ICC) reads the passage as preserving an early prophetic tradition of theological honesty about unfulfilled prediction.

A textual variant: 29:7's *yad* (hand) versus Targum's variant; LXX reads *cheir* and most modern critical editions follow MT. Greenberg discusses the apparent corruption in 29:7's *kol-māṯənāyim hāʿămadtā* (made all loins to stand) versus the more probable *təmaʿḥadətā* (made to shake), defended on the basis of Vulgate and Syriac.

## Lens 3 — Larger Story

In Jewish reception, the *tannin* of 29:3 became a stable trope for imperial pride. Rashi reads the dragon as Pharaoh and the streams as his branches of the Nile; Radak emphasizes the divine self-claim as the supreme blasphemy. Maimonides (*Guide* III.46) treats the chapter as part of the prophetic critique of *idolatrous self-divinization* — the heart of the Torah's prohibition against worshipping anything other than the unmade Maker. Targum Jonathan paraphrases the *tannin* as *malka rabba* (the great king), softening the cosmological imagery into political metaphor.

Christian reception is rich. **Origen's** lost commentary on Ezekiel survives in fragments preserved by Jerome; **Jerome's** own *Commentariorum in Ezechielem libri XIV* (c. 410–414 CE) reads Egypt as a type of the spiritual world that opposes the people of God, with the *tannin* as the diabolical principle figured in Job 41 and ultimately in Revelation 12. **Gregory the Great's** *Moralia in Job* — though structured around Job rather than Ezekiel — develops the *Leviathan/dragon-as-devil* typology that medieval readers carried into Ezekiel. **Calvin's** *Praelectiones in Ezechielem* (lectures delivered 1564, published posthumously 1565) treats the chapter at considerable length: the Egyptian boast *I made the Nile for myself* is, for Calvin, the canonical illustration of the *imago Dei* turned inside-out, the creature claiming creator-status and thereby unmaking itself.

The *cosmic-sea-monster-as-Egypt* tradition has been the focus of major modern scholarship. **John Day's** *God's Conflict with the Dragon and the Sea: Echoes of a Canaanite Myth in the Old Testament* (Cambridge, 1985) is the classic treatment; **Jon Levenson's** *Creation and the Persistence of Evil: The Jewish Drama of Divine Omnipotence* (1988) develops the theological implications. The argument is that the OT preserves, rather than erases, the Ancient Near Eastern chaoskampf inheritance — but it transforms it. Yahweh does not merely *defeat* the dragon at creation; he conscripts the dragon-trope as an ongoing political-theological diagnostic. Pharaoh, Tyre, Babylon, and (in Daniel 7) the four beasts of the sea are all variants of the same cosmic-sea-monster tradition. **Mary K. Wakeman's** *God's Battle with the Monster* (1973) and **Bernard Batto's** *Slaying the Dragon: Mythmaking in the Biblical Tradition* (1992) extend the analysis. The trajectory continues into the New Testament: **Revelation 12** explicitly identifies the dragon as *that ancient serpent*, and Rev 13's beast-from-the-sea inherits the Ezekiel-Daniel imagery for imperial power. **Richard Bauckham's** *The Theology of the Book of Revelation* and *The Climax of Prophecy* trace Ezekiel as the deepest substrate for John's apocalyptic imagination.

The Egyptian-as-dragon tradition was not a Jewish invention. The *Pyramid Texts* utterance 271 and the *Coffin Texts* preserve the ongoing Egyptian battle between Re and *Apophis*, the chaos-serpent of the underworld waters. Ezekiel's literary brilliance is to take an Egyptian image — the cosmic-serpent threatening order — and turn it on Egypt itself: Egypt the would-be Re-figure is, in fact, the Apophis-figure. **Othmar Keel's** *The Symbolism of the Biblical World* (1972, ET 1978) catalogs the iconographic background.

## Lens 4 — Theological Intent

**Tension 1 — Prophecy-amendment and divine contingency.** The 29:17–20 passage is the canonical cluster's most theologically pointed admission: an earlier prediction about Tyre was *not fulfilled in the form predicted*, and a fresh oracle re-aims the outcome. **Block** steelmans the contingency reading by appeal to the Jonah-Nineveh principle (Jonah 3:10 — *and God relented*) and to Jer 18:7–10's explicit declaration that prophetic threats and promises are conditional on response. The most rigorous recent treatment is **Terence Fretheim's** *The Suffering of God* (1984) and *God and World in the Old Testament: A Relational Theology of Creation* (2005), which argues that prophetic prediction is inherently relational and re-aimable — God means what he says at the moment of saying, but the future is not closed. The steelman of the open-prophecy view is this: **the chapter does not present 29:17–20 as a divine error or as a mere clarification; it presents it as a reassignment of the labor-credit, treating Nebuchadnezzar's siege of Tyre as work done for YHWH that requires payment elsewhere when the original payment-source proves unavailable.** The *because they worked for me* (29:20) is contractual and treats the unfulfilled Tyre-plunder not as a defeat of the divine word but as an obligation YHWH has to settle. Rigid inerrantist readings (e.g., **Robert Dick Wilson** in older Princeton treatments, more recently **Walter Kaiser**) insist that the Tyre-prediction was fulfilled in some attenuated form (Nebuchadnezzar did break Tyre's mainland power, even if the island was not plundered), and that 29:17–20 is a coda not a correction. The text itself is more candid than the rigid view allows.

**Tension 2 — The *tannin*-trope and the persistence of mythological imagery.** Ezekiel's casting of Pharaoh as the *great dragon* draws on the chaoskampf tradition that earlier Israelite tradition had nominally subordinated to creation-by-divine-speech (Gen 1). Levenson's argument is that the OT's so-called demythologization is not as complete as **Hermann Gunkel's** older *Schöpfung und Chaos* (1895) framing supposed: the dragon-tradition lives on as a political-theological diagnostic. The unresolved tension for theological readers is whether YHWH's victory over the *tannin* is (a) cosmologically completed at creation, (b) repeatedly enacted in history through imperial judgments, or (c) eschatologically anticipated in a final cosmic-dragon defeat that Rev 20 narrates.

**Unresolved question.** *And it shall never again be the reliance of the house of Israel, recalling their iniquity* (29:16). What is the precise theological function of *humiliated-but-restored* Egypt as a *lowly kingdom* — neither destroyed (as the surrounding judgment-language might suggest) nor restored to glory? The chapter declines to specify whether Egypt's eventual humility is intended as a permanent geopolitical demotion (Greenberg's reading), as a typological pointer toward the *eschatological gathering of the nations* in Isa 19:23–25 where Egypt is *my people*, or as a stable middle-state where Egypt remains theologically present but politically incapable. Block treats this verse as one of Ezekiel's most theologically generous notes about a foreign nation; Calvin reads it more strictly as a curse-reduction.

## The Thread

Four non-obvious cross-references. (1) **Isaiah 19:23–25** — *In that day there will be a highway from Egypt to Assyria… Blessed be Egypt my people*. The most generous OT note about Egypt sits in tension with Ezek 29's judgment, and the Ezek 29:14's *lowly kingdom* may be the exact bridge between the two. (2) **Job 41 and Psalm 74:13–14** — the *Leviathan/tannin* tradition that Ezekiel inherits and Egypt-izes; Day and Levenson read these as the theological substrate of Ezek 29:3. (3) **Jeremiah 44** — the Judean refugees' settlement at Tahpanhes and Migdol, the geographic frame of 29:10. Jeremiah's prophecy of Nebuchadnezzar's invasion (Jer 43:8–13) is the parallel oracle to Ezek 29:17–20 and confirms that the Babylonian-Egyptian campaign was a known prophetic expectation. (4) **Revelation 12:9 and 13:1** — the explicit identification of the dragon as *that ancient serpent* and the rise of the beast from the sea. Whatever else John is doing, he is reading Ezekiel: Ezek 29's *tannin in the streams* and Ezek 32's *dragon in the seas* are the most direct OT antecedents for the dragon-and-beast imagery that organizes Rev 12–13. The forward arc from Ezek 29 to Rev 12 is one of the densest single threads in the canon's apocalyptic imagination.
