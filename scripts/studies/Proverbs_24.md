# Proverbs 24 — Go Deeper

## Lens 1: The Historical World — Two Collections of "Sayings of the Wise" Meet

Proverbs 24 contains a second editorial seam. Verses 1–22 close out the first collection of **Sayings of the Wise** (*divrê chakamim*, דִּבְרֵי חֲכָמִים) that began at 22:17, while v. 23 opens a shorter, distinct collection: *"These also are sayings of the wise"* (*gam-eleh la-chakamim*, גַּם־אֵלֶּה לַחֲכָמִים). The book of Proverbs contains at least seven identifiable sub-collections marked by superscriptions; this is the fifth such marker (after 1:1, 10:1, 22:17, 24:23, with two more to come at 25:1 and 30:1, plus 31:1).

The first collection (22:17–24:22) ends having delivered roughly the **thirty sayings** advertised in 22:20 — Michael V. Fox's Anchor Bible commentary lays out the count, and most modern critical commentators (Murphy, Brown, Yoder, Kynes) agree that the structure intentionally mirrors the thirty chapters of the **Egyptian Instruction of Amenemope**. Pr 24 contains only modest direct Amenemope parallels (especially 24:11 on rescuing those taken to death, which has loose echoes in Egyptian *ma'at* texts on speaking for the silent), but the collection's overall shape is shaped by Egyptian instruction-form throughout.

The second collection (24:23–34) is shorter — only twelve verses, six or seven distinct units — and is sometimes called the **"Appendix to the Sayings of the Wise."** Its style is closer to sentence-proverb than to instructional discourse, suggesting either a different origin or a later editorial supplement gathered before Hezekiah's collection (chs. 25–29) was added.

The chapter closes with a striking literary set-piece: **24:30–34**, the **field of the sluggard** — a first-person narrative observation. *"I passed by the field of a sluggard ('atsel), by the vineyard of a man lacking sense (chasar-lev, חֲסַר־לֵב, 'lacking heart'), and behold, it was all overgrown with thorns…"* The form is unusual in Proverbs: a sage's autobiographical reflection on a literal scene, drawing wisdom from observation. The closing maxim (*"a little sleep, a little slumber, a little folding of the hands to rest…"*) repeats Pr 6:10–11 verbatim — one of the most exact intra-Proverbs cross-references, suggesting deliberate editorial framing of the whole book around the sluggard motif.

**Dating and origin.** The mainstream-critical position (Fox, Murphy, Brown) places the final compilation of these collections in the post-exilic period, with substantial older material absorbed; the conservative-evangelical position (Waltke, Longman) accepts pre-exilic — even Solomonic-era — origin for much of the content, with Hezekiah-era and post-exilic editing. Either way, the chapter is a rich site of **wisdom anthology**: multiple voices, multiple forms, multiple historical layers, edited into a coherent moral vision.

## Lens 2: The Literature — Forms Mixed, Voice Personal

Pr 24 contains an unusual variety of forms within a single chapter:

- **Prohibition + motivation** (24:1–2, 15–16, 17–18, 19–20, 21–22) — the dominant form of the *Sayings of the Wise*.
- **Synthetic praise of wisdom** (24:3–7) — a short hymn-like meditation: *"By wisdom (chokmah, חָכְמָה) a house is built, and by understanding (tevunah, תְּבוּנָה) it is established; by knowledge (da'at, דַּעַת) the rooms are filled with all precious and pleasant riches."* The triad *chokmah / tevunah / da'at* echoes the foundational triad of 1:2–7 and 9:10, and is reprised in the building of YHWH's tabernacle (Ex 31:3, 35:31).
- **Imperative + extended motivation** (24:11–12) — *"Rescue (hatsel, הַצֵּל) those who are being taken away to death… If you say, 'Behold, we did not know this,' does not he who weighs the heart (token libbot, תֹּכֵן לִבּוֹת) perceive it?"* The motivation here invokes divine judgment in the language of Pr 21:2 (the LORD weighs the heart).
- **Honey-and-wisdom emblem** (24:13–14) — *"Eat honey, for it is good… know that wisdom is such to your soul."* A taste-image binding sensory pleasure to wisdom.
- **First-person observation narrative** (24:30–34) — the field of the sluggard.

**Key Hebrew terms:**
- **חָכְמָה / תְּבוּנָה / דַּעַת** (*chokmah / tevunah / da'at*) — 24:3–4. Wisdom / understanding / knowledge. The classical wisdom triad; the same three words used for the artisans of the tabernacle (Ex 31:3) and the temple (1 Kgs 7:14).
- **רֶכֶב מִלְחָמָה** — not in this chapter, but the **counsel-in-war** image of 24:6 (*be-tachbulot ta'aseh-leka milchamah*, בְּתַחְבֻּלוֹת תַּעֲשֶׂה־לְךָ מִלְחָמָה, "by wise guidance you wage your war") becomes 11:14 and 20:18; war as paradigmatic decision-making domain in the wisdom literature.
- **תֹּכֵן לִבּוֹת** (*token libbot*, "weigher of hearts") — 24:12. A divine-name circumlocution; God as moral examiner. Cf. Pr 16:2, 21:2.
- **שֶׁבַע יִפּוֹל צַדִּיק וָקָם** (*sheva' yippol tsaddiq va-qam*, "the righteous falls seven times and rises") — 24:16. Among the most quoted verses in pastoral encouragement; the sevenfold repetition is idiomatic for *complete* falling, with complete rising.
- **שִׂמְחָה / שָׂמַח** (*simchah / samach*, "joy / rejoice") — 24:17. The verb governs the famous prohibition: *al-tismach* (אַל־תִּשְׂמַח), *"do not rejoice"* when your enemy falls.
- **עָצֵל** (*'atsel*, "sluggard") — 24:30. The recurring figure of Proverbs (cf. 6:6, 10:26, 13:4, 19:24, 20:4, 21:25–26, 22:13, 26:13–16). Here observed in the wild rather than described in the abstract.
- **חֲסַר־לֵב** (*chasar-lev*, "lacking heart") — 24:30. The Hebrew idiom for someone who lacks moral and intellectual judgment; *lev* is the will/intellect, not the emotions.
- **גְּדֵר** (*geder*, "wall") — 24:31. A stone field-wall; its breaking-down is a vivid image of long-term neglect, since stone walls do not collapse quickly.

**Parallelism** in the chapter is mostly synthetic (line B advances line A) and synonymous (lines mirror), with the closing observation-narrative breaking parallelism into prose-like verse.

## Lens 3: The Larger Story — Wisdom Canon, Jewish Trajectory, Christian Trajectory

**Within the wisdom canon**, Pr 24 develops several threads:

1. **Wisdom-builds-the-house** (24:3–4) — connects to Pr 9:1 (*"Wisdom has built her house"*), Pr 14:1 (*"the wisest of women builds her house"*), and ultimately the building of Solomon's Temple (1 Kgs 5–7), described using the same vocabulary. The Hebrew wisdom tradition does not separate sacred from secular building — both require *chokmah, tevunah, da'at*.
2. **The righteous falls seven times** (24:16) — taken up in Job's restoration (Job 42:10), Ps 34:19 (*"many are the afflictions of the righteous, but the LORD delivers him out of them all"*), and the prophetic restoration motifs (Mic 7:8: *"when I fall, I shall rise"*).
3. **Rescue those taken to death** (24:11–12) — joins the Torah's *lo ta'amod 'al-dam re'ekha* ("you shall not stand idly by the blood of your neighbor," Lev 19:16) and the prophetic critique of those who "do not stretch out the hand to the needy" (Ezek 16:49).

**Jewish trajectory.** Pr 24:11 (*"rescue those who are being taken away to death"*) became one of the **foundational texts of Jewish ethical obligation to intervene against injustice**. Maimonides (*Mishneh Torah, Hilkhot Rotzeach* 1:14) cites the verse alongside Lev 19:16 as the basis of the duty of rescue. The verse was invoked by Jews in Eastern Europe in the 1930s–40s and continues to anchor Jewish refugee-advocacy and human-rights traditions. **24:17** (*"do not rejoice when your enemy falls"*) is famously the source of the Passover Seder tradition of removing a drop of wine for each plague — diminishing one's joy because the Egyptians, even as enemies, were God's creatures suffering. The Talmud (*Megillah* 10b, *Sanhedrin* 39b) directly cites this verse in the context of God silencing the angels' song at the Red Sea: *"My handiwork is drowning in the sea, and you wish to sing?"* **24:21** (*yer'a et-YHWH beni va-melekh*, "fear the LORD, my son, and the king") shaped Jewish political theology under foreign rule, joining Pr 21:1 in grounding prayer for and obedience to civil authority within the limits of *kiddush ha-shem* (sanctification of the divine name).

**Christian trajectory.** Pr 24:11–12 has become the central biblical text in **Christian pro-life and anti-injustice activism** across the twentieth century — quoted in the abolitionist movement, civil rights movement, and pro-life movement. Reformed leaders (Francis Schaeffer, *A Christian Manifesto*) cited the verse to argue against passivity in the face of legalized injustice. **24:16** (*"the righteous falls seven times and rises"*) is among the most-quoted pastoral verses in Christian counseling — treated as a promise of recovery from failure, addiction, depression. **24:17–18** (*"do not rejoice when your enemy falls"*) is one of the clearest OT anticipations of Jesus's *love-your-enemies* ethic. **24:29** (*"Do not say, 'I will do to him as he has done to me; I will pay the man back for what he has done'"*) explicitly forbids retaliation — a remarkable text for the OT, parallel to Lev 19:18's *"you shall not take vengeance"* and anticipating Rom 12:17–21.

**NT use.** Pr 24:12 is alluded to in Rom 2:6 (*"He will render to each one according to his works"*) and Rev 22:12 — the divine judgment-as-weighing motif. **Pr 24:21** (fear the LORD and the king) is taken up by 1 Pet 2:17 (*"Fear God. Honor the emperor"*). **Pr 24:17–18, 29** undergird Romans 12:17–21 (*"Repay no one evil for evil… Beloved, never avenge yourselves… overcome evil with good"*) — though Paul more directly quotes Pr 25:21–22 in this passage. **Pr 24:30–34** (the sluggard's field) is in the same wisdom stream as Jesus's parables of the unfruitful vineyard (Lk 13:6–9) and 2 Thess 3:10 (*"if anyone is not willing to work, let him not eat"*).

## Lens 4: The Theological Intent — Tensions, Positions, Steelman

Two tensions in Pr 24 deserve honest reckoning.

**Tension 1: The enemy-not-rejoicing wisdom (24:17–18, 29) and Christian non-retaliation ethics.** Pr 24 contains some of the most striking OT anticipations of Jesus's enemy-love teaching. How should we read them?

- *Continuity reading.* Jesus's *love-your-enemies* ethic (Mt 5:43–48) is not novel in the OT but the flowering of an ethic already present in Lev 19:18, Ex 23:4–5 (*"if you meet your enemy's ox or his donkey going astray, you shall bring it back"*), and Pr 24:17–18, 25:21–22. Steelman: the OT's ethic is more developed than supersessionist readings allow; the seeds of *agape* are already here, and Pr 24 is direct evidence.
- *Discontinuity-with-fulfillment reading.* The OT contains both Pr 24:17 (do not rejoice) and the imprecatory Psalms (Ps 137:9, etc.). Jesus brings the enemy-love thread to its center, fulfills the law, and reorders the ethic around the cross. Steelman: the relationship is not flat continuity but progressive revelation; the OT prepares, the NT fulfills.
- *Mainstream-critical reading.* Pr 24:17–18 is a particular wisdom-stream voice, not the whole OT consensus. The book of Esther celebrates enemy-fall (Esth 9), the imprecatory Psalms invoke it. Steelman: the OT is a polyphonic library, and Pr 24 is one important voice that does not capture the whole. Honest reading recognizes the diversity.

**Where this leaves us:** Pr 24:17 does not say enemies are not enemies, or that justice does not require their fall. It says: when they fall, do not rejoice. The motivation (24:18) is theological — *"lest the LORD see it and be displeased, and turn away his anger from him."* Joy at an enemy's fall reveals a heart that wants destruction more than righteousness, and God will not partner with it. The Talmud's reading (silencing the angels at the Red Sea) is the deepest version: God himself does not rejoice at the fall of his enemies, even when justice requires their fall. That is the ethical floor on which Jesus stands when he says *love your enemies* — and Pr 24 is already standing there.

**Tension 2: The industrious-sluggard contrast (24:30–34) and modern work-ethic readings.** The closing observation-narrative is one of the most concrete moral images in the OT — a sage walking past a man's overgrown vineyard and drawing a lesson about laziness. How should we hold it?

- *Traditional Christian work-ethic reading.* The passage grounds the dignity of labor and the moral seriousness of diligence. Calvinist and Puritan traditions especially read 24:30–34 (with 6:6–11) as foundational to the Protestant work ethic. Steelman: Proverbs unambiguously condemns chronic indolence and praises diligence; this is canonical, not contingent.
- *Mainstream-critical genre-honesty reading.* The proverb describes a tendency, not a universal law. The book also acknowledges that the diligent sometimes lose (Pr 13:23, *"the fallow ground of the poor would yield much food, but it is swept away through injustice"*), and the rich sometimes inherit unjustly (cf. Eccl 9:11, *"the race is not to the swift, nor the battle to the strong"*). Steelman: the wisdom literature itself contains a self-correcting mechanism; Pr 24 should not be weaponized against the poor as if every poor person were a sluggard.
- *Liberation / structural-justice reading.* The same wisdom tradition that condemns the sluggard also condemns the oppressor (Pr 22:16, 23:10–11). Industriousness without justice is corrupt; neither can be reduced to the other. Steelman: pairing 24:30–34 with 24:11–12 (rescue those taken to death) gives the canonical balance — personal responsibility *and* structural obligation, not one against the other.

**Where this leaves us:** The field of the sluggard is a real moral image. Some lives are overgrown with thorns because the person folded their hands; many lives are overgrown with thorns because thorns were planted for them by injustice, illness, or circumstance. Wisdom requires distinguishing — both for self (the sluggard's mirror) and for neighbor (refusing to read every thorny field as moral failure). The chapter that contains 24:30–34 also contains 24:11–12; the same sage who watched the sluggard's vineyard knew he was obligated to rescue those being taken to death.

**Closing question:** Whose fall has secretly given you joy this week? And whose vineyard, growing wild beside you, are you walking past as if it were the sluggard's — when it might be the field of someone who was simply taken away to death and has no one to rescue them?
