# Ezekiel 17: The Eagles, the Cedar, and the Theology of the Broken Oath

## Lens 1 — Historical World

The riddle of chapter 17 is among the most precisely datable in the Hebrew Bible. The chapter assumes a known sequence of events: Nebuchadnezzar's first deportation in 597 BC, when Jehoiachin and the Jerusalem aristocracy were carried to Babylon (cf. 2 Kgs 24:10–17); the installation of Zedekiah (originally Mattaniah) as Babylonian client-king under loyalty oath; and the unfolding diplomatic flirtation with Pharaoh Hophra (Apries, r. 589–570 BC) that culminated in the revolt of 588 and the destruction of Jerusalem in 587/586. Ezekiel ben Buzi, a priest deported in the 597 group, delivers the oracle from Tel-abib by the river Chebar (the *nār Kabari*, a major irrigation canal off the Euphrates near Nippur). His audience is the Judahite golah colony — almost certainly the kind of community now documented in cuneiform from the Murashu archive (455–403 BC) and especially the **Al-Yahudu tablets** published by Pearce and Wunsch (*Documents of Judean Exiles and West Semites in Babylonia*, CUSAS 28, 2014). The Al-Yahudu corpus shows Judahite deportees holding land tenancies under the *bīt ritti* royal-grant system and engaging in commerce within a generation of arrival — confirming the Babylonian "land of trade…city of merchants" image of 17:4 as ethnographically accurate, not merely metaphorical.

The decisive interpretive datum for Lens 1 is the **Esarhaddon Vassal Treaty** of 672 BC (Wiseman 1958; SAA 2.6 in Parpola and Watanabe 1988). Its Akkadian formulation of vassal oaths — invoking the suzerain's gods, calling down curses on covenant-breaking, and treating *adê* (loyalty oath) violation as cosmic treason — is the legal substrate behind verses 13–19. When Ezekiel reports that Nebuchadnezzar "made a covenant with him, putting him under oath" (17:13), the Hebrew *bĕrît* and *ʾālâ* render exactly the *adê* / *māmītu* pair familiar from Neo-Assyrian and Neo-Babylonian treaty texts. Moshe Greenberg (Anchor 22A, 1983) underscores that the riddle's force depends on its audience knowing the political reality: the eagle-cedar imagery is not floating allegory but coded reportage that any Babylonian-era Judahite would decode within a verse or two. Daniel Block (NICOT, 1997) extends this by noting that Ezekiel's *ben adam* address (the prophet is "son of man" 93 times in this book) deliberately situates the prophet *under* the imperial transcript: the priest-prophet speaks from inside the empire's documentary culture.

Walther Zimmerli (Hermeneia, 1979) reconstructs the chapter as a primary oracle (vv. 1–10, 22–24) with later prose interpretation (vv. 11–21), though most contemporary scholars (Block, Greenberg, Margaret Odell 2005) defend its essential unity. G. A. Cooke (ICC, 1936) and Walther Eichrodt (OTL, 1970) read 17 as one of the cleanest historical correspondences in the corpus. Charles Cutler Torrey's old hypothesis of a Hellenistic Ezekiel — never widely accepted — collapses entirely under the Al-Yahudu evidence.

The Hebrew terminology is dense. *Kāvôd* does not appear in 17, but the chapter is undergirded by Ezekiel's standing concerns: *gillûlîm* ("dung-pellets," his preferred slur for idols) form the moral background against which oath-treachery is judged, and the recognition formula *wîdaʿtem kî-ʾănî YHWH* ("you shall know that I am the LORD") closes both judgment (v. 21) and salvation (v. 24) sections. The phrase *nātan yād* (17:18, "he gave his hand") is a Northwest-Semitic legal idiom for sworn submission, paralleled in 1 Chronicles 29:24 and Ezra 10:19 — physical gesture sealing the *ʾālâ*.

Jewish medieval reading is sophisticated. Maimonides treats the chapter only obliquely (the *Guide* 3.1–7 discussion of Ezekiel's chariot does not extend here), but Rashi reads the eagles as Nebuchadnezzar and Pharaoh literally, and Radak (David Kimhi) develops the messianic reading of vv. 22–24 with caution: the "tender twig" planted on the high mountain is the Messiah from the Davidic stump, but Radak situates this within his rationalist *peshat* method, refusing the more luxuriant midrashic identifications. Ibn Ezra preserves the political reading and is conspicuously sober about messianic application.

Patristic readers move quickly to typology. Origen (in *Homilies on Ezekiel*, preserved partially in Latin via Jerome) reads the two eagles as world-empires opposing the people of God; the cedar-sprig is unambiguously the incarnate Logos. Jerome's *Commentariorum in Ezechielem* (composed in Bethlehem, c. 410–414, after the sack of Rome) takes verse 22 as the locus classicus for Christ as *ramus tener* — the "tender Branch." Gregory the Great's *Homilies on Ezekiel* (delivered 593–594, also during a political collapse — the Lombard siege of Rome) reads the chapter as ecclesiology: the Church is the noble cedar in whose shade all nations nest. Calvin's *Lectures on Ezekiel* (delivered 1564, published posthumously) is unusual for the Reformation in retaining strong attention to the *political* covenant: Calvin insists that Zedekiah's oath, though sworn to a pagan, was binding *coram Deo* — and that breaking it constituted cosmic perjury, not merely diplomatic miscalculation.

## Lens 2 — Literature

The chapter is structurally a *māšāl* (riddle/parable) followed by a *pēšer* (interpretation). Verse 2 commands: *ḥûd ḥîdâ ûmšōl māšāl* — "propound a riddle, speak a parable" — a doublet that signals to the audience that the form is interpretive theater. The riddle (vv. 3–10) employs deliberately ambiguous referents: two eagles, a cedar, a vine. The interpretation (vv. 11–21) decodes the political referents. The closing oracle (vv. 22–24) re-deploys the cedar imagery in salvation key.

The leitwort technique is unusually tight. *Šĕbûʿâ / ʾālâ / bĕrît* (oath / curse-oath / covenant) cluster in verses 13–19, recurring seven times in seven verses; the repetition is the argument. The verb *māʾas* ("despise") and its derivatives recur (v. 16, 18, 19) to mark Zedekiah's specific guilt: not merely defection but contempt. The recognition formula *wîdaʿtem kî-ʾănî YHWH* punctuates both halves (v. 21, v. 24), binding judgment and promise into one grammatical structure.

The rhetoric of the rhetorical question — *hatiṣlāḥ?* ("Will it thrive?") — appears three times (vv. 9, 10, 15), each driving the audience toward forced verdict. Greenberg notes that the form mirrors a courtroom: the prophet seats his hearers as jurors and asks them to deliver the verdict against their own king. By verse 15 the question turns on Zedekiah personally; the audience that has already condemned the vine cannot now exempt the king.

Allegory in Ezekiel 17 differs from chapters 16 and 23. Where 16 and 23 use sustained pornographic invective, 17 uses *political* cipher: the eagle is a known imperial emblem (cf. the Mesopotamian *anzû* tradition; the Lagash standard with eagle-and-prey iconography), and the cedar is Lebanon's specific imperial tribute species — Nebuchadnezzar's own building inscriptions (Wadi Brisa) boast of cedar-cutting expeditions. Block argues that the riddle would have been politically dangerous to deliver *uncoded* in the Babylonian heartland, and the cipher form gave Ezekiel plausible deniability to imperial authorities while making clear sense to the golah.

The Hagigah restrictions in rabbinic literature (m. Hag. 2:1; b. Hag. 13a–14b) limit public exposition of *maʿăśê merkābâ* (the chariot-vision of Ezek 1) and *maʿăśê bĕrēʾšît* (Genesis 1's account of creation), but Ezekiel 17 is not under those strictures — its political-allegorical surface is rabbinically classed with the prophetic *neviʾim* corpus available for synagogue reading. The fact that 17 is *uncensored* in rabbinic instruction while 1 is restricted suggests the tradition could see clearly that 17's danger was political rather than esoteric.

Textual variants are modest. The MT is well preserved here. The LXX renders the eagle as *aetos megas* and the "land of trade" as *gēn Chanaan* ("Canaan") at v. 4 — a reading that almost certainly derives from a translator's misreading of *kĕnaʿan* as the place-name rather than its older sense of "merchant" (cf. Hos 12:8, Zech 14:21; *kĕnaʿănî* = trader). Greenberg corrects on philological grounds: the Hebrew here means commercial Babylon, not Canaan-the-land. The Targum Jonathan paraphrases more freely, identifying the first eagle explicitly as Nebuchadnezzar.

## Lens 3 — Larger Story

**Jewish reception** treats Ezekiel 17 along two axes: as a precise historical retrospective on the Davidic monarchy's collapse, and as a messianic seedbed for the eschatological Branch. Sirach 49:8 honors Ezekiel as the visionary "who saw the chariot," concentrating Jewish memory on chapters 1 and 10; chapter 17 receives less direct citation in Sirach and the Apocrypha. The rabbinic tradition, however, returns repeatedly to vv. 22–24. Targum Jonathan messianizes the "tender twig" explicitly: "I will bring near a child from the kingdom of the house of David" (Tg. Ezek 17:22). The midrash reads the cedar's restoration as the *malkhut beit David* (Davidic kingdom) restored at the eschaton. Hekhalot literature does not engage 17 directly — its energies are absorbed by Ezekiel 1 — but the messianic-arboreal imagery flows into later kabbalistic readings of the *ʿētz ḥayyîm*, the Tree of Life, with the Davidic Messiah as its trunk.

**Christian reception** universally Christologizes vv. 22–24. Origen, Jerome, and Gregory the Great agree that the "tender twig" is the incarnate Logos, the Davidic Branch (cf. Jer 23:5–6, 33:14–26; Isa 11:1's *nēṣer*; Zech 6:12's *ṣemaḥ*). Augustine cross-reads with the parable of the mustard seed (Mt 13:31–32; the birds nesting in the branches in Ezek 17:23 prefigure the Gentile inclusion in Mt 13). Calvin holds the Christological reading firmly but distinctively grounds it: the cedar's restoration is not the spiritual Church abstracted from history but the literal Davidic line carried forward through judgment and exile to its messianic consummation. Modern critical Christian readers — Greenberg, Block, Zimmerli, Eichrodt — generally agree that vv. 22–24 are messianic in *direction* but disagree about whether the original audience would have heard a single eschatological figure or a generic Davidic restoration.

**Where the readings diverge** is on the eagles and the political theology. Jewish readers (Rashi, Radak, Ibn Ezra) hold the political referent steady: Nebuchadnezzar and Pharaoh are the eagles, and the chapter is about kings violating loyalty oaths. Christian patristic readers (Origen, Jerome) often dilute the political specificity into generic "powers of this world." Critical scholarship (Cooke, Eichrodt, Greenberg, Block) restores the specifically Babylonian-Egyptian political reading and treats it as the *primary* sense, with messianic typology as legitimate but secondary trajectory. The mystical tradition — Hekhalot, kabbalah — largely passes the chapter by, since its imagery is not theophanic. The tetramorph tradition (Ezek 1's four-creatures applied as evangelist symbols by Irenaeus and Jerome) does not extend to chapter 17.

## Lens 4 — Theological Intent

**Tension 1: Does YHWH guarantee oaths sworn to pagan kings?** The chapter's logic is startling. Zedekiah swore loyalty to Nebuchadnezzar. He broke that oath. YHWH treats the broken oath as a personal offense — *my oath that he despised, my covenant that he broke* (17:19). The Hebrew construction collapses two covenants: Zedekiah's *political* covenant with Babylon and his *theological* covenant with YHWH. Breaking the first counts as breaking the second.

This raises the classical question of oath theology. **Aquinas** (*ST* II-II.89) argues that an oath, once sworn — even to a wicked party — binds in foro divino unless the object sworn is itself sin. Catholic just-war and oath tradition follow: pacta sunt servanda; the oath's binding force derives from God's name invoked, not the oath-receiver's character. **Reformed natural-law tradition** (Calvin, Althusius, Vitoria) extends Aquinas: the moral law binds across confessional boundaries; treaties between Christian and non-Christian powers create real obligations. Calvin's *Lectures on Ezekiel* uses 17:18–19 as proof-text for civil-magistrate oath-keeping. **Anabaptist tradition** (Schleitheim Confession Article 7, 1527; Mt 5:33–37 read absolutely) refuses oaths altogether: the Christian's word should not need swearing. From this angle, the chapter's theology is *itself* a critique of oath-culture — Zedekiah should never have sworn at all, but having sworn, he was bound. **Modern political theology** (Karl Barth's *Church Dogmatics* IV/2 on the State; Oliver O'Donovan's *The Desire of the Nations*) treats the chapter as foundational for a theology of *political* fidelity — the prophet's God is the God of treaties, not only of temple-cult.

Steelman the position one finds least congenial. If you tend toward the prophetic critique of empire, steelman the *Aquinas-Calvin* line: the moral order that holds nations to their treaties is the same moral order that constrains imperial overreach; without enforceable oaths, the strong devour the weak more freely, not less. If you tend toward law-and-order political theology, steelman the *Anabaptist* line: oath-culture has historically conscripted God's name into imperial service (the *adê* tradition is exactly that), and the chapter's logic, taken to its conclusion, has YHWH guaranteeing Babylonian hegemony — a reading the Anabaptists rightly resist.

**Tension 2: Is the "tender twig" a messianic prophecy or a historical promise of Davidic restoration?** Verses 22–24 promise that YHWH himself will plant a sprig from the cedar's lofty top on a high mountain, where it will become a noble cedar in whose shade all the birds of every sort will nest. The Christian tradition reads this Christologically — the Davidic Messiah, the Branch (cf. Jer 23, Isa 11, Zech 6). Daniel Boyarin's *The Jewish Gospels: The Story of the Jewish Christ* (2012) argues that Second Temple Jewish readers were already developing a messianic-divine figure from this kind of imagery, and the Christian reading is not an alien imposition but a continuation of one available Jewish reading. Jewish messianic-future tradition — Maimonides' twelfth principle, the *Amidah*'s fourteenth and fifteenth blessings, Targum Jonathan's reading of v. 22 — agrees on a Davidic messiah but locates him in the future, awaiting realization. The critical reading (Greenberg, Block, Zimmerli) reads vv. 22–24 historically: Ezekiel promises that the Davidic line, apparently destroyed in the exile, will be restored through divine action — a promise partially fulfilled in Zerubbabel (Hag 2:23, the "signet ring") and held open eschatologically by the canon.

The five positions:
1. *Christological-realized*: the twig is Christ, and the prophecy is fulfilled in the incarnation (Origen, Jerome, Calvin, classical Reformed).
2. *Christological-already/not-yet*: the twig is Christ, the prophecy is initiated at the incarnation and consummated at the Parousia (modern evangelical and Catholic eschatology, N.T. Wright in *Jesus and the Victory of God*).
3. *Jewish-messianic-future*: the twig is the awaited Davidic Messiah, prophecy unrealized (rabbinic, medieval Jewish, modern Orthodox).
4. *Historical-Davidic*: the twig is the Davidic line generically, restored through Zerubbabel and held open (Greenberg, Block, much of critical scholarship).
5. *Boyarin's Jewish Christ*: the messianic-divine figure is already present in Second Temple Jewish exegesis; Christian and Jewish readings are sibling developments rather than rival systems.

Where this leaves us: the chapter's promise is genuinely irreducible. To collapse vv. 22–24 into pure Christology is to miss its grounding in the historical Davidic crisis; to collapse it into pure historical restoration is to miss its eschatological surplus, which the canon itself preserves by leaving the promise unconsumed by Zerubbabel.

ONE unresolved question: When the chapter binds together oath-keeping among nations and the promise of the Davidic Messiah, is it claiming that the same God who *enforces* imperial treaties is the one who eventually *transcends* the imperial order through a planted sprig? If so, the chapter is doing political theology of an unusually demanding kind — refusing the easy moves of either accommodation or revolt.

## The Thread

Three non-obvious cross-references. First, **Daniel 4** — Nebuchadnezzar's dream of the great tree cut down and re-sprouted, the most direct intertext within the canon. The arboreal imagery, the felling, and the regrowth all overlap; both passages address a Babylonian king's place in YHWH's economy, but from inverse angles (Daniel from inside Nebuchadnezzar's court, Ezekiel from inside the deportee community). Second, **Genesis 22's binding of Isaac**, where the *ʾălê* (going up) of the firstborn is redirected to the ram caught in the thicket — a redirection that prepares the canonical logic by which the cedar's son (Davidic Messiah) is offered up and yet restored, a thread Levenson follows in *The Death and Resurrection of the Beloved Son*. Third, **Hosea 11:10–11** — the lion's roar that calls Israel home from Egypt and Assyria, "trembling like birds" — which deploys the same bird-imagery Ezek 17:23 uses for the Gentile influx into the messianic cedar's shade, suggesting that what looks like Christian re-reading (Mt 13's mustard seed) is recovering an inner-canonical pattern already present in eighth-century prophetic imagination.
