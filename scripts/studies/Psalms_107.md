# Psalm 107 — Go Deeper

## Lens 1: The Historical World

Psalm 107 stands at a hinge in the Psalter. It opens **Book V** (Pss 107-150), the final and longest of the Psalter's five books, and in Wilson's canonical-shape reading it does so deliberately: Book V is the post-Davidic-failure restoration response. Book III ended in catastrophe — Ps 89's anguished question of where the Davidic covenant has gone. Book IV answered that "the LORD reigns" even when no Davidic king does (Pss 90-106, anchored by the Mosaic enthronement psalms 93-99). Book V opens here, with thanksgiving from those who have been redeemed and gathered "from the lands, from the east and from the west, from the north and from the south" (107:3), and proceeds through Davidic recovery (Pss 108-110, 138-145), the great Hallel (113-118), the longest psalm (119), the Songs of Ascents (120-134), and the closing doxology (146-150). The placement is theological: thanksgiving is the right speech of a people who have come through judgment and return.

The psalm has no superscription — anonymous like most of Books IV-V. Internal evidence points to the post-exilic period. The opening triad — *redeemed*, *gathered*, "from the east and from the west, from the north and from the south" — echoes the exilic-restoration vocabulary of Isa 43:5-6 and Isa 56:8 (the LORD who gathers the dispersed of Israel). The Hebrew *ge'ulei Yahweh* (גְּאוּלֵי יְהוָה, "the redeemed of the LORD," 107:2) is a near-quotation of Isa 62:12. Most modern critical commentators (Hossfeld-Zenger, Goldingay, Kraus, Allen) date the core of the psalm to the early Persian period (late sixth or fifth century BCE), when returnees had reason to thank God for deliverance from literal exile and could read their own story into the four narrative panels.

In **Gunkel's** form-critical taxonomy, Ps 107 is a **Todalied** — a thanksgiving hymn — but with an unusual feature: rather than the individual *todah* of Pss 30, 32, 116, this is a *communal* thanksgiving organized as a fourfold liturgy. Each panel follows the same structural template: a class of sufferers, the cry to YHWH, the deliverance, the refrain. Gunkel called this the *Danklied der Einzelnen* expanded to communal use; Mowinckel saw the form as cultic — likely a thanksgiving liturgy at the temple where four classes of pilgrims (those returned from desert journey, from prison, from sickness, from sea voyage) brought their *todah* sacrifices and joined a common refrain. Verse 22, "let them offer sacrifices of thanksgiving" (*zivchei todah*, זִבְחֵי תּוֹדָה), names the cultic occasion explicitly.

The two key Hebrew formulas dominate the psalm. The opening line is the great *hodu* formula: *hodu la-Yahweh ki-tov ki le-olam chasdo* (הוֹדוּ לַיהוָה כִּי־טוֹב כִּי לְעוֹלָם חַסְדּוֹ), "Give thanks to the LORD, for he is good, for his steadfast love endures forever" (107:1). This formula recurs at three pivot points in Book V — Pss 107:1, 118:1-29, 136 (the antiphonal *Hallel ha-Gadol* where every verse repeats the second clause). It is also the formula chanted at the dedication of Solomon's temple (2 Chr 5:13, 7:3) and at the laying of the second temple's foundation (Ezra 3:11). Book V, in other words, opens with the same words that opened the temple — and the rebuilt temple — placing the post-exilic community in continuity with the covenant cult.

The second formula is the recurring refrain: *yodu la-Yahweh chasdo ve-niflotav livnei adam* (יוֹדוּ לַיהוָה חַסְדּוֹ וְנִפְלְאוֹתָיו לִבְנֵי אָדָם), "Let them thank the LORD for his steadfast love, for his wondrous works to the children of man" (107:8, 15, 21, 31). The fourfold repetition is liturgically deliberate. The word *chesed* (חֶסֶד) — "steadfast love," covenant loyalty — is the keyword of the entire composition, framing it (107:1, 43) and refraining through it.

The four narrative panels — desert wanderers (vv. 4-9), prisoners (vv. 10-16), the sick (vv. 17-22), seafarers (vv. 23-32) — have ANE parallels but are not directly borrowed. Akkadian *šuilla* prayers from sufferers in similar straits (illness, imprisonment) survive from the Mesopotamian milieu, and Egyptian Hymn of the Sea figures voyage as divine encounter. But the *fourfold* organization, with a common refrain and a common cult-context (sacrifices of thanksgiving), is distinctively Israelite. The panels likely correspond to typical occasions for *todah* offerings under priestly law (Lev 7:11-15), now retold as paradigm cases of YHWH's *chesed*.

There is no DSS scroll preserving Ps 107 in the great 11QPs-a (11Q5), which begins at Ps 101 and runs through Ps 151 but with Ps 107 in fragmentary state. The LXX (Psalm 106 by Greek numbering) is straightforward, with the standard one-off in Hebrew/Greek numbering since Ps 9-10 (which the LXX combines). The LXX preserves all four panels and refrains intact.

## Lens 2: The Literature

Ps 107 is one of the Psalter's most architecturally explicit compositions. The whole psalm is governed by a fourfold structure, each panel built on a six-element template:

1. **Description of distress** (a class of sufferers in a particular trouble)
2. **Cry to YHWH formula**: *va-yitz'aqu el-Yahweh ba-tzar lahem* (וַיִּצְעֲקוּ אֶל־יְהוָה בַּצַּר לָהֶם, "Then they cried to the LORD in their trouble," 107:6, 13, 19, 28)
3. **Deliverance formula**: *mi-metzuqotehem yatzilem* (מִמְּצוּקוֹתֵיהֶם יַצִּילֵם, "and he delivered them from their distress")
4. **Specific deliverance** (matched to the specific distress — straight way, broken bonds, sent word, stilled storm)
5. **Thanksgiving refrain**: "Let them thank the LORD for his steadfast love" (vv. 8, 15, 21, 31)
6. **Theological tagline** (a generalization of God's character — vv. 9, 16, 22, 32)

This is **Lowth's** *synthetic parallelism* — line two extends and intensifies line one — operating at the macro-strophic scale. Within the bicola, Lowth's *synonymous parallelism* dominates: "He turns rivers into a desert, / springs of water into thirsty ground" (107:33). The synonymous form fits the liturgical, antiphonal use: a leader could intone the first colon, the assembly the second.

The four panels exhibit a deliberate progression. The first three (desert, prison, illness) are progressively more interiorized — first geographic, then institutional, then bodily-existential. The fourth (sea storm) is then the catastrophic external return. The four panels move outward from the soul (the lost, the bound, the dying) and back outward to the wild (the storm-tossed seafarer). Read this way, the psalm is a tour of human extremity — and a fourfold testimony that *chesed* finds the sufferer wherever the sufferer is.

There is an inclusio: *chesed* in v. 1 ("for his steadfast love endures forever") and *chesed* in v. 43 ("let them consider the steadfast love of the LORD"). The whole psalm is bracketed by the keyword.

The fourfold *hodu* structure (vv. 1-32) is followed by a wisdom-coda (vv. 33-43) that universalizes the lesson. Verses 33-38 invert imagery — God turns fertile land to waste because of evil (echo of the curses of Deut 28), and turns desert to springs (echo of Isa 35). Verses 39-41 repeat the pattern at the level of social order: God brings down princes and raises the needy (echoing Hannah's song, 1 Sam 2). Verse 42 — "the upright see it and are glad, / and all wickedness shuts its mouth" — closes with a sapiential tableau. Verse 43 is the wisdom signature: *mi-chakham ve-yishmor-eleh* (מִי־חָכָם וְיִשְׁמָר־אֵלֶּה, "Whoever is wise, let him attend to these things"), a near-formula from late wisdom-influenced editorial layers (cf. Hos 14:9, Eccl 12:13).

There is no *selah* in the psalm — Book V uses *selah* sparingly. The prosody is otherwise classical: most lines are 3+3 stress, with the refrains dropping to 4+3 for liturgical emphasis.

## Lens 3: The Larger Story

**Jewish Reception.** *Midrash Tehillim* on Psalm 107 reads the four panels as the four classes who must offer the *birkat ha-gomel* — the blessing for one delivered from danger. The Talmud (*Berakhot* 54b) codifies this from Ps 107: those returning from a sea voyage, from a desert journey, from sickness, from prison — these four must publicly thank God when they reach safety. The *gomel* blessing is recited to this day in synagogue immediately after one of these four deliverances. The psalm thus moves directly from text to halakhic practice.

**Rashi** (eleventh century, Troyes) reads the four panels straightforwardly: the desert-wanderers are the wilderness generation; the prisoners are Joseph; the sick are Hezekiah; the seafarers are Jonah. He treats the psalm as a composite memorial of paradigmatic deliverances. **Ibn Ezra** (twelfth century, Spain) reads the panels less typologically — the sufferers are anyone who has been in such straits — and emphasizes the rationality of *todah* as the proper response of a creature to providence. **Radak** (thirteenth century, Provence) connects the psalm to the return from Babylon (107:3, gathered from the four corners) and reads it as a post-exilic composition, a position that would not become consensus in critical scholarship until the nineteenth century.

In modern Jewish reception, **Buber** read Ps 107 as the model of Israel's great life-encounter with the Thou — the four panels as concrete situations of the *I-Thou* in extremis. **Levenson** has emphasized the social-justice scope of the four panels: they include not just the religiously observant but the foolish, the rebellious, the merchant — a wide-angle picture of who is heard when they cry. **Sarna** (in his JPS commentary) underscored that Ps 107 is the Psalter's strongest argument that thanksgiving, not lament, is Israel's normative mode — Books I-III are dominated by lament, Books IV-V by thanksgiving and praise.

**Christian Reception.** **Augustine** (*Enarrationes in Psalmos* on Ps 106 LXX = Ps 107 MT) read the four panels Christologically as the four kinds of soul Christ rescues: the soul lost in the desert of unbelief, the soul bound in the prison of sin, the soul sick from spiritual disease, the soul tossed on the seas of the world. Each panel becomes a stage of conversion. **Cassiodorus** developed the schema sacramentally: the four panels are healed by baptism (the dry desert finds the spring), by absolution (the bonds broken), by the eucharist (the sent word that heals), by the resurrection (the storm stilled). The eucharistic anaphora itself draws on the *hodu* formula — both the Roman *Te Deum* and Eastern liturgies open consecration with thanksgiving language saturated with this psalm.

**Aquinas** read the wisdom-coda (vv. 33-43) as the great summary of providence: God's reversals of fortune are not capricious but moral, ordered to the eventual flourishing of the upright. **Luther** in the *Operationes in Psalmos* lectures (1519-1521) read Ps 107 as the gospel in Old Testament dress — God's *chesed* is not earned by the four classes (note that the prisoners are guilty: "they had rebelled against the words of God," 107:11) but is given freely when the sufferer cries. Luther underlined that the psalm is *evangelical*: deliverance is unilateral grace. **Calvin** (1557 commentary) read the wisdom-coda as the doctrine of providence, distinguishing God's general providence over creation from his particular providence over the church.

In modern critical scholarship, **Gunkel** and **Mowinckel** classed it as a temple-cult thanksgiving liturgy. **Westermann** (in *Praise and Lament in the Psalms*) made Ps 107 central to his argument that praise is the natural response to deliverance, the structurally inevitable answer to lament. **Kraus** and **Allen** developed the post-exilic dating. **Hossfeld-Zenger** (vol. 3 of their three-volume Psalms commentary) made Ps 107 the structural opening of their reading of Book V — a Book that begins with "from the lands, from the east and from the west" (107:3) and ends with "let everything that has breath praise the LORD" (150:6), a movement from gathered diaspora to universal doxology. **Mitchell** (*The Message of the Psalter*) reads Ps 107 as deliberately introducing Book V's eschatological program — the gathering of the dispersed is the prelude to the messianic kingdom Book V will then unfold through Pss 110, 132, 149.

**Brueggemann** has been the most insistent voice on the psalm's social-political range. The four classes — wanderers, prisoners, the sick, seafarers — are not pious archetypes but social locations. The prisoners (vv. 10-16) are bound in *barzel* (iron chains); the sufferers in panel three are "fools through their sinful ways" — not the spiritually elite. The psalm's *chesed* extends to those society would rather not include in its liturgy. For Brueggemann, the psalm refuses any moralized restriction of God's mercy.

In **NT use**, Ps 107 is not directly quoted, but its imagery saturates the gospel of Mark. Mark 4:35-41 (Jesus stills the storm) is in direct conversation with Ps 107:23-32: Jesus does what only YHWH does — *He made the storm be still, / and the waves of the sea were hushed* (107:29). The disciples' question "Who then is this, that even wind and sea obey him?" (Mark 4:41) is answered by the psalm: this is the LORD whose deeds the seafarers saw in the deep. Similarly, Acts 27 (Paul's shipwreck) is narrated in Lukan idiom that consciously echoes the seafarer panel.

## Lens 4: The Theological Intent

**Tension 1: Thanksgiving as canonical strategy after failure.**

The first interpretive question is what Ps 107 is *doing* at the head of Book V. Three positions:

*Position A: Wilson and Mitchell — the canonical-shape reading.* Book V is the theological response to the catastrophe at the end of Book III. After Ps 89's lament that the Davidic covenant has failed, Book IV answered "the LORD reigns." Book V now adds: "and the redeemed give thanks." The Psalter is teaching its post-exilic readers that thanksgiving is the right speech of a returned community — not lament (which Books I-III exhausted), not ahistorical praise, but *eucharistic memorial* of specific deliverances. On this reading, the structure of Book V (thanksgiving → Davidic recovery in 108-110 → Hallel → Ascents → final doxology) is a programmatic theology of restoration.

*Position B: Hossfeld-Zenger — the diaspora-Zion movement.* The psalm opens with gathering "from the lands" (107:3) and Book V will close in the temple ("praise him in his sanctuary," 150:1). Book V is not primarily about Davidic restoration but about the geographic-liturgical reconstitution of Israel — diaspora returning to the place where YHWH's name dwells. Ps 107 announces that the people scattered to the four corners are now home, and the home they have come to is *thanksgiving*.

*Position C: Brueggemann — the dialectic of orientation, disorientation, reorientation.* Ps 107 is the great *reorientation* psalm — the speech of those who have been in disorientation (the four panels are all extremity) and have come through. The theological function is to teach Israel that disorientation is not the last word; the last word is *yodu la-Yahweh chasdo*. The danger is that thanksgiving becomes complacent — that the four panels are read as resolved past rather than as ongoing reality for someone, somewhere.

**Steelman:** Each reading captures something the other misses. Wilson grasps the canonical architecture; Hossfeld-Zenger grasps the geographic-liturgical movement; Brueggemann grasps the existential function. The tension between them is productive, not contradictory: Book V opens with thanksgiving precisely because the post-exilic community needs *all three* — a theology of restoration after failure, a liturgy of homecoming, and a discipline of remembering disorientation in order to praise rightly.

**Tension 2: The four panels as social-justice scope.**

The four classes include, conspicuously, the foolish (panel 3, those who suffer because of "their sinful ways," 107:17) and the rebellious (panel 2, the prisoners who "had rebelled against the words of God," 107:11). Two positions on what to make of this:

*Position A: The standard Christian reading — undeserved grace.* Luther and Calvin both emphasized that the psalm's *chesed* extends to those who have brought their suffering on themselves. This is gospel: God hears the cry of the guilty as well as the innocent. The point is not that suffering is always merited but that *chesed* is unilateral.

*Position B: The Brueggemann/liberation reading — social-political scope.* The four panels are social locations. The prisoners are not metaphorical — they are people in literal chains. The sick are not just spiritually ill — they are people excluded by ritual purity codes. The point is that YHWH's *chesed* refuses the moralizing closure that the religious community might want to put on it. Anyone who cries out is heard. The eucharistic-Christian appropriation must not domesticate this into private soul-rescue.

**Steelman:** Both readings are right and need each other. The grace-reading without the social-political reading risks becoming pietistic individualism. The social-political reading without the grace-reading risks losing the unilateral character of *chesed*. The psalm holds them together: the *individual* fool, the *individual* prisoner is heard, *and* the four panels are public categories the community is asked to recognize.

**Where this leaves us.** Ps 107 teaches that thanksgiving is structurally appropriate to a community that has come through failure. It teaches that the right answer to "where was God in our exile?" is not to insist God was in control but to *thank* God for the deliverance now visible. It teaches that thanksgiving must include the wide-angle scope of who is delivered — the foolish, the rebellious, the merchant, the sick. It teaches that the great cult-formula *hodu la-Yahweh ki-tov ki le-olam chasdo* is Israel's right speech and the church's eucharistic inheritance.

**Closing question.** If Ps 107 is the fitting opening of a community's life after failure, what does it mean that Christian liturgy has so often given lament more cultic space than thanksgiving — and what would happen if the church's central eucharistic prayer were heard, as Cassiodorus heard it, as the four-panel testimony of those whose desert became a city, whose chains were broken, whose word-sent healing came, whose storm was stilled?
