# Ephesians 1: One Sentence in the Heavenly Places

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

Ephesus in the mid-first century was the showpiece port of Roman Asia, the third or fourth city of the empire after Rome and Alexandria, with somewhere between 200,000 and 250,000 inhabitants. As the seat of the Roman proconsul of Asia and home of the *koinon Asias* (the provincial assembly responsible for coordinating imperial-cult observance), it was a city saturated with cult: the Artemision, the temple of the goddess Artemis Ephesia, was one of the seven wonders of the ancient world, a structure roughly four times the footprint of the Parthenon, encircled by 127 Ionic columns. Its priestesses, the *megabyzoi*, and its neokoroi (temple wardens) wielded substantial civic power, and the silversmith riot of Acts 19:23-41 is a sociologically intelligible response to a religious teacher whose preaching threatened the workshops that fed off pilgrim traffic. Layered onto this was the imperial cult: by the late 80s Ephesus had been granted *neokoros* status for a temple of the Sebastoi (the Flavian emperors), and even under Nero (the most plausible window for a Pauline letter) imperial loyalty was woven into civic festivals, gymnasium dedications, and the rhetoric of Asian provincial elites.

The material culture matters because the letter's vocabulary about powers and principalities lands inside it. Ephesus was famous in antiquity for the *Ephesia grammata* — six magical words inscribed on Artemis's statue and carried as amulets — and for a flourishing trade in incantation manuals. Acts 19:19 reports Christian converts burning fifty thousand drachmas of magical books, a vignette that fits the wider Greek Magical Papyri (PGM) corpus, much of which originates from Asia and Egypt. Excavations along the Curetes Street, the Library of Celsus (completed c. 117), and the East Slope terrace houses give us the urban texture: mosaicked dining rooms, frescoes, a literate merchant class. The Jewish community, attested by Josephus *Antiquities* 14.225-264, had secured under Roman governors the right to keep Sabbath, send the temple tax to Jerusalem, and be exempt from military levies — privileges that made Ephesus one of the more durable diaspora settlements.

Authorship is contested. Critical scholarship since C.L. Mitton's *The Epistle to the Ephesians: Its Authorship, Origin, and Purpose* (1951) has argued for post-Pauline pseudonymity on stylistic and theological grounds: the abnormally long sentences (1:3-14 is one continuous Greek period, the longest in the New Testament), heaped genitive constructions (*kata tēn eudokian tou thelēmatos autou*, "according to the good pleasure of his will"), an ecclesiology in which *ekklēsia* names the universal Church rather than a local assembly, a more realized eschatology in which believers are already raised and seated with Christ (2:6), and roughly thirty percent verbal overlap with Colossians. Andrew Lincoln (Word Biblical Commentary), Rudolf Schnackenburg, Petr Pokorný, Margaret MacDonald, and Ernst Käsemann lean pseudonymous; they place composition c. 70-90 AD by a Pauline-school disciple. Conservative defenders — Harold Hoehner, whose massive *Ephesians* commentary works through every disputed passage, Peter O'Brien, F.F. Bruce, Frank Thielman, Markus Barth in the Anchor Bible, and Clinton Arnold — argue that the stylistic shift is genre-driven (epideictic prose in a circular letter) and that the theological "advance" sits comfortably within Pauline trajectory. A middle position, increasingly common, attributes the prose to a secretary (perhaps Tychicus, named in 6:21) writing under Pauline supervision from the Roman imprisonment c. 60-62.

The textual evidence for the address is decisive in a different way. The phrase ἐν Ἐφέσῳ (*en Ephesō*, "in Ephesus") in 1:1 is **absent from the earliest substantial witnesses: P46 (c. 200), Sinaiticus א in its original hand, and Vaticanus B in its original hand**; later correctors inserted it. Bruce Metzger's *Textual Commentary on the Greek New Testament* records the omission and the impersonal tone of a letter to a city Paul had lived in for over two years (Acts 19:8-10, 20:31), with no personal greetings of the sort that fill Romans 16, and concludes — with most modern editors — that the letter was very likely an encyclical to multiple Asian churches, with the destination filled in per local copy. Marcion in the second century called it the letter "to the Laodiceans" (cf. Col 4:16), which fits the encyclical theory.

Five Greek key terms govern the chapter: εὐλογητός (*eulogētos*, "blessed"), the opening word that signals a *berakah* in the synagogue tradition; προορίσας (*proorisas*, "having predestined") and ἐκλέγομαι (*eklegomai*, "to choose"), both denoting prior divine action; υἱοθεσία (*huiothesia*, "adoption as sons"), a Greco-Roman legal term Paul presses into theological service; οἰκονομία (*oikonomia*, "household management, plan, dispensation") and μυστήριον (*mystērion*, "mystery"), paired in 1:9-10 to describe God's eschatological administration; ἀνακεφαλαιόω (*anakephalaioō*, "to sum up, recapitulate") in 1:10; and ἀρραβών (*arrabōn*, "down payment, earnest"), a commercial term from Phoenician trade in 1:14.

## Lens 2 — The Literature

Genre matters. Ephesians is epideictic — celebratory, praising — more than deliberative, and the opening movement (1:3-14) is shaped as a *berakah*, the Jewish synagogue blessing that opens "Blessed be (the Lord)…" Its closest first-century parallels are the Eighteen Benedictions and the Qumran *Hodayot* (1QH); the Greco-Roman analogue is the Hellenistic encomium. The prose is hymnic, possibly liturgical, with some scholars (J.T. Sanders, Ralph Martin) proposing that an existing baptismal hymn underlies the period. The threefold refrain "to the praise of his glory" (1:6, 12, 14) divides the blessing into three trinitarian movements — election by the Father (1:3-6), redemption in the Son (1:7-12), sealing by the Spirit (1:13-14) — though the seams are softer than that schema suggests, since *en Christō* and its variants appear roughly thirty-six times across Ephesians, eleven of them in this opening period alone.

The literary devices are aggressive. The Greek of 1:3-14 is one sentence of 202 words: dependent clauses stacked on dependent clauses, participles dangling on participles, genitives heaped (*tou theou kai patros tou kyriou hēmōn Iēsou Christou*, six genitives in a row in v. 3). English translations chop this into seven or eight sentences for readability, but the rhetorical effect of the original is breathlessness — a single uninterrupted exhalation of praise that mimics liturgy. An *inclusio* binds 1:3-14 to the intercession of 1:15-23: the blessing tells what God has done; the prayer asks that the readers be enabled to grasp it. The vocabulary "riches of his grace" (1:7) recurs as "riches of his glorious inheritance" (1:18); the "working of his great might" (1:19) maps onto the cosmic enthronement of Christ (1:20-23).

The relationship to Colossians is a puzzle that no consensus has dissolved. Roughly a third of the wording overlaps. Mitton argued that Ephesians depends on Colossians and post-dates it; Markus Barth and a minority counter-argue Colossians depends on Ephesians; the most defensible position is common authorial workshop with two distinct rhetorical aims (Colossians polemical, Ephesians celebratory). The textual variants in this chapter are mostly minor — except for the *en Ephesō* question already noted, and 1:15, where some witnesses read "your love toward all the saints" while others read "your love" without the qualifier. Within the book's argument, chapter 1 establishes the indicative ground (what God has done in Christ) on which chapters 4-6 will build their imperative (how the Church now walks).

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

In the canonical arc, Ephesians 1 sits at a pivot. Israel's election language — "chose" (Deut 7:6-8), "predestined" (less explicit but implied in covenantal categories), "inheritance" (the land, then by extension the *nahalah* of God's people) — is here transposed into Christ. The "fullness of time" (1:10) echoes Galatians 4:4 and signals that Israel's story has reached its eschatological hinge. The cosmic enthronement of 1:20-23 reads Psalm 110:1 and Psalm 8:6 in tandem, a combination already established in 1 Cor 15:25-27.

**Christian Interpretive Trajectory.** Origen's *Commentary on Ephesians* (extant in fragments) pressed the cosmological scope of *anakephalaioō* in 1:10, the verb that Irenaeus would soon make the load-bearing pillar of his *recapitulatio* theology in *Adversus Haereses* III.16-22: in Christ all things are summed up, and the second Adam reverses the first. Chrysostom's twenty-four homilies on Ephesians treated 1:3-14 as a hymn of pastoral comfort, downplaying speculative predestination. Jerome's commentary preserved Origenist exegesis; Marius Victorinus produced the first Latin commentary, with neoplatonic categories shaping his reading of the heavenly places. Theodore of Mopsuestia anchored the Antiochene reading, more historical-grammatical, less allegorical. Aquinas's commentary (*Super Epistolam ad Ephesios Lectura*) developed a careful single-predestination framework: God positively predestines the elect to glory; reprobation is permissive, not symmetrical (*Summa Theologiae* I q.23). Bernard of Clairvaux's bridal mysticism leaned on chapter 5 but drew its Christological scaffolding from the cosmic Christ of 1:20-23.

The Reformation pivot was sharp. Luther read 2:8-9 as the gospel-key but found in chapter 1's election a refuge against works-righteousness anxiety. Calvin's commentary on Ephesians is among his most rigorous; the doctrine of double predestination he develops in *Institutes* III.21-24 takes 1:4-5 and 1:11 as primary texts, arguing that the *eklogē* logically requires its corresponding *reprobatio*. Arminius and the Remonstrants in 1610 countered with conditional election: God elects those whom he foreknows will believe. The Catholic *De Auxiliis* controversy (1597-1607) between the Dominican Báñez (closer to Augustinian-Thomist predestination) and the Jesuit Molina (with his theory of *scientia media*, middle knowledge, that grounds election in God's foreknowledge of free counterfactuals) was suspended by Paul V without verdict — a rare conciliar non-decision. Critical scholarship from F.C. Baur's Tübingen school onward read Ephesians as an early-Catholic synthesis distant from Paul; H.J. Holtzmann formalized the post-Pauline thesis; Mitton consolidated it. Lincoln and Schnackenburg's commentaries continue this trajectory.

Contemporary readings split along several axes. Karl Barth's *Church Dogmatics* II/2, his most original contribution to the doctrine of election, reframes the entire question Christologically: Jesus Christ is the elect One *and* the rejected One, and humanity is elect *in him*; the older question "am I among the elect?" is dissolved into the prior question "who is the elect?" — Christ. N.T. Wright reads the language corporately and Israel-fulfilled: God elected Israel for the world, that election is fulfilled in the Messiah, and Gentile believers are grafted into the elect people. John Barclay's *Paul and the Gift* (2015) names six "perfections" of grace (superabundance, singularity, priority, incongruity, efficacy, non-circularity) and argues Ephesians presses incongruity (grace given to the unworthy) and efficacy (grace that accomplishes what it intends), without necessarily implying Reformed double predestination. Stephen Chester's *Reading Paul with the Reformers* (2017) recovers the actual exegetical moves of Luther and Calvin in dialogue with the New Perspective.

**Jewish Scholarly Critique and Its Evolution.** Jewish reception of Ephesians 1's election language has historically read it as either hostile usurpation of Israel's covenant categories or as Christian over-individualization of what was always corporate. Krister Stendahl's 1963 essay "Paul and the Introspective Conscience of the West" argued that Western Christianity, especially Augustinian-Lutheran Christianity, mistook Paul's corporate Israel-Gentile question for an individual soteriological one. E.P. Sanders's *Paul and Palestinian Judaism* (1977) rebuilt the picture of Second Temple Judaism as covenantal nomism — election by grace, response by Torah-keeping — undercutting the Lutheran caricature. Daniel Boyarin (*A Radical Jew*) reads Paul as a Jewish thinker pushing the universal scope of Israel's God; Mark Nanos (*The Mystery of Romans*) reads him as remaining within Judaism. Pamela Eisenbaum's *Paul Was Not a Christian* (2009) and Paula Fredriksen's *Paul: The Pagans' Apostle* (2017) push further: Paul is best read as a Jewish apostle to Gentiles, addressing Gentile inclusion in the people of Israel's God, not founding a new religion.

For Ephesians 1 specifically, the friction point is whether the Christological narrowing of election (election "in Christ") implicitly disinherits ethnic Israel. Post-Holocaust dialogue has reshaped the question. Vatican II's *Nostra Aetate* §4 (1965) repudiated supersessionism. R. Kendall Soulen's *The God of Israel and Christian Theology* (1996) argues that standard Christian theology has operated on a "structural supersessionism" in which Israel is a placeholder. Mark Kinzer (*Postmissionary Messianic Judaism*, 2005) and Michael Wyschogrod (*The Body of Faith*) defend the continuing election of ethnic Israel as theologically required. Where contemporary Jewish scholarship still pushes back is on the rhetorical totality of Ephesians 1's "all things in Christ" — does this leave room for Israel's particular calling, or does it absorb everything into a Christological universal?

**Where this leaves us.** The dialogue is no longer a dialogue between caricatures. Christian readers who take the New Perspective and post-Holocaust theology seriously increasingly read Ephesians 1's election language corporately and Israel-fulfilled rather than as individual predestination eclipsing Israel.

## Lens 4 — The Theological Intent

**Tension 1: What does προορίσας actually claim?** Ephesians 1:5 and 1:11 use "predestined" twice in the densest predestination text in the New Testament. The Augustinian-Reformed reading, from *De praedestinatione sanctorum* through Calvin's *Institutes* III.21-24 to the Westminster Confession 3.3-7, takes this as unconditional individual election to salvation, with reprobation either symmetrical (Calvin, Beza) or permissive (Aquinas, *ST* I q.23). Lutheran orthodoxy in the *Formula of Concord* affirmed election to salvation but refused to mirror it with reprobation, holding that God genuinely wills the salvation of all (1 Tim 2:4) while predestining only the elect — an asymmetry many critics consider incoherent but which Lutherans defend as biblical reverence before mystery. Wesleyan-Arminian theology, drawing on Arminius and refined by Wesley's *Predestination Calmly Considered*, reads election as conditional on foreseen faith, *prothesis* and *prognōsis* working together. Roman Catholic teaching since Trent and through CCC §§600-605 affirms genuine predestination of the elect to glory while insisting that no one is predestined to evil; the *De Auxiliis* controversy left Báñezian Thomism and Molinism both as permissible Catholic positions. Eastern Orthodox theology (Lossky's *Mystical Theology of the Eastern Church*, Kallistos Ware) is synergistic: God foreknows and provides; the human response is genuine, and "predestination" describes God's eternal love that both precedes and respects creaturely freedom.

The position I find least intuitive is the high Calvinist reading, so let me steelman it. The Reformed insist that in 1:11 the predestining God *energountos* (works) "all things according to the counsel of his will" — *all things*, not some. They argue any conditional reading collapses *charis* into a divine response to human merit, the very thing Paul attacks. Barclay's "incongruity" perfection of grace — grace given without regard to worthiness — fits Calvinism better than its critics want to admit; the Reformed preserve incongruity at maximum strength. Karl Barth then takes Calvin's instinct (election is first about God's free determination) and Christifies it: the elect One is Jesus, and we are elect in him, dissolving the anxious "am I elect?" question. **Where this leaves us:** the text does claim genuine divine priority and effective grace, but its grammar is corporate and Christological — *en Christō*, "in him" — and this corporate Christological frame does not adjudicate the individual-predestination question that the post-Augustinian West has asked of it.

**Tension 2: The "in Ephesus" textual question and canonical reception.** If P46, Sinaiticus א*, and Vaticanus B* lack ἐν Ἐφέσῳ at 1:1, and if the impersonal tone confirms a circular letter whose destination was filled in per copy, what does that imply about how Scripture comes to us? Roman Catholic teaching in *Dei Verbum* 11 grounds canonical authority in Spirit-led reception within the Church across centuries; Trent's decree on the canon (Session 4, 1546) listed the books as authoritative without requiring detailed historical certainty about every textual variant. The textual contingency is therefore absorbed: the Spirit guided the Church to receive *this* letter as canonical, in the form the Church received it. Eastern Orthodox theology likewise privileges ecclesial reception (Bulgakov's *Bride of the Lamb*; Florovsky on *paradosis*). Confessional Protestantism, especially the Reformed under sola scriptura, has been more nervous: if "in Ephesus" was added later, what else might have been? Yet B.B. Warfield, Bruce Metzger, and Daniel Wallace would all answer that responsible textual criticism *is* the Protestant practice — we receive the best-attested text, including its variants noted, and the doctrinal substance is undisturbed. The critical-academic position simply stipulates contingency: ancient texts had editorial histories, and treating the variant honestly does not destabilize the letter's meaning.

The position I find least intuitive is the Catholic-Orthodox confidence in ecclesial reception as itself a theological organ, so let me steelman it. The Spirit who inspired the writers also guides the reception, and the same God who chose to deliver his word through fragile manuscripts and fallible copyists chose to deliver it through a community whose discernment of which letters were *kanōn* was itself a Spirit-led act. To treat the manuscript fragility as undermining is to imagine a docetic scripture, a text untouched by history. **Where this leaves us:** the variant doesn't destabilize the letter's substance; it does mean we should read Ephesians as addressed to "the saints" of Asia generally, which actually strengthens the universal-Church accent that the letter sounds throughout.

**Unresolved question:** If Ephesians 1's blessing celebrates a plan to "unite all things in Christ, things in heaven and things on earth" (1:10), and if the powers of 1:21 are "far below" his throne — what does it mean, concretely, that this cosmic enthronement is invisible to the empires it relativizes, including the one that imprisoned the writer?

## The Thread

- **Daniel 2:44** — the stone that fills the whole earth and the kingdom that shall never be destroyed sits behind the cosmic-rule language of 1:20-23 in a way that is rarely noted. Where Paul says Christ is "above every name," Daniel says the kingdom shall not be left to another people; the inheritance language of 1:11, 14, 18 is also Danielic.
- **Psalm 110:1 + Psalm 8:6** — the catena that produces "seated him at his right hand … and put all things under his feet" (1:20, 22) is the same catena Paul uses in 1 Cor 15:25-27 and that Hebrews 1-2 develops in detail. The non-obvious link is to Hebrews 2:8: "we do not yet see everything in subjection," a tension Eph 1 holds in suspension.
- **Romans 8:28-30** — the "predestined / called / justified / glorified" chain, traditionally read individualistically, reads differently when read alongside Eph 1:4-14: a single corporate movement of incorporation into Christ.
- **Revelation 5:9-13** (later in reading order) — the *new* song "to him who sits on the throne and to the Lamb," with every creature in heaven and on earth, is the eschatological completion of Eph 1:10's "to unite all things in Christ." Ephesians blesses what Revelation visualizes.
