# Ezekiel 1: The Chariot Vision and the Glory on the Move

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

**Political situation**: **Exilic period (593–571 BC)**. Ezekiel's call comes in *the fifth year of King Jehoiachin's exile* (1:2 = 593 BC), and his datable ministry stretches to 571 BC (the latest dated oracle, 29:17). He was deported in 597 BC with Jehoiachin and the elite of Judah — the "good figs" of Jeremiah 24, distinguished from the rump community in Jerusalem (the "bad figs") who would be destroyed in the second deportation of 586 BC. The chronological note in 1:1 ("the thirtieth year") is famously contested: rabbinic tradition (Targum Jonathan, Rashi, Radak) reads it as the thirtieth year from Josiah's reform of 622 BC, which arithmetically lands on 593; **Origen** reads it as Ezekiel's own age (placing him squarely in the priestly maturity threshold of Numbers 4:3); critical commentators (Zimmerli, Greenberg) regard it as redactional or simply ambiguous. The setting is **Tel-abib by the river Chebar** (3:15) — the *Chebar* (כְּבָר) corresponding to the Babylonian *nāru kabari*, the *grand canal* in the Nippur region of southern Babylonia, where the deported Judeans were settled.

**Material culture**: Babylonian exilic community in Mesopotamia, in the heartland of Nebuchadnezzar's empire. The *nāru kabari* / **Chebar canal** ran through the Nippur region, an irrigation artery threading through a settled agricultural plain. The **Murashu archive** (Nippur, 5th c. BC, ~700 cuneiform tablets) and especially the **Al-Yahudu tablets** (6th c. BC, recently published in Pearce & Wunsch, *Documents of Judean Exiles and West Semites in Babylonia*, 2014) directly attest the Jewish exilic community in Babylonia, naming individual Hebrew householders, recording their leases, marriages, and commercial dealings, and confirming the picture given in Jeremiah 29 of an exile that was settled rather than enslaved. **Babylonian temple imagery and wider ANE iconography** is essential to reading Ezekiel 1: the *kerubim* (cherubim, identified retrospectively in 10:15, 20) parallel **the Mesopotamian *karibu* / *šēdu / lamassu*** — the winged composite creatures with human faces and animal bodies that guarded palace and temple thresholds at Khorsabad, Nineveh, and Nimrud. The chariot/throne imagery parallels **Mesopotamian *kudurru* boundary stones**, where deities stand or sit upon emblematic animals, as well as **Akkadian throne iconography** (the seated king with subordinated supernatural attendants). The *firmament* of 1:22 reflects the *gabled heavens* widely attested across Mesopotamian cosmology — a solid expanse beneath which the divine throne is mobile.

**Authorship & dating**: Ezekiel ben Buzi, a priest of the Zadokite line (1:3), exiled with Jehoiachin in 597 BC. His ministry spans 593–571 BC. The book has remarkable structural unity (compared, say, with Jeremiah's compositional layering), which has been read traditionally as evidence of single-author origin — the position defended in modern scholarship by **Moshe Greenberg (Anchor, two volumes 1983/1997 — the standard literary-unity defense)**, **Daniel Block (NICOT, two volumes 1997/1998)**, and earlier by Cooke and Eichrodt. The radical alternative — **Charles C. Torrey's *Pseudo-Ezekiel and the Original Prophecy* (1930)** — argued for 3rd-c. BC pseudonymous composition; this is rejected by virtually all subsequent scholarship as overcorrection. Other standard commentators: **Walther Zimmerli (Hermeneia, two volumes 1979/1983 — the German classic, redaction-critical)**, **Margaret Odell (Smyth & Helwys)**, **John Wevers**, **G. A. Cooke (ICC 1936)**, **Walther Eichrodt (OTL)**, and contemporary work by Lapsley, Wilson, and Wright.

**Archaeological & textual evidence**: **The Murashu archive** and **the Al-Yahudu tablets** anchor the exilic setting in concrete documentary evidence. **Babylonian throne iconography** at Nimrud, Khorsabad, and Nineveh — the *karibu/lamassu* with human faces and animal bodies — supplies the visual grammar Ezekiel inherits and subverts. **The Marduk processional way**, **Esagila temple**, **Etemenanki ziggurat**, **the Ishtar Gate**, and **the *kudurru* stones** are all part of the Babylonian temple-cosmology that surrounds the exiled priest. **Qumran**: 4QEzek-a, b, c are extant; 4QEzek-a is well-preserved through chapter 24. **Pseudo-Ezekiel** (4Q385–390) at Qumran is a separate, dependent work (a Second Temple reception of Ezekiel rather than a manuscript of it).

**Key terms**: *kavod YHWH* (כְּבוֹד יְהוָה, "glory of the LORD" — the central theological term of the book, climaxing the vision in 1:28); *ben adam* (בֶּן אָדָם, "son of man," Ezekiel's vocational title, 93× in the book); *merkavah* (מֶרְכָּבָה, "chariot/throne" — though the word itself does not occur in chapter 1, it becomes the rabbinic title for the vision and the basis of Jewish *Merkavah mysticism*); *ḥayyot* (חַיּוֹת, "living creatures," 1:5 — identified as cherubim in 10:15); *ofanim* (אוֹפַנִּים, "wheels," 1:15–21).

## Lens 2 — The Literature

Genre: **prophetic vision-report** of the most elaborate and visually-mediated kind in the Hebrew Bible. Ezekiel is the most *visual* of the prophets, and chapter 1 is its great inaugural set-piece. The literary movement is bottom-up: the storm out of the north (4), the four creatures (5–14), the wheels beside them (15–21), the firmament above their heads (22–25), the throne above the firmament (26), and finally the human-likeness on the throne (26–28). The reader's eye is led upward through a series of refractions, each layer described with hedging language — *likeness*, *appearance of*, *as it were* — that becomes nearly liturgical in its repetition. **The hedging is itself the theology**: the prophet refuses direct predication of the divine.

The chapter shows Ezekiel's signature literary devices in concentrated form. **Leitwort** technique is everywhere: *demut* (likeness, 10×), *mar'eh* (appearance, 14×), *ke-* (like, *as*, 20+×). **Chiastic structure** is debated but plausible, with the human-likeness on the throne (26–28) framed against the human-likeness of the creatures (5). **Oracle-formula** is largely absent in chapter 1 — the vision is descriptive, not pronounced — but the *kavod*-formula at 1:28 inaugurates a leitmotif (*kavod* 19× in the book) that returns at every major structural seam (chs. 8–11 the glory's departure from the temple; ch. 43 its return to the new temple).

Note that **Ezekiel's bizarre symbolism becomes the foundation of *Merkavah mysticism* in Second Temple Judaism**: 1 Enoch's heavenly throne visions, 4QShirShabbat (the *Songs of the Sabbath Sacrifice* at Qumran), and the later *Hekhalot* literature (3rd–8th c. AD) all trace back to this chapter. **Mishnah Hagigah 2:1** prohibits the public exposition of *Ma'aseh Merkavah* (the work of the chariot); only one sage at a time, only with one student of *binah* (understanding), only in private. The **Bavli Hagigah 11b–14a** transmits the *Pardes* story: four entered the orchard (*pardes* — paradise, but also a code-name for chariot-mysticism), Ben Azzai died, Ben Zoma went mad, Aher (Elisha ben Abuyah) became a heretic, and only Akiva exited unharmed. *The text Jewish boys were prohibited from studying before age thirty* — its dangers reckoned in lives. The literary text is, by tradition, also a thin spot in reality.

Textual variants are present but largely meaning-stable. The LXX of Ezekiel is shorter than MT in places (most notably in 36–39), but chapter 1's variants are minor — the Greek *zōia* renders *ḥayyot* in a way that flows into Revelation 4. 4QEzek-a confirms the MT for chapter 1 in the surviving fragments.

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

Place in the canonical arc: Ezekiel is *the priest in exile* — paired with Jeremiah (the prophet in Judah) and Daniel (the courtier in Babylon) as the three exilic-period theological voices, each working out a different angle on the same catastrophe. Chapter 1 is the first move of the longest sustained prophetic vision-cycle in Scripture, and in canonical reading it is the source-text of the heavenly-throne tradition that runs through Daniel 7, 1 Enoch, the Synoptic Son-of-Man sayings, and Revelation 4–5.

**Jewish trajectory**: Sirach 49:8 already singles Ezekiel out — *who saw the vision of glory* (*hazon kavod*) — making chapter 1 the headline of the prophet's legacy by the 2nd c. BC. The rabbinic restrictions are foundational: **Mishnah Hagigah 2:1** ("one does not expound the chariot in the presence of even one, unless he is a sage who understands of his own knowledge"); **Bavli Hagigah 11b–14a** with the *Pardes* narrative and the related stories of Ben Zoma standing at the edge of the firmament and Rabbi Yohanan ben Zakkai dismounting his ass when his student Eleazar ben Arakh expounded the chariot — *fire descended from heaven and surrounded all the trees in the field*. Targum Jonathan moderates the anthropomorphism by reading the human-likeness on the throne as *the glory of the Shekinah*. Pesikta Rabbati and Midrash Rabbah extend the tradition. **Rashi** (11th c.) takes the vision as a real-time prophetic experience and refuses speculation beyond the text. **Radak** and **Ibn Ezra** attend to the philological. **Maimonides *Guide of the Perplexed* 3.1–7** delivers the most ambitious medieval-Jewish reading: the chariot is decoded into Aristotelian metaphysics — the four creatures as the four sublunar elements or as separate intelligences, the wheels as the celestial spheres, the human-likeness on the throne as the *Active Intellect* (or beyond it). The *Hekhalot* literature ("palaces" or "ascent") imagines mystical journeys through seven concentric heavenly halls toward the throne — direct textual descendants of Ezekiel 1. Modern: Greenberg's literary-unity reading; **Jon Levenson (*Theology of the Program of Restoration of Ezekiel 40–48*, 1976 — the Harvard dissertation that recovered Ezekiel's priestly-spatial theology)**; **Benjamin Sommer (*The Bodies of God*, 2009 — the *fluid-divine-self* reading where YHWH genuinely travels)**; Yochanan Muffs.

**Christian trajectory**: Patristic — **Origen's *Homilies on Ezekiel*** (14 surviving homilies, c. 240) read the chariot allegorically as the soul's ascent and the church's progress. **Jerome's *Commentary on Ezekiel*** (414, fourteen books, written under the shock of the sack of Rome in 410 — the *kavod* leaving Jerusalem read against the *kavod* leaving Rome). **Gregory the Great's *Homilies on Ezekiel*** (twenty-two homilies, the most influential medieval treatment) reads the chariot as the church and its four-fold gospel ministry, anchoring the Christian *tetramorph* tradition in pastoral homiletics. **Irenaeus *Adversus Haereses* 3.11.8** establishes the canonical Christian reading of the four faces as the four evangelists: man = Matthew (the human genealogy), lion = Mark (the royal prophetic voice), ox = Luke (the priestly sacrifice), eagle = John (the soaring divine Word). This becomes the universal **Christian tetramorph iconography** — the four creatures of medieval and Byzantine art, the four corners of countless gospel books and basilica apses. Theodoret continues the line. Medieval — Hugh of St. Victor; **Richard of St. Victor's *De Visione Ezechielis*** (12th c.) — extended contemplative-mystical reading; Aquinas. Reformation — **Calvin's *Lectures on Ezekiel* (1565, two volumes — left incomplete at his death, covering only chs. 1–20)** insists on *accommodation*: God *condescends* to our weakness, and the vision tells us nothing about the divine essence, only about how the divine *appears* to a human. Critical — Torrey's rejected *Pseudo-Ezekiel* (1930); G.A. Cooke (ICC 1936); **Zimmerli (Hermeneia)** as the standard German-tradition critical commentary; **Greenberg (Anchor)** as the standard literary-unity-defense English. Contemporary — **Block (NICOT)**; Odell; **Lapsley (*Can These Bones Live?*)**; Christopher Wright; **Robert R. Wilson (Yale)**.

**Where they diverge**: the chariot is the locus of *mystical* interpretation in Jewish tradition (Merkavah → Hekhalot → Kabbalah) and *Trinitarian-Christological* interpretation in Christian tradition (the four creatures as the four Gospels; the human-likeness on the throne read forward to the Son in Revelation 4–5; medieval icons of Christ-in-Majesty surrounded by the tetramorph). Both traditions share the conviction that the chapter resists exhaustive interpretation, but they route the resistance differently: Judaism by *prohibition* and *initiation*, Christianity by *typology* and *fulfillment*.

## Lens 4 — The Theological Intent

**Tension 1: The interpretive ceiling — what does it mean that the canonical text is *deliberately overwhelming*?** The hedging language of chapter 1 — *demut*, *mar'eh*, *ke-* — is not stylistic ornament but theological statement. The text itself enforces *apophasis*: we are told what the vision is *like*, never what it *is*.

The **Catholic and Orthodox apophatic tradition** runs straight through this chapter. **Pseudo-Dionysius's *Mystical Theology*** (5th–6th c.) takes the via negativa as constitutive of authentic God-knowledge: every affirmation must be unsaid in the higher recognition that the divine exceeds it. **Gregory Palamas** (14th c.) and the Eastern hesychast tradition build their theology of the *uncreated energies* (the *kavod* / *doxa* / *taborlight*) on exactly this distinction — what we encounter is divine glory, not divine essence. **Reformed *accommodation* doctrine** (Calvin Institutes 1.13) reads the same hedging as God's lisping: Scripture *speaks baby-talk* to us because we cannot bear adult speech. **Jewish mystical tradition** (the *en-sof*, the infinite without-end of Kabbalah; the *Pardes* warning) frames the same point as initiatic: not everyone can survive the vision unmediated. **Maimonides** reads the multiplied hedges as philosophical: positive predication of the divine essence is impossible; only negation and effect-language are licit. The least intuitive position to a modern reader may be the rabbinic-prohibitive one — *do not study the chariot under thirty* feels obscurantist. The steelman: the rabbis understood *something true* about how human cognition collapses under direct contact with ultimate reality. The *Pardes* deaths and madness are not legend but experiential testimony to a real spiritual hazard. The text's multiplied hedges encode the same warning that the rabbis encoded by gating the text. Where this leaves us: chapter 1 is a *trained text* — it tutors the reader in restraint by enacting restraint. To miss the apophasis is to miss the chapter.

**Tension 2: The *kavod* on the move — does YHWH's glory *travel*?** The throne in 1:26 has wheels. The book's structural arc is the glory's *departure* from the Jerusalem temple (chs. 8–11) and its *return* to a new temple (ch. 43). This is theologically combustible.

**Catholic immutability tradition** (CCC 200, *Catechism* on divine immutability; Aquinas *ST* I.9) holds that God does not change in being or location; what *moves* is creation in relation to God. **Reformed omnipresence and immutability** (Calvin Institutes 1.13–14) reads the wheels as accommodated speech: the fact that God is *available* to the exiles is depicted as the divine-throne *coming to* them, but the underlying truth is omnipresence. **Process theology** (Whitehead, Hartshorne, Cobb) takes the wheels seriously as ontology: the divine is genuinely *in process*, the "fellow-sufferer who understands." **Jewish *Shekinah* tradition** (Targum, **Bavli Megillah 29a** — "in every place to which they were exiled, the Shekinah went with them") inhabits a middle position: the divine presence accompanies Israel into exile without compromising the unity of the divine. **Sommer's *The Bodies of God*** argues that the Hebrew Bible itself attests a *fluid divine self* — YHWH's *kavod* genuinely moves, takes localized form, separates and rejoins. The least intuitive option for a confessional Catholic or Reformed reader is the Sommer/Process line. The steelman: the canonical text really does describe a wheeled throne climbing into the sky over Jerusalem and a wheeled throne returning over the new temple, and theological abstraction has to begin with the data, not over it. If divine immutability cannot accommodate the chariot leaving and returning, divine immutability has been imported and the text has been silenced. The Shekinah tradition shows that even within strict monotheism a real divine *traveling-with* is articulable. Where this leaves us: Ezekiel 1 inaugurates a theology in which the deepest sign of divine sovereignty is not stasis but pursuit — the *kavod* will not be confined to one geography, and exile is not abandonment.

**The unresolved question**: If the *kavod* travels, what does it mean to say that any place — temple, land, body, congregation — is *the* place of God? The chapter offers no answer, but it puts the question on the table that the rest of the book, and arguably the rest of Scripture, cannot stop turning.

## The Thread

The chariot of Ezekiel 1 reappears as the heavenly throne of **Revelation 4–5** — the four living creatures around the throne, the rainbow, the sea of crystal — but the human-likeness on the throne is now identified, and the slain Lamb stands in the midst. Daniel 7 takes Ezekiel's *ben adam* address and turns it into the *one like a son of man* who comes with the clouds, and the New Testament reads the line through to Christology: Jesus's preferred self-designation as *the Son of Man* assumes the entire Ezekielian vocational chain. The *kavod*'s departure-and-return arc (Ezekiel 8–11; 43) reappears in Christ's "I will not leave you as orphans" (John 14) and in the eschatological "Behold, the dwelling place of God is with man" (Rev 21:3). The chariot is also the silent referent of Jewish *Merkavah* mysticism, of Christian iconographic tetramorph, and of the long apophatic tradition in both faiths — the text that taught Judaism and Christianity, in different keys, how to talk about a God who exceeds talk.
