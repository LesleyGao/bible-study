# Mark 16: The Empty Tomb and the Gospel That Ends in Fear

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

The chapter opens with three named women — Mary Magdalene, Mary the mother of James, and Salome — buying spices "when the Sabbath was past" (16:1). The detail is concrete and chronologically precise: Sabbath ended at sundown Saturday; the spice merchants would have reopened, and three women out at first light Sunday with aromatics in hand fits the Jerusalem festal calendar. The spices (*arōmata*) were almost certainly myrrh and aloes — a mixture documented in the Mishnah (*m. Shabbat* 23.5) and Josephus (*Antiquities* 17.199, on Herod's funeral, with five hundred spice-bearers). These are not embalming spices in the Egyptian sense — Jewish burial did not embalm — but rather aromatics intended to mask decomposition during the secondary mourning ritual, when the family returned to the tomb on the third day to confirm death and tend the body.

The ***rolling-stone tomb*** of 16:3–4 is a specific archaeological category. Most first-century Judean tombs were sealed with rectangular *blocking stones* (*golel*) wedged into a doorway — chambers fronted by a low door, often with a *kokh* niche system inside for individual ossuaries. Genuinely round, disc-shaped rolling stones (*kalal*) running in a track were rare and prestigious — only about four are known in the Jerusalem area from the Second Temple period: at the Tomb of the Kings (Helena of Adiabene's family tomb, 1st c. CE), at the so-called Tomb of Herod's Family near the King David Hotel, at the Tomb of the Sanhedrin in the northern necropolis, and at one or two smaller sites. **Amos Kloner**'s 1999 *Biblical Archaeology Review* survey ("Did a Rolling Stone Close Jesus' Tomb?") concluded that the rolling-stone type was the elite minority. If Mark's stone was a true *kalal* — and the women's anxiety in 16:3 about needing help to move it suggests it was — Joseph of Arimathea's tomb was an unusually expensive burial, consistent with Mark 15:43's "respected member of the council." The Holy Sepulchre site preserves first-century *kokh* tombs in the Edicule, with disturbed evidence of how the original tomb's blocking-stone arrangement worked.

The ***young man*** (*neaniskos*, 16:5) sitting on the right side of the tomb, dressed in white, has clear angelic resonance — Matthew 28:2 and Luke 24:4 explicitly call him an angel, and white garments are the standard apocalyptic-angelic uniform (Daniel 7:9; 2 Macc 3:26; Mark 9:3 at the Transfiguration, where Jesus's garments became "intensely white"). But Mark says only *neaniskos*, the same word used at 14:51–52 for the young man who fled naked at Jesus's arrest. The vocabulary linking is deliberate (engage Lens 2 below). White-robed figures at tombs are also a Jewish funerary trope; the *Mishnah* records the white linen *takhrikhin* in which corpses were wrapped (*m. Ma'aser Sheni* 5.12).

Three Greek terms are decisive. ***ēgerthē*** (ἠγέρθη, 16:6, "he has been raised") — passive voice; the Father raises the Son. This is the standard New Testament resurrection vocabulary (Romans 4:25; 1 Corinthians 15:4) and contrasts with active *anastēnai* used elsewhere; the passive emphasizes the Father's act and avoids any suggestion of Jesus's autonomous self-resuscitation. ***ephobounto gar*** (ἐφοβοῦντο γάρ, 16:8, "for they were afraid") — likely the original ending of Mark, a sentence ending with the postpositive particle *gar*. ***neaniskos*** (νεανίσκος, 16:5) — the same noun as 14:51, structurally tying the empty tomb to the Gethsemane flight scene.

The site of the tomb is the same Holy Sepulchre / Garden Tomb question discussed in chapter 15. The first-century tombs around the Holy Sepulchre rock are well documented — six rock-cut tombs survive in the immediate area, all dating before 70 CE (engage **Dan Bahat** *The Illustrated Atlas of Jerusalem*; **Shimon Gibson**, *The Final Days of Jesus*). The Garden Tomb is a real Second Temple period tomb but the wrong typology for Mark 15:46's description.

The chapter's geographical pivot to Galilee (16:7) reflects a historically distinctive feature of Mark's resurrection theology: Mark sends the disciples *back* to the place where it began. *Galilaia* — the unnamed northern province of Capernaum, the lake, the call of the fishermen — is where the Gospel started in 1:14 and where, in Mark's framing, the resurrection is to be witnessed. Matthew 28:16–20 narrates a Galilean appearance; Luke and John keep the disciples in Jerusalem. Mark stops before any appearance is described.

## Lens 2 — The Literature

Mark 16 is the textual problem chapter of the New Testament. Three endings circulate in the manuscript tradition.

The **Short Ending at 16:8** is the form preserved in **Codex Sinaiticus** (ℵ, 4th c.) and **Codex Vaticanus** (B, 4th c.) — the two oldest complete uncial manuscripts of the Gospels. Both end the Gospel mid-sentence with *ephobounto gar* — "for they were afraid" — and leave the rest of the column blank. **Eusebius** (*Quaestiones ad Marinum* 1) and **Jerome** (*Epistle* 120 to Hedybia) attest in the 4th–5th centuries that "the most accurate copies" ended at 16:8, and that the longer ending was known but disputed. Several Old Latin, Sahidic Coptic, and Armenian witnesses also end at 16:8 or mark 16:9–20 with critical sigla. **Bruce Metzger**'s *A Textual Commentary on the Greek New Testament* gives the short ending an A-rating; **Bart Ehrman**'s *The Orthodox Corruption of Scripture* concurs; modern critical editions (NA28, UBS5) print 16:9–20 in double brackets.

The **Longer Ending (16:9–20)** is the form printed in most English translations and preserved in the majority of later manuscripts: **Codex Alexandrinus** (A, 5th c.), **Codex Bezae** (D, 5th c.), **Codex Washingtonianus** (W, 5th c., though see below), the Byzantine majority, the Latin Vulgate, and most Syriac and Coptic traditions. Internal evidence weighs against authenticity: vocabulary and style differ markedly from the rest of Mark; transitional gaps (Mary Magdalene is reintroduced in 16:9 as if she has not just been named in 16:1); seventeen Markan signature words drop out and new vocabulary appears. The pericope is widely judged a 2nd-century addition, possibly composed by drawing on Matthew 28, Luke 24, John 20, and Acts. **R.T. France** (NIGTC), **Joel Marcus** (AB), **Adela Yarbro Collins** (Hermeneia), **Morna Hooker** (BNTC), Vincent Taylor, **Raymond Brown** all judge it inauthentic. **James Snapp Jr.** (*Authentic: The Case for Mark 16:9–20*) defends Markan authenticity from a minority traditional position; the case rests on the breadth of late attestation rather than the early manuscripts.

The **Shorter Ending** (one or two sentences) appears in a small group of manuscripts and reads, "But they reported briefly to those around Peter all that had been commanded; afterward Jesus himself sent out through them, from east to west, the sacred and imperishable proclamation of eternal salvation." This is preserved in some Old Latin and Syriac witnesses and in a few late Greek manuscripts that include both the shorter and the longer endings — direct evidence that scribes encountered an unsatisfying short ending and produced supplements.

The **Freer Logion** is a peculiar additional saying inserted between 16:14 and 16:15 in **Codex Washingtonianus** (W, the Freer Gospels Codex now in the Smithsonian). Jerome (*Dialogue Against the Pelagians* 2.15) preserves a Latin version of the same logion. It has the disciples beg Jesus to reveal "the righteousness of God" now, and Jesus responds about the limits of Satan's reign. The Freer Logion looks like a 2nd-century catechetical insertion responding to disappointment that the parousia had not yet come.

The textual evidence is, by the standards of NT criticism, remarkably clear: **the Gospel of Mark almost certainly ends at 16:8**.

This is the literary problem. A Gospel that ends mid-sentence with *ephobounto gar* — "for they were afraid" — is unprecedented. *Gar* is a postpositive conjunction; ending a sentence (let alone a *book*) with *gar* is rare in Greek prose. **R.H. Lightfoot** (*The Gospel Message of Saint Mark*, 1950) argued the abrupt ending could not be original on grammatical grounds alone. But subsequent scholarship has produced examples (Plotinus, *Enneads* 5.5.13; some passages in 32 Plutarch's *Moralia*) showing that *gar*-final sentences are unusual but not impossible. The grammatical objection has not survived close inspection.

The literary case for the abrupt ending as Markan masterstroke is now the dominant scholarly position. **Frank Kermode**'s *The Genesis of Secrecy* (1979) is the foundational text. Kermode argued that Mark's ending is the most theologically generative move in the Gospel — it forces the reader into the gap, into the position of the women who fled in fear, and the *resolution* of the Gospel must be supplied by the reader's own response. Mark refuses to comfort. **Donald Juel**'s *A Master of Surprise: Mark Interpreted* (1994) is the most extended argument for the ending as deliberate Markan theology: the Gospel that opened with God tearing open the heavens (1:10) and ended with the temple veil torn (15:38) cannot end with the women's flight — it must end *outside* the text, in the reader's own decision whether to go to Galilee. **Mary Ann Tolbert** (*Sowing the Gospel*), **Joanna Dewey**, **Gail O'Day**, **Adela Yarbro Collins**, and **Joel Marcus** all defend the abruptness as deliberate. **N.T. Wright** (*Mark for Everyone*; *The Resurrection of the Son of God*) accepts that Mark ended at 16:8 but argues this is *not* the Gospel ending in fear — it is an open-ended ending that points the reader toward Galilee, where the resurrected Christ awaits.

The structural logic is breathtaking. Mark opens (1:1) with *archē tou euangeliou* — "the beginning of the gospel" — and ends (16:8) with the women's silence. The Gospel is *the beginning*; the rest, including any resurrection appearance the reader might wish for, is the reader's task. The structural pairing with the *neaniskos* is similarly purposeful: at 14:51–52, an unnamed young man flees naked in shame from Jesus's arrest; at 16:5, an unnamed young man sits in white at the empty tomb proclaiming the resurrection. The Gospel is bracketed by these two *neaniskoi* — the one who failed and the one who proclaims — and Mark's reader is invited to identify with the path between them.

A final literary observation. Mark 16 contains *no resurrection appearance*. The angel announces the resurrection; the women flee. The Christ of Mark is, until the very end, the Christ of the cross. Even the resurrection is held at one remove — *announced*, not seen. This is the consummation of Markan theology of the cross: there will be no theology-of-glory ending.

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

The empty tomb tradition is one of the earliest and best-attested elements of the Christian kerygma. **1 Corinthians 15:3–7** — Paul citing creedal tradition he received "as of first importance," dated by most scholars to within five to seven years of the crucifixion — names the resurrection (*egēgertai*, perfect passive) and the appearances (to Peter, to the Twelve, to five hundred at once, to James, to all the apostles, to Paul). Paul does not specifically name the empty tomb, but the *burial* is part of the creed (1 Cor 15:4) and a missing body is the implied counterpart. **N.T. Wright**'s *The Resurrection of the Son of God* (2003) is the most comprehensive modern treatment; Wright argues the historical evidence for an empty tomb is robust and that the women-as-first-witnesses detail (a fact every Gospel preserves, against the legal-rhetorical preference of the ancient world that would never invent women as primary witnesses) is a marker of historical anchoring. **Dale Allison**'s *Resurrecting Jesus* (2005) is the most rigorous recent skeptical-critical engagement.

Within Second Temple Judaism, bodily resurrection was a contested doctrine. **Daniel 12:2** is the clearest Hebrew Bible witness; the Pharisees affirmed resurrection (Josephus *Antiquities* 18.14; Acts 23:8); the Sadducees denied it (Mark 12:18). The hope that some Jews would be raised on the eschatological day was widespread by the first century — at Qumran, in 1 Enoch, in 2 Baruch. What was *not* expected was the resurrection of a single individual *in advance of* the general resurrection. **N.T. Wright** (*The Resurrection of the Son of God*) and **Geza Vermes** (*The Resurrection*) emphasize that the early Christian claim was structurally novel within Jewish thought: not just "Jesus has gone to be with God" but "God has raised Jesus bodily, ahead of schedule." **Daniel Boyarin** (*The Jewish Gospels*) and **Amy-Jill Levine** (*The Misunderstood Jew*) place the claim within the spectrum of contested first-century Jewish messianism without supersessionist framing.

The Christian interpretive trajectory has labored at this chapter — and at its missing or contested ending — across two millennia. **Origen** (*Commentary on Matthew* 12.43; *Contra Celsum* 5.56–58) defended the resurrection as bodily but accepted that the risen body was transformed (*sōma pneumatikon*, drawing on Paul). **Augustine** (*City of God* 22; *Sermons on the Resurrection*) systematized resurrection theology and engaged the empty tomb account. **Aquinas** (*ST* III q. 53–55) treats the resurrection's modes and ramifications. The Reformers (Calvin *Harmony of the Evangelists*; Luther sermons on the resurrection) preached the empty tomb as the foundation of justification. **Bonhoeffer** (*Discipleship*; *Letters and Papers from Prison*) makes the resurrection the ground of the church's existence in a "world come of age."

**Jürgen Moltmann** (*The Crucified God*; *The Coming of God*) builds his eschatology on the resurrection of the Crucified: the future of God enters history at Easter and pulls the present forward. **Hans Urs von Balthasar** (*Mysterium Paschale*) reads the empty tomb against the descent into Hades — the resurrection is the *return* from absolute absence, not a continuation of life. **Karl Barth** (*Church Dogmatics* III/2 §47; IV/1 §59) reads the resurrection as the verification by God of the act of the cross, the eternal Yes that confirms the obedience of the Son.

The longer ending (16:9–20), though almost certainly not original, has had reception of its own. The promises of 16:17–18 — believers will cast out demons, speak in new tongues, pick up serpents, drink poison, and heal the sick — became the basis for Pentecostal and charismatic tongues practice in the 20th century, and for the **snake-handling churches** of Appalachia (engage **Ralph Hood Jr.** *Them That Believe*; **David Kimbrough** *Taking Up Serpents*). The Pentecostal movement that emerged at Azusa Street in 1906 explicitly cited Mark 16:17 in its founding documents. The serpent-handling tradition, which began with George Hensley around 1910 in Tennessee, has never numbered more than a few thousand adherents but has produced a striking sociological literature; Hood and Kimbrough document the deaths from snakebite among practitioners. The hermeneutical question is sharp: what authority does a likely-non-original passage carry in churches that nonetheless treat it as Scripture? **Bart Ehrman**'s *The Orthodox Corruption of Scripture* and **Misquoting Jesus* sharpened the public version of this question in the 2000s.

## Lens 4 — The Theological Intent

**Tension One: Does the Gospel really end in fear?**

The ***Markan masterstroke*** position (Kermode *Genesis of Secrecy*; Juel *A Master of Surprise*; Tolbert; Marcus; Yarbro Collins; the dominant modern critical view) reads 16:8 as Mark's deliberate theological achievement. The Gospel ends with the women's silence and fear because the reader must complete the story. To understand the resurrection is to be sent to Galilee yourself. The strength: it accounts for the textual evidence and reads Mark's ending as continuous with Mark's other features (the messianic secret, the failure of the disciples, the open-ended parables of chapter 4). The weakness: it asks the reader to bear theological weight that no other Gospel asks; it can feel like making a virtue of textual misfortune.

The ***lost ending*** position (older critical view, e.g., **C.H. Turner**; some modern conservative defenders) holds that Mark wrote a longer ending that was lost early — perhaps the last leaf of the autograph was torn off, or the original ending was destroyed in some accident of transmission. The 16:9–20 longer ending is then a 2nd-century replacement. The strength: it preserves the assumption that a Gospel must end with appearances. The weakness: it is unprovable and exegetically unnecessary if 16:8 can be read as deliberate.

The ***longer ending authentic*** position (Snapp; some traditionalist Catholic and Orthodox commentators) holds 16:9–20 as Markan or at least apostolic, on the strength of broad ancient attestation. The strength: it preserves Markan completeness and the longer ending's canonical use. The weakness: it sits against the weight of the earliest manuscripts and the internal stylistic evidence.

**Where this leaves us**: Mark's reader faces a Gospel that gives no comforting ending. Whether the ending was lost or composed, the church has lived for at least 1,800 years with a Gospel that compels response rather than closing the story. The theological achievement is the same: Mark refuses to let the reader finish the book and walk away. *Galilee* is the ending, and Galilee is in the future.

**Tension Two: How are we to read the longer ending pastorally and canonically?**

The ***critical-removal*** position (Ehrman; many modern Protestants) argues the longer ending should be removed from preaching and teaching since it is almost certainly not original. The strength: it respects historical and textual evidence. The weakness: it sets the church against its own canonical history; the longer ending has been read as Scripture in most of the church for most of church history.

The ***canonical-but-bracketed*** position (most modern critical Bible editions; many mainline Protestants) prints the longer ending with critical brackets and a note, treating it as part of the canonical text but not original Mark. The strength: it preserves both historical accuracy and ecclesial reception. The weakness: it can feel intellectually unstable — what is the canonical authority of a passage we mark as inauthentic?

The ***reception-authoritative*** position (Catholic Magisterium; Eastern Orthodox; many traditional Protestants) holds that the longer ending, having been received as canonical Scripture by the church and used in liturgy and preaching for nearly two millennia, possesses canonical authority by virtue of the church's reception, regardless of the original textual question. The strength: it takes ecclesial reception seriously as a theological category. The weakness: it can detach canonicity from textual integrity in ways that destabilize the doctrine of Scripture.

**Where this leaves us**: the snake-handling debate is the practical edge. If the church reads 16:18 as authoritative, can a believer be required to drink poison to prove faith? Most traditions answer no on the basis of the broader teaching of Scripture; the longer ending's promises are descriptive, not prescriptive. But the question marks the danger of treating disputed text as univocally authoritative without the broader Scriptural framework.

**An unresolved question**: if Mark really did end at 16:8 — and the textual evidence says he did — then *the earliest written Gospel ends without a resurrection appearance*. The church's confession that "Jesus is risen" rests, in Mark's telling, on an empty tomb, an angelic announcement, and the women's terrified silence. Everything else — the appearances, the meals, the wounds, the breath of the Spirit — comes from later witnesses. Mark trusted his reader to take the announcement and go to Galilee. He still does.

## The Thread

16:7's "going before you to Galilee" reaches all the way back to 1:14 ("after John was arrested, Jesus came into Galilee, proclaiming the gospel of God") — Mark's resurrection sends the reader to the beginning of the book, inviting a re-reading in light of Easter. The *neaniskos* in white (16:5) pairs with the *neaniskos* who fled naked in shame (14:51–52) — the disciple's failure and the resurrection's proclamation are bracketed by the same word. 16:6's *ēgerthē* — "he has been raised" — answers the trembling cry of dereliction at 15:34: the Father who appeared silent has spoken in the only word that matters. And 16:8's *ephobounto gar* — "for they were afraid" — does not contradict the discipleship Mark has been teaching. Mark's first reader was afraid too. The Gospel begins again every time someone, despite the fear, goes to Galilee.
