# Numbers 20: Because You Did Not Believe in Me

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

Numbers 20 is the hinge of the book. Miriam dies. Moses strikes a rock he was told to speak to. Aaron is stripped of his priestly garments on a mountain and dies. Edom refuses Israel safe passage. In one chapter the triumvirate that led the exodus is effectively reduced to one, and that one has just been told he too will not cross the Jordan. The chapter is *Bĕmidbar* ("in the wilderness") at its darkest — the senior generation, the architects of the escape from Pharaoh, are removed from the narrative one by one, and the wilderness itself is revealed as the place where leaders die.

Chronology matters. Verse 1 places the congregation in the wilderness of Zin "in the first month," and rabbinic and Christian commentators from antiquity on have read this as the first month of the fortieth year — the narrative jumping from the second year (end of Numbers 19 / earlier) to the final year of wandering, with thirty-eight years of silence between. Milgrom (JPS *Numbers*, Excursus 50) and Levine (AB *Numbers 21–36*) both argue this jump is built into the text's literary structure, not a redactional error, because Numbers 33's itinerary list treats the period as elapsed. Kadesh-barnea, the setting, is generally identified with ʿAin el-Qudeirat in the northeastern Sinai, where Iron Age fortifications and Bronze Age occupation layers have been excavated; Kitchen (*On the Reliability of the Old Testament*) takes the identification as a reasonable fit for a Late Bronze pastoral encampment on the edge of the Negev, while Finkelstein and other minimalists argue that the absence of Late Bronze remains at the site undercuts the narrative's historicity. Mount Hor (v. 22), where Aaron dies, is traditionally identified with Jebel Harun ("Aaron's Mountain") near Petra in modern Jordan; modern scholarship (Levine; Hoffmeier) favors a site closer to Kadesh, possibly Jebel Madurah, because Deuteronomy 10:6 names the site of Aaron's death as "Moserah," which fits a different topography.

The Edomite refusal (vv. 14–21) has political-historical weight. Edom is Esau's line (Gen 36), and Moses's address to Edom — "thus says your brother Israel" (v. 14) — explicitly invokes kinship. The "King's Highway" (*derek ha-melek*, v. 17) is the north-south trade route running through Transjordan, attested archaeologically as a major Bronze and Iron Age thoroughfare with waystations at Aqaba, Bozrah, and Heshbon. The question of whether Edom in the late thirteenth or early twelfth century B.C.E. had a state capable of fielding "a large army and a strong force" (v. 20) is contested: Crystal-M. Bennett's excavations at Buseirah (biblical Bozrah) showed Iron II but not Late Bronze occupation, a point Finkelstein presses hard; Kitchen and Piotr Bienkowski's later surveys (*Early Edom and Moab*) argue for a pastoral-tribal polity capable of military mobilization earlier than the sedentary archaeology suggests.

Key Hebrew terms. *Mĕrîbâ* (מְרִיבָה, v. 13) is "quarreling," "strife" — a doublet with Exodus 17's *massâ-u-mĕrîbâ*; it names the place and accuses the people. *Hiqdîš* (הִקְדִּישׁ) / *lĕhaqdîšēnî* (לְהַקְדִּישֵׁנִי, v. 12) — "to sanctify me" / "to treat me as holy" — is what Moses failed to do. The verb is causative *Hiphil* of *qādaš*, "to be holy"; the sense is "to make manifest as holy." *Lōʾ heʾĕmantem bî* (לֹא־הֶאֱמַנְתֶּם בִּי, v. 12), "you did not believe in me," employs *ʾāman* in the Hiphil, the same verb used of Abraham in Genesis 15:6. *Nēʾāsapt ʾel ʿammeykā* (נֵאָסַפְתָּ אֶל־עַמֶּיךָ, v. 24), "be gathered to your people," is the archaic Pentateuchal euphemism for death among kin (Gen 25:8, 35:29, 49:29). And the detail of v. 26 — *hafšēṭ ʾet-ʾahărōn ʾet-bĕgādāyw* ("strip Aaron of his garments") — uses the same verb (*pāšaṭ*) employed in priestly investiture texts (Lev 16:23; Ezek 44:19), signaling a ritual de-investiture performed in full view of the people.

## Lens 2 — The Literature

The chapter braids four narrative units — Miriam's death (v. 1), the Meribah incident (vv. 2–13), Edom's refusal (vv. 14–21), and Aaron's death (vv. 22–29) — into a single literary unit about the passing of the founding generation. The three deaths are flagged literarily: Miriam buried at Kadesh, Aaron stripped and gathered to his people, and Moses's future death announced (v. 12) even though its enactment waits until Deuteronomy 34. The Edom episode breaks the pattern of deaths to inject a political failure — not only are the leaders dying, but Israel is also being refused recognition by its closest kin. This is a deliberate literary arrangement, not a chronological accident.

Source criticism here is substantial. Noth's classic analysis in *The History of Pentateuchal Traditions* pairs Numbers 20:2–13 with Exodus 17:1–7 as two versions of a single water-from-rock tradition: the place-names overlap (both called Meribah), the core narrative elements match (people grumble, Moses takes his staff, rock yields water), but the evaluation of Moses differs (Exodus 17 is neutral, Numbers 20 condemns). Frank Moore Cross (*Canaanite Myth and Hebrew Epic*) developed Noth's view, arguing the Exodus 17 version is JE and the Numbers 20 version P — one tradition preserved twice, with different theological emphasis. Milgrom and Thomas Dozeman have pushed back: the doubling is intentional, not accidental, and Numbers 20 stages the Meribah tradition anew in order to explain *why* Moses does not enter the land.

Literary devices. The sequence in vv. 7–11 is a carefully built structural irony: YHWH's instruction is "take the staff, assemble the congregation, and *tell* (*wĕ-dibbartem*, v. 8) the rock"; Moses takes the staff, assembles the people, and then *speaks* — but he speaks to the people, not the rock, and what he says ("Hear now, you rebels: shall we bring water for you out of this rock?", v. 10) positions himself and Aaron as water-bringers. Then he strikes the rock twice, and the very abundance of the water (*mayim rabbîm*, v. 11 — "many waters") underscores divine grace in contradiction to Moses's failure. The name Meribah (v. 13) crystallizes the scene: strife — Israel's strife with Moses, Moses's strife with Israel, God's strife with both. The thirty-day mourning for Aaron at the chapter's end (v. 29) matches the thirty-day mourning for Moses at Deut 34:8, a literary binding of the two deaths even across the book boundary.

Textual variants are consequential. 4QNumᵇ preserves fragments of chapter 20 generally agreeing with the MT. The LXX at v. 10 renders Moses's address as *akousate mou hoi apeitheis*, "listen to me, you disobedient ones" — slightly softening the Hebrew's sharpness (*šimʿû-nāʾ ha-mōrîm*, "hear now, you rebels"). SP at v. 13 adds a short expansion tying the Meribah event to Deut 32:51. The variant worth attention is in v. 12: the MT's "you did not believe in me, to sanctify me" is preserved in LXX, but Targums (Onkelos, Pseudo-Jonathan) expand with interpretive glosses — Pseudo-Jonathan specifies that Moses's fault was speaking angrily rather than softly to the rock. These targumic expansions crystallize the rabbinic debate over Moses's precise sin.

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

**Jewish reception**: The question of *why* Moses was punished so severely has occupied Jewish commentary for two millennia. Abraham Ibn Ezra, in his short commentary, identifies at least *ten* distinct rabbinic answers, settling himself on Moses's doubt ("you did not believe"). Rashi follows Tanḥuma: Moses was told to speak but struck instead, and this substitution — hitting instead of speaking — was the sin. Ramban (Nachmanides) rejects Rashi's reading as insufficient: striking a rock is not a capital offense of faith; the sin lay in "*shall we* bring water" (v. 10), where Moses took credit alongside God. Maimonides, in the *Shemonah Perakim* (the introduction to *Pirqe Avot*), gives yet another answer: Moses lost his temper, calling the people "rebels" (*mōrîm*); for a prophet of his stature, even anger is a failure of modeling. Sforno blends readings: Moses's haste and his misdirected word together constituted the sin. Bavli *Avodah Zarah* 2b preserves a related tradition about water-from-rock miracles. Bamidbar Rabbah 19 gathers many of these readings into a florilegium. Leibowitz's *Studies in Bamidbar* walks through the spectrum carefully, and Jonathan Sacks argues in *Covenant and Conversation* that the punishment reflects a change in the generation being led: the first generation could be commanded by demonstration of power (striking); the second generation, the one that will enter the land, must be led by persuasion (speech), and Moses, trapped in the old mode, is unfit for the new. Avivah Zornberg (*Bewilderments: Reflections on the Book of Numbers*) reads Moses's failure psychoanalytically — the accumulated exhaustion of forty years, recently compounded by Miriam's death, produces a moment of affective breakdown in which the symbol (staff as tool) overrides the instruction (voice as medium).

The death of Aaron is narratively quieter but halakhically significant. Bavli *Rosh Hashanah* 2b-3a discusses the thirty-day mourning (v. 29) as halakhic precedent. Maimonides (*Hilchot Klei ha-Miqdash* 4:20, and *Hilchot Beit ha-Bechirah*) codifies the priestly investiture pattern: Eleazar is invested with Aaron's garments before Aaron dies — a ritual of continuity ensuring that the high priesthood never lapses. Sifre Deuteronomy 339 notes that Aaron's death is described with the language of *nĕʾĕsap ʾel ʿammāv* (gathered to his people) — an honor not given to every patriarch.

The Edomite refusal (vv. 14–21) has a dense Jewish afterlife. Rabbinic midrash (Bamidbar Rabbah 19:17) treats Edom as a cipher for Rome and later for the Christian West; the refusal of safe passage becomes a typological memory of Jewish experience under Christendom. Rashi reads Moses's "your brother Israel" as an appeal to kinship Edom will not honor — a standing rebuke.

**Christian reception**: Origen's *Homilies on Numbers* 12, building on Paul's 1 Corinthians 10:4 ("the Rock was Christ"), reads Meribah typologically: the rock struck twice by Moses prefigures Christ struck by the cross, and Moses's sin in striking rather than speaking is a figural error — Christ need be struck only once (Heb 9:28, 10:10: "once for all"), so to strike twice is to symbolically deny the sufficiency of Christ's single sacrifice. This reading shapes the patristic tradition: Ambrose (*De Spiritu Sancto*) and Cyril of Alexandria develop it; Augustine, in *Contra Faustum* and in sermons on the Pentateuch, reads Moses's failure as an allegory of law without grace — Moses the representative of law can strike but cannot speak the word that gives life, because that Word belongs to Christ alone. Aquinas (*ST* III q. 46 a. 6, on the passion of Christ) cites the double striking as one of the Old Testament prefigurations of Christ's suffering, and in II-II q. 77 a. 4 discusses Moses's impatience as an instance of how a minor fault in a great soul produces great consequences. Calvin's commentary on Numbers 20 takes the striking-vs-speaking typology seriously but cautions against over-reading; he identifies the sin as a composite of anger, pride (in "shall we"), and disobedience. Luther reads Meribah as a cautionary tale about ministers who speak their own word instead of God's word.

Critical scholarship (Wellhausen, Noth, Dozeman) emphasizes the doublet with Exodus 17. Milgrom's JPS *Numbers* (Excursus 50) defends the narrative integrity of Numbers 20 and identifies Moses's sin precisely: striking when told to speak, and usurping divine agency in "shall we bring water?" Levine (AB) agrees that the Hebrew *lōʾ heʾĕmantem* locates the fault in a failure of faith with public consequences (public because the people witnessed it). Wenham (Tyndale) emphasizes the leadership-pedagogy reading. Cole (NAC) and Budd (WBC) survey the rabbinic options. Walter Brueggemann, in *The Message of the Psalms* and elsewhere, reads Moses's failure as part of the biblical pattern that leaders, however great, are never the savior; the tragedy of Moses is a theological necessity — if the exodus-leader himself entered the land, the land would become his achievement rather than God's gift. Terence Fretheim (*Numbers*) highlights the pastoral theology of leaders held to exacting standards "because" of their greater responsibility. Abraham Heschel (*The Prophets*) read the episode as paradigmatic of the tension between prophetic pathos and prophetic error: the prophet bears God's emotion publicly, but exactly that public visibility makes any misrepresentation of God disastrous.

**Convergence and divergence**: Jewish tradition keeps the focus on Moses himself — what exactly did he do, and what does it teach about leadership, anger, and faith? Christian tradition, while preserving that question, rapidly typologizes the rock as Christ (1 Cor 10:4) and reads Moses's failure as pointing beyond itself to what only Christ can do. Both traditions agree, strikingly, on two points: the punishment fits the severity of the sin because of Moses's unique public station; and the narrative refuses to let Moses be a hero. Where they diverge most sharply is in the afterlife of the rock: Paul's reading that "the rock was Christ" makes the Meribah rock itself a Christological figure, which has no direct Jewish analogue.

## Lens 4 — The Theological Intent

**Tension 1: What exactly was Moses's sin?** The rabbinic spectrum maps the main possibilities. Rashi: he struck when he should have spoken. Ramban: he took credit with "shall we bring water." Maimonides: he lost his temper and insulted the congregation. Ibn Ezra: he doubted. A less intuitive position comes from Jacob Milgrom and, in a different register, from Avivah Zornberg: the "sin" is structurally underdetermined because the text itself gathers several failures (anger, usurpation, striking, unbelief) into a single indictment — not because the narrator could not decide but because the leadership failure is real when all of these come together and any one on its own would not have disqualified Moses. Steelmanning this composite position: on Rashi alone, the punishment looks disproportionate (striking a rock is a minor infraction for forty years of service); on Ramban alone, the "shall we" might be read as rhetorical rather than appropriative. But the combination — anger + appropriation + disobedience + unbelief, all performed publicly — is precisely what "failing to sanctify God in the eyes of the people" means: a leader who, in one moment, embodies the opposite of faith while using a divine sign. Read this way, Moses's sin is not any single act but a complete pattern visible to Israel in a single incident. **Where this leaves us**: the Torah's silence on which single factor counts most is not coyness but precision — the sin is the whole failure, and no reductive account catches it. Both the rabbinic and critical-theological instinct to refuse to settle on one reading is correct.

**Tension 2: Is the punishment just?** Three positions. First, the classical reading: yes — greater leaders are held to greater accounts, and Moses's public failure had public consequences that warranted a public punishment. Second, the tragic reading (Heschel, Brueggemann): no, in the ordinary moral sense — the punishment is severe beyond the sin's proportion, and the biblical writers know this; Moses's exclusion from the land is a theological necessity to prevent the exodus from becoming Moses's achievement, and the narrative's refusal to soften the verdict acknowledges the tragedy. Third, the constructive reading (Sacks, Zornberg): the punishment is fitting in a different sense — Moses was appropriate for the generation of the exodus but not for the generation of the entry; his exclusion is less a punishment than a structural retirement, and the severity is the text's way of letting Moses himself not feel the sting of succession. Steelmanning the tragic reading: the text at Deut 3:23–27 shows Moses pleading to be allowed to cross and being refused explicitly and painfully; this is not retirement, it is loss. The biblical narrative is honest about the cost — the greatest prophet in Israel is buried in Moab because he struck a rock in the wilderness, and the text does not pretend this is easy. **Where this leaves us**: the three readings correspond to three registers — moral (just), tragic (costly), and structural (fitting). All three are in the text, and flattening to any one reading (especially flattening to "just" or "merely tragic") loses what makes the Meribah story so enduring. God's holiness and Moses's greatness are both preserved, and the cost to Moses of that preservation is not hidden.

**The unresolved question**: If God's stated purpose was to be "sanctified in the eyes of the people" (v. 12), was the naming of Moses's punishment — rather than, say, a hidden inner rebuke — itself what sanctified God? The text does not say whether Moses's visible exclusion from the land was the restoration of the sanctity he had failed to demonstrate, or whether it was a secondary consequence. And Israel's reaction to the punishment is strikingly unreported: the congregation is not said to mourn, marvel, or learn. The theological work of the punishment, if any, is left to the reader's judgment.

## The Thread

Four non-obvious cross-references. First, Deuteronomy 32:48–52 and 34:1–8 — Moses's own death on Mount Nebo — mirrors Aaron's death on Mount Hor in Numbers 20:22–29 with deliberate parallels: both go up a mountain in the sight of Israel, both are buried on the edge of the land, both are mourned thirty days. The chapter's hinge-work prepares Moses's own end. Second, Psalm 106:32–33 — "they angered him at the waters of Meribah, and it went ill with Moses on their account; for they made his spirit bitter, and he spoke rashly with his lips" — distributes the blame across Israel and Moses; the psalm's reading (Moses sinned *because of* Israel's provocation) is a theological softening that Numbers itself refuses. Third, 1 Corinthians 10:4, "and all drank the same spiritual drink. For they drank from the spiritual Rock that followed them, and the Rock was Christ" — Paul picks up a rabbinic tradition (preserved in Tosefta *Sukkah* 3:11) that a well-rock followed Israel through the wilderness; in Paul's hands the rock becomes Christ, and the Meribah striking gains a Christological depth it did not have within Numbers itself. Fourth, Obadiah 1:10–14 — the prophet's oracle against Edom for "the violence done to your brother Jacob" — preserves the wound of Numbers 20:14–21 (Moses's "your brother Israel" rebuffed) and re-opens it centuries later at a new national injury, showing that the Edomite refusal at Kadesh was not forgotten in prophetic memory but carried forward as a running grievance of kin.
