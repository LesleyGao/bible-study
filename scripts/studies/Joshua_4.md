# Joshua 4: Twelve Stones and the Question Your Children Will Ask

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

Gilgal (*gilgāl*, גִּלְגָּל, from *gālal*, "to roll") sits on the western floor of the Jordan valley, roughly two miles east of Jericho, about 850 feet below sea level. The site has never been conclusively identified — several tells (Khirbet el-Mafjar area, Khirbet en-Nitla, Tell Eshtori) have been proposed, but no single location has produced the kind of ceremonial material one would expect of a major early Iron I cultic center. The absence is itself historically suggestive; a *gilgāl* was a circle of stones, a ritual monument type known from across the Levant and Transjordan, typically not a settlement. The chapter's reference to the twelve stones being at Gilgal "to this day" (4:9 of the in-river set, and the Gilgal set implicitly in 4:20) is the Deuteronomistic Historian's signature phrase (*ʿad hayyôm hazzeh*, עַד הַיּוֹם הַזֶּה), which occurs some eighty-three times in the Hebrew Bible, clustered heavily in Joshua, Judges, and Samuel–Kings. The phrase functions as an appeal to living, visible evidence at the time of writing — a date that, depending on one's view of the Dtr composition history, could be Josianic (late 7th c. BCE, per Cross's Dtr1) or exilic (6th c. BCE, per Cross's Dtr2), meaning the stones were at least thought to be visible as late as the monarchy's late decades.

Adam Zertal's extensive Manasseh hill country survey produced one candidate for Gilgal that has provoked strong debate: a series of oval foot-shaped stone enclosures Zertal identified in the Jordan valley and Samarian hills, which he termed "*gilgal*-shaped sites." Zertal's 2008 article "The 'Foot' Ceremonies in Ancient Israel" (published posthumously in fuller form in *The Manasseh Hill Country Survey*, vol. 3) argued these enclosures were the Iron I Israelite ceremonial centers referenced in Joshua 4 and Deuteronomy 11. The interpretation is contested; Israel Finkelstein and others have argued the structures may be later or serve other functions. No consensus has formed. What is clear is that the tradition of commemorative stone circles was widespread in the region — the Rogem Hiri / Gilgal Refaim site on the Golan Heights, with its 40,000 basalt stones in concentric circles, dates to the Early Bronze Age and demonstrates the deep antiquity of the cultural form.

The chapter's chronology is precise: the people crossed on the tenth day of the first month (4:19), which in the Jewish ceremonial calendar is the day each household was to select its Passover lamb (Exod 12:3). Four days later Israel will observe Passover at Gilgal (Josh 5:10). The deliberate parallel to the exodus is calendric, not just typological.

Authorship and dating: Joshua 4 is a showcase chapter for the Deuteronomistic History debate. Martin Noth (1943) assigned it to his single-exilic Dtr. Frank Moore Cross's 1973 double-redaction model assigned the core to Dtr1 (Josianic), with minor exilic retouching. Alexander Rofé and Richard Nelson have both argued that the chapter's internal doublings — two sets of twelve stones (4:3, 8 and 4:9), two "when your children ask" teachings (4:6-7 and 4:21-24), two accounts of the ark's passage (4:11 and 4:15-18) — reflect compositional layering rather than a single author's rhetorical repetition. Trent Butler (1983 Word commentary) and A. Graeme Auld (*Joshua: Jesus Son of Nauē in Codex Vaticanus*, 2005) argue instead for deliberate narrative doubling, noting that the LXX preserves essentially the same doublings, which would be odd if the text were layered over time.

Five Hebrew terms organize the chapter. First, *zikkārôn* (זִכָּרוֹן, "memorial," 4:7), from the root *zākar* (to remember) — the same word used for the Passover in Exod 12:14. Memory in Hebrew is not passive recall but active present-tense re-enactment. Second, *ʾôt* (אוֹת, "sign," 4:6), the same word used for the Passover blood (Exod 12:13) and for the rainbow (Gen 9:12-13) — a public, covenantal semiotic. Third, the phrase *kî yišʾālûn bĕnêkem māḥār* (כִּי יִשְׁאָלוּן בְּנֵיכֶם מָחָר, "when your children ask tomorrow," 4:6, 21), a distinctive pedagogical formula. Its cousin *mah zōʾt* ("what is this?", Exod 13:14) and *mah hāʿēdôt* ("what are these testimonies?", Deut 6:20) appear in the Passover and Shema contexts; all three became the four-questions structure of the Passover Haggadah. Fourth, *maṣṣēvâ* (מַצֵּבָה, "standing stone") versus *gal* (גַּל, "heap") versus the twelve stones set up — the chapter deliberately avoids *maṣṣēvâ*, which by Deut 16:22 has become forbidden, and uses instead neutral language for erected stones. Fifth, *wayyāqem* (וַיָּקֶם, "and he set up," 4:9, 20), the verb used for covenant-making — to set up stones is to establish covenant.

## Lens 2 — The Literature

Genre is etiological memorial narrative: a story that explains why a visible feature exists. The closest biblical analogues are Gen 28:18-22 (Jacob's pillar at Bethel), Gen 31:45-52 (Jacob and Laban's heap of witness), and 1 Sam 7:12 (Samuel's Ebenezer stone). Etiology has been read skeptically since Hermann Gunkel — as a sign that the narrative post-dates and retrojectively explains a feature it finds already present — but the more sophisticated position (developed by Brevard Childs in *Memory and Tradition in Israel*, 1962) is that etiology is theologically constitutive, not merely narratively convenient: the point is to tie present witness to past redemption.

The chapter's literary architecture is doubled in a way that has puzzled readers since antiquity. Verses 1-8 describe twelve stones taken from the Jordan and set up at Gilgal (though the setting-up is deferred to 4:20). Verse 9 abruptly introduces a *second* set of twelve stones set up *in the middle* of the Jordan — "and they are there to this day." Verses 10-18 describe the final crossing and the ark's emergence. Verses 19-24 restate the Gilgal memorial. Are there two sets of stones? The plain reading of MT is yes. LXX, Vulgate, and Targum preserve the double set. Some medieval Jewish commentators (Radak, David Kimhi, 12th-13th c.) held there was only one set and read 4:9 as the stones-in-Jordan being the same stones later brought to Gilgal; others (Rashi, Ramban) accept two sets. The Qumran 4QJosh^a reorders chapters 3-8 but the fragmentary state leaves the stones question unresolved.

The chapter's most distinctive literary device is the *inclusio* of question-and-answer. Both 4:6-7 and 4:21-24 frame the stones as answer to a future child's question. Danna Fewell and David Gunn (*Gender, Power, and Promise*, 1993) argue the chapter is self-consciously meta-narrative: the text is a memorial that names itself as memorial, knowing it will be read by future generations asking the very question it predicts. The text narrates its own reception.

Robert Alter notes the alternation of divine voice (4:1-3, 15-16), Joshua's voice (4:4-7, 17, 21-24), and narrator's voice (4:8-14, 18-20). The chapter is a layered liturgy. Thomas Dozeman (Anchor Yale, 2015) observes that the repetition of "from the midst of the Jordan" (*mittôk hayyardēn*, five occurrences) is the chapter's dominant topographic marker: the stones come from the deep water's heart, from the place where the river should have been at full flood.

Textual variants: the LXX of Joshua 4 is slightly fuller than MT at several points, with small clarifying additions (particularly at 4:10). 4QJosh^a places an Ebal altar episode (from ch. 8 in MT) earlier in the narrative, which would reposition the whole Gilgal-to-Ai sequence. Eugene Ulrich (*The Dead Sea Scrolls and the Origins of the Bible*, 1999) has argued the Qumran order may be original and MT's order a later reorganization. If so, the stones-memorial of Joshua 4 originally stood in a different narrative position. The implication for reading is that the Gilgal memorial is fluidly situated in Israel's memory of the crossing; it is not fixed to a single narrative moment.

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

In the canonical arc from creation to exile, Joshua 4 does something distinctive: it takes a moment of divine action and deliberately converts it into a teaching mechanism. The chapter is about the invention of transmission. How will the second generation — the one that actually crossed — make this crossing available to the third generation, who will never have seen it? The stones are the answer. The chapter is the Hebrew Bible's most self-conscious piece of pedagogy.

### Jewish Interpretive Trajectory

Second Temple exegesis of Joshua 4 was modest; Josephus (*Antiquities* 5.1.4) gives a straightforward retelling with a single set of twelve stones visible "until this day." Ben Sira does not mention the stones. The *Dead Sea Scrolls* preserve references to Gilgal but no extended meditation on the stones.

Rabbinic treatment is where the chapter blooms. *Mishnah Sotah* 7:5 and *Tosefta Sotah* 8:1-9 take Joshua 4 as the juridical foundation for the later Ebal-Gerizim inscription of the Torah (Deut 27, Josh 8), constructing a halakhic chain: the Jordan stones prefigure the Gerizim-Ebal stones, both commemorative. *Talmud Sotah* 35b-36a debates whether the twelve stones in the Jordan were still visible, and whether the stones at Gilgal could be identified. The *Mekhilta de-Rabbi Ishmael* on Exod 13:14 ties the Joshua 4 children's-question formula directly to the Passover Haggadah's *mah nishtana* — the "what is different?" question children ask on seder night. The rabbinic reading sees Joshua 4 as the template for all Israelite pedagogy of redemption: the child's question is the hinge of transmission.

Rashi (11th c.) on 4:9 explains the two sets of stones: one set in the Jordan as witness to the place, one set at Gilgal for public memorial. Ramban (13th c.) emphasizes the theological precision of 4:6-7 — the stones are not for the generation who saw, but for the children who did not. The stones are in the future tense. Ibn Ezra is characteristically attentive to the geography. Abarbanel develops the pedagogical theme: the stones exist because Torah knows that miracles fade and must be retrieved.

Modern Jewish reading has made Joshua 4 central to discussions of memory. Yosef Hayim Yerushalmi's *Zakhor: Jewish History and Jewish Memory* (1982) opens with a meditation on the Deuteronomic imperative "remember" (*zākôr*) as Judaism's constitutive command, and Joshua 4 is one of his paradigm texts: the stones are not about the past but about the constitutive act of remembering that makes future Jews Jewish. Martin Buber (*Moses*) reads the crossing-stones as the mechanism by which a generation's experience becomes another generation's faith. Jon Levenson, in *The Death and Resurrection of the Beloved Son* (1993), treats Joshua 4 as a meditation on the difference between witnessing and inheriting — and argues Judaism's genius is to refuse the difference, to make the inheritor a witness via ritual repetition. Avivah Zornberg reads the children's-question formula as the origin of the Jewish child's permission to interrogate tradition.

### Christian Interpretive Trajectory

Patristic readers mined the chapter for symbolic numerology and typological reference. Origen's *Homilies on Joshua* (homily 4) reads the twelve stones as the twelve apostles, transported from the flowing waters (baptism) to the promised land (the church). The twelve-in-water and twelve-on-land doubling becomes the two covenants, the stones in the Jordan representing the prophets and patriarchs who remained in the old, the stones at Gilgal representing the apostles who entered the new. Jerome's *Epistle* 53 to Paulinus uses the stones as emblem of scripture itself: stones taken from the depths, carried on the shoulders, set up for instruction. Augustine (*City of God* 16.43 and *Tractates on John* 4.12) reads the stones as monuments to grace; the chapter's insistence on the children's question anchors Augustine's teaching that faith is taught, not invented.

Medieval exegesis continued the typology. The *Glossa Ordinaria* on Joshua 4 compiles the patristic readings. Aquinas (*Summa Theologiae* II-II, q. 94, a. 1, on religion) uses Joshua 4 as an example of legitimate external signs in worship, a contrast to idolatrous images.

The Reformation split sharply on the chapter. Protestant iconoclasm was uncomfortable with the paradigm of erected stones. Calvin (*Commentary on Joshua*, 1564) on 4:9 distinguishes memorial stones from idols: the stones were instructive, not devotional. The purpose was explicitly didactic — to answer the children's question — not to be worshipped. Luther similarly reads the stones as a *visual aid to faith*, not an object of faith, and on this basis defends the Lutheran retention of limited visual elements in worship against the Reformed iconoclasts. The Anabaptist tradition was more thoroughly suspicious; the stones were fine in themselves but memory should live in the community's practice, not in stones. The Catholic tradition found in Joshua 4 a biblical warrant for sacramentals — physical objects that teach and direct the mind to God without themselves being divine.

Critical scholarship repositioned the chapter. Wellhausen (1883) read the doubled stones as evidence of separate sources; Gunkel read the chapter as etiology; Noth placed it in Dtr. Brevard Childs in *Memory and Tradition in Israel* made Joshua 4 his central case for the distinctively Hebrew theology of memory as covenant-constituting act. Richard Nelson (OTL 1997) carefully examines the doublings as deliberate rhetorical emphasis.

Contemporary scholarship has been notably rich on this chapter. Danna Fewell and David Gunn read the chapter as self-aware memorial literature. L. Daniel Hawk treats it as the pivot of the conquest narrative — the moment when the wilderness generation's covenant becomes land-generation's covenant. John Goldingay (*Joshua, Judges, Ruth for Everyone*, 2011) emphasizes the chapter's pedagogical urgency: the point is the children, not the stones. Douglas Earl argues Joshua 4 is the clearest window into how the book of Joshua understood its own task — teaching Israel to be Israel by rehearsing what made Israel Israel.

Post-colonial critique has been pointed. Laura Donaldson and Musa Dube have observed that the chapter stages the indigenous Canaanite as structurally absent from the memorial: the children who will ask about the stones are Israelite children, and the stones commemorate dispossession for one people while ignoring it for another. The pedagogy the chapter inaugurates is a pedagogy of selective memory — as perhaps all nation-building pedagogy is.

**Where the traditions converge**: Jewish and Christian readings agree the stones are pedagogy, that the children's question is the chapter's hinge, that memory is constitutive of covenant identity. Both traditions read Joshua 4 as inaugurating a mode of transmission (Haggadah for Judaism, catechesis and sacramental teaching for Christianity). **Where they diverge**: Christian typology reads the twelve stones as ecclesial and Christological; Jewish reading keeps them pedagogical and geographical. Christian tradition's fondness for allegory finds many Christs in Joshua 4; Jewish tradition resists that allegorizing and keeps the stones as stones. The fundamental difference is about the status of the material object. For the Jewish reading, the stones are witness-objects whose significance is preserved by the community's ritual-telling. For the Christian tradition, the stones tend to be types superseded by the new-covenant reality they prefigure. Jewish reading honors the stones; Christian reading reads past them.

## Lens 4 — The Theological Intent

What is God doing? Building a bridge from experience to inheritance. The generation that crossed will die. The land they crossed into will be inhabited by their children, who will not have seen the waters pile up. The stones are God's mechanism for making inherited faith possible — not merely a transmission of information, but a ritual staging of the child's right to ask, and the parent's obligation to answer. The chapter is about how revelation crosses generations.

### Tension 1: Memory, Monument, and Text

How should a covenant community preserve its founding events? Joshua 4 preserves through stone; Deuteronomy insists the text be written on doorposts (Deut 6:9) and taught to children (Deut 6:7); later Judaism preserves through ritual (seder, tefillin, mezuzah); Christianity through sacrament, liturgy, and Scripture. What is the theological relation among these modes?

- **Catholic** tradition holds all three — text, sacrament, and memorial object — as complementary modes of one tradition. The CCC §1159-1162 defends sacred images and memorials as *aids to faith*; §1066-1075 treats liturgy as memorial re-enactment (*anamnesis*). Joshua 4 is a biblical precedent for sacramentality: physical objects that do not replace the reality they signify but convey it.
- **Reformed** tradition (Calvin, Zwingli, later Puritans) privileges text above monument and sacrament. Calvin permits memorial objects as teaching aids (citing Joshua 4 explicitly) but insists they are strictly pedagogical, not channels of grace. Protestant iconoclasm in the 16th and 17th centuries took Deut 16:22's prohibition of *maṣṣēvâ* as controlling, pushing back against medieval sacramental readings.
- **Lutheran** tradition holds the middle: text is primary, sacrament (baptism and eucharist) is real means of grace, memorial objects (crucifixes, images) are permissible and useful but not *means*.
- **Anabaptist** tradition radicalizes the Reformed move: memory lives in the community's practice of discipleship, not in physical monuments. The stones of Joshua 4 are tolerated but not emulated; the early Anabaptists generally resisted commemorative church architecture.
- **Orthodox** tradition's theology of the icon, developed definitively by John of Damascus (*On the Divine Images*, 8th c.) and affirmed at the Second Council of Nicaea (787 CE), treats physical objects as legitimate carriers of divine presence when sanctified by the tradition. Joshua 4 is a biblical precedent for what becomes, in full Orthodox theology, an ontology of sacred matter.
- **Jewish** reading, especially rabbinic, privileges text-and-ritual: Torah is the primary memorial (Deut 6:6-9), seder ritual is its enacted commentary, stones are a minor third-order memorial. The Talmudic instinct (*Sotah* 35b-36a) is less interested in whether the stones still exist than in what the Haggadic tradition does with their memory.
- **Critical** scholarship brackets the theological question and reads each mode — text, monument, ritual — as competing strategies of cultural reproduction in ancient Israel, eventually harmonized in Second Temple practice.

Steelmanning the iconoclast position, which is least intuitive to sacramentally-minded readers: the iconoclast worries that physical objects of memory always tend, over generations, to *replace* what they were meant to recall. Gideon's ephod became idol (Judg 8:27); the bronze serpent Moses made became idol and was destroyed by Hezekiah (2 Kgs 18:4); the temple itself became idol in Jeremiah's sermon (Jer 7). The iconoclast points to Joshua 4's insistence that the stones only make sense when paired with the *spoken* answer to the child's question — the moment the stones stand alone, uninterpreted, they cease to be what Joshua set them up to be. The Protestant concern is not aesthetic but historical: memory without interpretation petrifies into idol. The Protestant reads Joshua 4 as instituting a memorial that is only safe because it is married to an ongoing verbal practice.

**Where this leaves us:** The chapter itself resists any pure mode. Stones without question are just rocks. Question without stones is ungrounded. Text without community forgets the stones ever mattered. The chapter's pedagogy requires all three together — which is probably why no single Christian tradition has felt fully satisfied by any one of its moves.

### Tension 2: The Pedagogy of Inherited Faith

The stones are for the children. What does it mean for faith to be inherited? Is inherited faith the same thing as first-generation faith?

- **Catholic** reading through the tradition of catechesis and the sacrament of confirmation affirms that inherited faith is genuine faith. Baptism (often in infancy) inaugurates; catechesis forms; confirmation ratifies. The CCC §1229-1233 holds that faith can be truly one's own even when initially received from parents and community.
- **Reformed** reading (especially through the doctrine of the covenant of grace, and infant baptism as covenant sign) similarly affirms inherited faith but emphasizes the necessity of personal appropriation. The Westminster Confession insists that faith is the gift of God to individuals, though given through ordinary means that include family and community.
- **Lutheran** reading holds a high view of the transmission of faith through family, catechism (Luther's *Small Catechism* is literally a family pedagogy), and church, while maintaining that faith is always personally given by the Holy Spirit.
- **Anabaptist / Baptist** reading is sharply different. Faith is personal, voluntary, the result of conscious adult commitment. Children inherit the culture of faith but not faith itself; each generation must be convicted and converted freshly. Believer's baptism, rather than infant baptism, reflects this theology. The Anabaptist reading of Joshua 4 would emphasize that the stones prompt the child's *question*, not the child's automatic inheritance — each generation must ask, and must hear, afresh.
- **Wesleyan** reading tends to affirm the possibility of inherited faith while insisting on the necessity of the new birth and sanctifying experience.
- **Orthodox** reading, through the theology of *chrismation* and the family as *ecclesia domestica*, affirms that faith is genuinely inherited and genuinely personal — the tradition does not pose the question as alternatives.
- **Jewish** reading, through the Haggadah's *mah nishtana*, honors the question as the essence of Jewish identity. The child who asks is already Jewish; the asking is itself the inheritance. Ramban and later Levinas have both emphasized that Jewish identity is constituted by the question-and-teaching relationship, which means inherited faith is not lesser but is precisely what Jewish identity means.
- **Critical scholarship** often brackets the theological question but notes anthropologically that the pedagogy of Joshua 4 is the universal structure of cultural reproduction — every tradition depends on a ritual mechanism for making its founding events available to later generations.

Steelmanning the Anabaptist suspicion of inherited faith: the concern is not ingratitude to parents but spiritual honesty. A faith inherited without confrontation becomes a cultural marker rather than a relationship to God. The strongest Anabaptist case is that the children of faith can only be helped by a tradition that refuses to pretend their parents' faith is automatically theirs. Joshua 4's genius, on this reading, is precisely that the stones do *not* presume the child's faith; they create the occasion for the child's question, which is the moment the child becomes a possible believer. Inherited faith that never passes through the moment of questioning never becomes believed.

**Where this leaves us:** Joshua 4 refuses the dichotomy. The stones are for children who have not seen, and the children's question is the way they come to see. Inheritance and appropriation are not alternatives but phases of a single movement that the chapter constructs.

**One unresolved question:** What happens when the stones are gone? The chapter says "they are there to this day" — but they are not. No one today can point to them. Gilgal is archaeologically obscure, and the in-river stones (if they were ever physical) have long since been buried by silt or carried away. If memory depends on the stones, and the stones are gone, what has carried the memory forward? Is it the text itself — Joshua 4 as the stones that replaced the stones — that is now the memorial? And if so, what does that mean for any claim that physical objects are essential carriers of sacred memory?

## The Thread

Exodus 12:26-27 and 13:14-15 — the Passover pedagogy: "And when your children say to you, 'What do you mean by this service?' you shall say, 'It is the sacrifice of the LORD's Passover.'" The identical question-and-answer structure that frames Joshua 4 is the structure of the Passover seder. When you read the first Passover, remember that Joshua 4 is deliberately echoing it, and when later Jews create the Haggadah, they are completing a liturgy the Torah started.

Deuteronomy 6:20-25 — the Shema's own instruction: "When your son asks you in time to come, 'What is the meaning of the testimonies and the statutes and the rules that the LORD our God has commanded you?'" The same formula, now covering the whole Torah. When you reach Deuteronomy 6, remember Joshua 4 is the narrative realization of Deuteronomy's pedagogical theory.

1 Samuel 7:12 — Samuel sets up a stone called Ebenezer ("stone of help"), saying, "Till now the LORD has helped us." A later memorial stone, doing what Joshua's stones did, in the language Joshua's chapter made possible. When you reach Samuel's memorial, remember it is a genre Joshua 4 institutes.

Matthew 16:18 — "On this rock I will build my church." When you reach Jesus's naming of Peter as *petra*, remember that the Hebrew Bible has a long tradition of stones that carry meaning forward — and that Jesus's claim to found a community on a rock is, on that tradition, a claim to institute a new memorial mechanism. The stones of Joshua 4 are "there to this day"; Jesus says the gates of Hades will not prevail against his.
