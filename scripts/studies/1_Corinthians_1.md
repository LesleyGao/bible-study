# 1 Corinthians 1: The Cross as Counter-Wisdom

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

- **Political/social situation**: Roman Corinth was not the Greek polis Mummius destroyed in 146 BC. It was a Roman colony—Colonia Laus Iulia Corinthiensis—refounded by Julius Caesar in 44 BC and populated initially with freedmen, veterans, and merchants. By Paul's day (mid-first century AD) it was the capital of the Roman province of Achaia, controlling the Isthmus and its two ports (Lechaion on the Corinthian Gulf, Cenchreae on the Saronic). The biennial Isthmian Games drew crowds from across the Mediterranean and supplied Paul with the athletic imagery he later mobilizes (9:24-27). The older Strabo line about "a thousand sacred prostitutes" on the Acrocorinth has been firmly qualified by Bruce Winter (After Paul Left Corinth, 2001) and Jerome Murphy-O'Connor (St. Paul's Corinth, rev. 2002): that description belonged to fifth-century BC Greek Corinth, not the Roman city Paul knew. What Paul actually faced was a status-saturated colonial society organized around patron-client networks. Gerd Theissen (The Social Setting of Pauline Christianity, 1982) and Wayne Meeks (The First Urban Christians, 1983) document how the Corinthian church was internally stratified—a minority of relatively well-off patrons (Erastus, Gaius, Stephanas, Crispus) alongside a majority of poor laborers, slaves, and freedpersons.
- **Material culture**: The Asclepieion at Corinth, with its anatomical votives—terracotta hands, feet, eyes, genitalia hung on the walls in thanks for healing—gives a vivid backdrop to Paul's later body talk in chapter 12. Roman household religion, dining in the triclinium with seating ranked by status, and the Isis and Demeter cults all set the cultural temperature. Manual labor was widely despised among the cultivated; Paul's tentmaking (Acts 18:3) marked him in elite eyes as déclassé—an important fact to keep in the room when reading 1:26-29.
- **Authorship & dating**: Universally accepted as authentic Pauline. Paul writes from Ephesus (1 Cor 16:8), c. AD 53-54. The Gallio inscription at Delphi (Fouilles de Delphes III.4 286) dates Gallio's proconsulship to AD 51-52 and anchors Pauline chronology. This is at minimum the second letter Paul has sent to Corinth, since 5:9 references a prior letter (sometimes labeled "Letter A," now lost). The co-sender Sosthenes (1:1) is plausibly the synagogue ruler beaten before Gallio in Acts 18:17—Paul's antagonist turned coworker, though the identification is not certain.
- **Archaeological & textual evidence**: P46 (c. AD 200, Chester Beatty) is our earliest substantial witness, with P11, P14, P15 supplementing; the great fourth-century uncials Sinaiticus and Vaticanus carry the chapter cleanly. The Erastus pavement inscription, still in situ near the theater—"Erastus, in return for his aedileship, laid this pavement at his own expense" (ERASTVS PRO AEDILIT[AT]E S P STRAVIT)—names a high-ranking civic official whom many (including Theissen, with caution from Meggitt) identify with the Erastus of Rom 16:23. The bema seat where Paul stood before Gallio (Acts 18:12-17) is still visible in the forum.
- **Key terms**: *schismata* (σχίσματα, "splits, tears" — the metaphor is from torn cloth), *kerygma* (κήρυγμα, "proclamation, the thing preached"), *mōria* (μωρία, "folly, idiocy" — the root of "moron"), *sophia tou kosmou* (σοφία τοῦ κόσμου, "wisdom of the world"), *klēsis* (κλῆσις, "calling").

## Lens 2 — The Literature

- **Genre**: Paraenetic / problem-solving letter. Margaret Mitchell's monograph *Paul and the Rhetoric of Reconciliation* (1991) reclassified the whole letter as a sustained piece of *deliberative rhetoric* arguing for *homonoia* (concord) against factionalism—the standard genre of ancient civic speeches against stasis. Ben Witherington reads the letter rhetorically along similar lines; Hans Conzelmann (Hermeneia, 1969) and Anthony Thiselton (NIGTC, 2000) both take chapter 1 as the *exordium* and *propositio* (1:10) of the whole argument. Thiselton emphasizes the speech-act dimension—Paul is not just naming factions but performing reconciliation by speaking.
- **Devices**: The chapter's literary architecture is tight. The salutation (1:1-3) is unusually loaded with vocabulary that becomes thematic ("called," "sanctified," "saints"). The thanksgiving (1:4-9) is, in Schubert's classic analysis of Pauline thanksgivings, a foreshadowing of issues to come—Paul thanks God for *charismata* and *gnōsis* precisely because these are the very things being abused. The propositio at 1:10 ("that all of you agree, and that there be no divisions among you") states the deliberative thesis. 1:18-25 deploys diatribe ("Where is the wise? Where is the scribe?") and OT proof-texting (Isa 29:14 LXX at 1:19). 1:26-31 is a tightly chiastic vice/honor inversion. 1:30-31 quotes Jer 9:24 ("let the one who boasts, boast in the Lord") to close the unit.
- **Locate in argument**: Chapter 1 sets the foundation for everything that follows. The factionalism of 1:10-17 is the diagnosed symptom; the misjudged "wisdom" of 1:18-31 is the underlying disease; chapters 2-4 expand the diagnosis; chapters 5-16 apply the cure to particular cases. Without 1:18-31, the rest of the letter has no theological spine.
- **Textual variants**: 1:14-16 contains one of Paul's rare admissions of faulty memory ("Beyond that, I do not know whether I baptized anyone else"). The text is stable but theologically arresting—an apostle conceding he doesn't remember. At 1:14 some Western witnesses omit "I thank God"; the variant is widely judged secondary. 1:2's "called to be saints" (*klētois hagiois*) is textually firm but exegetically rich—the same root *kale-* recurs eight times in the chapter. At 1:8 "the day of our Lord Jesus Christ" has minor variation in the divine names but no meaning-affecting change.

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

**Christian Interpretive Trajectory**

- Patristic: Origen's commentary survives only in fragments (Greek catenae and Latin citation by Jerome) but his reading of 1:24 ("Christ the power of God and the wisdom of God") seeded the high Christology of the East. John Chrysostom devoted forty-four homilies to 1 Corinthians; Homily 3 on 1:10 treats schism as the gravest ecclesial sin and reads the cross as the great leveler of social rank. Theodoret of Cyrus and Ambrosiaster (the anonymous Latin commentator long misattributed to Ambrose) both stress the Jewish-Gentile balance in 1:22-24. Pelagius's commentary (preserved in Souter's edition) reads 1:26-31 against aristocratic complacency. Augustine (Confessions, City of God, anti-Pelagian works) turns 1:26-31 into the rhetorical center of his doctrine of grace.
- Medieval: Aquinas's *Super Primam Epistolam ad Corinthios* (lectures at Paris) reads 1:24 in light of the divine processions—Christ as eternal Wisdom—and treats 1:30 as foundational for the doctrine of imputed and imparted righteousness held together. The *Glossa Ordinaria* preserves the patristic harmonization.
- Reformation: Luther's lectures and his 1518 Heidelberg Disputation crystallize *theologia crucis* against *theologia gloriae* directly out of 1:18-25; this chapter is arguably the engine of Luther's whole theological method. Calvin's 1546 commentary on 1 Corinthians treats 1:30 as the hinge of the *duplex gratia*—justification and sanctification given together in Christ.
- Modern critical: F.C. Baur set the agenda by reading 1:12 ("I follow Paul... Cephas... Christ") as evidence for his Petrine-Pauline antithesis in early Christianity; later scholarship (Munck, Barrett, Mitchell) has substantially dismantled that reconstruction. Johannes Weiss's Meyer commentary (1910) remains a benchmark for source-critical work. C.K. Barrett's BNTC (1968), Hans Conzelmann's Hermeneia (1969), Gordon Fee's NICNT (1987, revised 2014, Pentecostal sensibilities visible throughout), Anthony Thiselton's NIGTC (2000, the most thorough English-language commentary), Joseph Fitzmyer's Anchor Yale (2008, Catholic and philological), David Garland's BECNT (2003, evangelical), Richard Hays's Interpretation (1997, ecclesial), Roy Ciampa & Brian Rosner's Pillar (2010, biblical-theological), Alan Johnson's NIVAC (pastoral), and Raymond Collins's Sacra Pagina (Catholic literary) each leave fingerprints on contemporary readings. On the social side, Theissen, Meeks, Dale Martin (*The Corinthian Body*, 1995), David Horrell, and Justin Meggitt (*Paul, Poverty, and Survival*, 1998) debate whether the Corinthian community was internally stratified (Theissen, Meeks) or uniformly poor (Meggitt). Rhetorical: Mitchell, Frank Hughes, Witherington, Duane Watson. Feminist/gender: Antoinette Wire (*The Corinthian Women Prophets*, 1990) reads the wisdom rhetoric of chapter 1 as Paul's bid to rein in pneumatic women already empowered by Apollos's preaching; Schüssler Fiorenza and Cynthia Long Westfall extend the argument. New Perspective: E.P. Sanders, Dunn, N.T. Wright, with Mark Nanos and Pamela Eisenbaum pushing the Paul-within-Judaism line further. Apocalyptic Paul: J. Louis Martyn, Beverly Gaventa, Douglas Campbell read 1:18-25 as cosmic-revelatory rupture rather than wisdom-tradition critique.

**Jewish Interpretive Trajectory**

- Daniel Boyarin (*A Radical Jew*, 1994), Paula Fredriksen (*Paul, the Pagans' Apostle*, 2017), and Mark Nanos read Paul as a Jewish thinker addressing Gentile *ekklesiai* in continuity with Israel's prophetic critique of human pretension. The "stumbling block to Jews" of 1:23 is read against Deut 21:23 ("a hanged man is cursed by God"), which 11QTemple 64 in the Dead Sea Scrolls applied specifically to crucifixion—making Jewish revulsion at a crucified Messiah halakhically grounded, not mere prejudice. Paul is wrestling with Israel's own wisdom tradition (Proverbs, Sirach, Wisdom of Solomon 7-9 especially) when he claims that *sophia* now centers on the cross—a move that radicalizes Israel's critique of Gentile wisdom while turning the same critical edge on Israel's own scribes.
- Maimonides on the Noahide commandments and the gentile righteous (*Mishneh Torah*, Hilkhot Melakhim 8.11) provides a different framework for Gentile inclusion; modern post-Holocaust dialogue (Krister Stendahl, Jacob Neusner's *A Rabbi Talks with Jesus*, the Boyarin-Fredriksen line) increasingly resists supersessionist readings of 1:18-31 in which the cross "replaces" Torah wisdom.

**Where the traditions converge and diverge**: They converge on the sociological observation—God's redemptive action subverts elite expectation. They diverge sharply on whether the cross *fulfills* Israel's wisdom (most Christian readings) or *displaces* it (older supersessionist readings, now widely repudiated) or *radicalizes* it from within (Boyarin, Wright, Hays). The deeper divergence is christological: 1:24's "Christ the power of God and the wisdom of God" is a Christian high-christological reading of pre-existent Wisdom (Prov 8, Wis 7) that no Jewish interpreter accepts as descriptive of Jesus.

## Lens 4 — The Theological Intent

What Paul believed God was doing in this chapter is best captured as a *re-grounding of identity*. The Corinthians had begun sorting themselves by allegiance to teachers as if those teachers were the source of their status. Paul's response is not to call for politeness across faction lines but to dissolve the metric by which they were ranking themselves at all. The cross, he argues, has revealed that God's economy of value is inverted relative to the world's—and therefore the entire game of comparing wisdoms and powers is over before it begins. Two genuine theological tensions emerge.

**Tension 1: How does the cross actually save?** 1:18 ("the word of the cross is the power of God") and 1:30 ("Christ Jesus became to us wisdom from God, righteousness and sanctification and redemption"—widely read as a pre-Pauline confessional fragment) demand an account of *what is happening on the cross*. The atonement traditions diverge:

- *Catholic* (CCC 599-618; Aquinas ST IIIa qq. 46-49): satisfaction. Christ's voluntary suffering, of infinite worth because of his divine person, satisfies the debt owed by sin. Aquinas inherits Anselm but blends in merit and exemplary causation.
- *Reformed* (Calvin, Inst. II.16-17; Charles Hodge; J.I. Packer): penal substitution. Christ bears the legal punishment due to sin; God's justice is propitiated by transferring the penalty to Christ. The Reformed read 1:30 as imputation of Christ's righteousness.
- *Lutheran*: shares penal substitution but adds Luther's *theologia crucis*—God's chosen mode of self-revelation is hiddenness in suffering, which is precisely the point of 1:18-25. Lutheran soteriology is more christological than juridical at its core.
- *Wesleyan/Arminian*: governmental theory (Grotius's revision) is common, with the cross demonstrating God's hatred of sin and securing the moral conditions for forgiveness.
- *Anabaptist*: cross as nonviolent unmasking of the powers (J. Denny Weaver's *Nonviolent Atonement*); discipleship under the cross.
- *Eastern Orthodox* (Athanasius, *On the Incarnation*; Lossky, *Mystical Theology*): recapitulation and theosis. Christ's death-and-resurrection heals human nature and reopens the path to participation in divine life. Christus Victor (Aulén's recovery of the patristic motif) is closer to the Orthodox grain than satisfaction.
- *Critical/academic*: Fleming Rutledge (*The Crucifixion*, 2015) argues the NT actually holds multiple metaphors together; J. Louis Martyn and Beverly Gaventa read apocalyptically—the cross as cosmic invasion that defeats the powers (Sin, Death, Flesh as personified rulers).

The position least intuitive to most modern readers is satisfaction in its strict Anselmian form. *Steelmanned*: Anselm is not asserting a vindictive God needing blood. He is arguing that God's holiness is identical with God's being, that sin is an offense against the order of being itself, and that no created thing can repair an offense of infinite magnitude. The God-man alone can offer satisfaction commensurate with the wound. This is, in its own logic, a deeply *ontological* account—not a transactional one—and it sits closer to Orthodox recapitulation than its Reformed inheritors usually admit. Where this leaves us: 1:18-25 will not yield a single atonement theory. Paul has refused to systematize, and the church has paid the price of treating him as if he had.

**Tension 2: Election, calling, and the freedom of God.** 1:26-29 is the most concentrated election text in the Pauline corpus outside Romans 9. "God *chose* what is foolish... God *chose* what is weak... God *chose* what is low and despised." The *eklegomai* verb is repeated three times and ends with "so that no human being might boast in the presence of God." The traditions diverge on what *eklegomai* means here:

- *Augustinian-Reformed* (Augustine *On the Predestination of the Saints*; Calvin Inst. III.21-24; Westminster Confession III): unconditional election. God's choice is logically prior to and the ground of human faith. 1:26-29 is read as paradigmatic—God elects without regard to the elected's wisdom, power, or birth, which is the same as without regard to merit.
- *Wesleyan/Arminian*: conditional election based on God's foreknowledge of faith. The "calling" of 1:26 is corporate (a calling of communities) rather than effectual at the individual level. Prevenient grace makes the response possible; free response actualizes the calling.
- *Catholic* (CCC 600-602; Trent Session 6; Vatican II *Lumen Gentium*): predestination *ad gloriam*—God positively wills the salvation of all and predestines those who freely cooperate with grace, with Aquinas's careful distinction between predestination (positive) and reprobation (permissive). Trent rejected double predestination.
- *Eastern Orthodox*: synergism (sun-ergeia). Salvation is a cooperation between divine grace and human freedom; the language of "election" is descriptive of God's foreknowledge and invitation rather than determinative decree. Lossky reads 1:26-29 as the apostolic critique of self-sufficient *autexousion*.
- *Critical/academic*: Many recent Pauline scholars (Wright, Dunn, Gaventa) read *klēsis* in 1:26 corporately and apocalyptically—God has called this community into existence as the harbinger of the new creation, not picked individuals for heaven.

Steelmanned position least intuitive to most: Eastern Orthodox synergism. To Reformed ears it sounds Pelagian. Properly understood it is not. Synergism does not say grace and freedom contribute equal halves; it says grace is the necessary and sufficient ground, and human response is the form grace takes in a creature endowed with the divine image. The cooperation is asymmetric—grace is always prior—but real, because to deny the reality of human response is to deny the human as image-bearer. Lossky's claim is that Augustinian-Reformed monergism risks turning grace into mechanism, which is its own form of forgetting that humans are persons. Where this leaves us: 1:26-29 unambiguously roots the church's existence in God's free choice and unambiguously refuses the choice as a basis for human boasting. Whether the choice is effectual at the individual level or operative at the corporate level is exegetically underdetermined.

**One unresolved question**: If 1:18 is right that the cross *is* the power of God, and 1:24 is right that it *is* God's wisdom, then Paul has not provided a critique of human wisdom from a higher human wisdom—he has claimed that an event in the world has rewritten the categories of wisdom and power themselves. What does it mean, sociologically and politically, to live inside a community whose central claim is that the system of valuation by which the surrounding civilization runs is *literally inverted*—and is the modern church anywhere close to inhabiting that claim, or has it long since smuggled the old metric back in under new vocabulary?

## The Thread

- **1:30 → Jeremiah 9:23-24 (LXX)**: Paul closes the unit with "let the one who boasts, boast in the Lord," a near-direct quotation of Jeremiah's polemic against boasting in wisdom, might, and riches. The OT background is doing more work than most readings notice: Paul is not inventing the inversion, he is arguing the cross has fulfilled the Jeremianic prophecy.
- **1:23 ("Christ crucified, a stumbling block to Jews") → Deut 21:23 / Galatians 3:13**: The "scandal" is halakhically specific—Deut 21:23 was applied to crucifixion at Qumran (11QTemple 64), and Paul himself works the same text in Gal 3:13. Reading 1:23 without the Deuteronomic background turns it into vague offense rather than the specific theological problem it was for first-century Jews.
- **1:26-29 → Luke 1:46-55 (the Magnificat)**: Mary's song of social reversal anticipates Paul's point with the same vocabulary of low and high, weak and powerful. The connection is rarely drawn because the genres differ, but the theology is identical—God's reign upends human ranking.
- **1:9 ("God is faithful, by whom you were called into the *koinōnia* of his Son") → 2 Corinthians 13:14**: The word *koinōnia* threads through the Pauline corpus. 1 Cor 1:9 grounds the chapter's call to unity not in moral exhortation but in the prior fact of fellowship in Christ; the Trinitarian benediction at the end of 2 Corinthians develops the same instinct toward shared participation as the deepest Christian fact.
