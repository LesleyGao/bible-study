# Exodus 7: The First Blow to Egypt's Gods

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

If Ramses II is the stubborn pharaoh of the exodus narrative — the reading Kenneth Kitchen defends in *On the Reliability of the Old Testament* on the basis of the Pi-Ramesses toponym and the Merneptah Stele's presumption of Israel already in Canaan by c. 1208 BCE — then the encounter on the Nile bank of Exodus 7:15 occurs within a dense ritual ecosystem. Nineteenth-dynasty pharaohs routinely conducted morning visits to the Nile. These were not casual; they were cultic. Pharaoh, as earthly son of Ra and living Horus, participated in the morning rites by which the inundation was acknowledged and the cosmic order maintained. James Hoffmeier in *Israel in Egypt* and *Ancient Israel in Sinai* catalogs the evidence that Egyptian pharaohs performed early-morning water rituals connected to Hapi, the androgynous Nile deity whose rising flood sustained the Black Land. When YHWH instructs Moses to confront Pharaoh "in the morning, as he is going out to the water" (v. 15), the narrator is placing the confrontation precisely inside Egypt's theological calendar.

The Nile itself was Egypt, materially and religiously. Hapi, depicted with pendulous breasts and a belly of bounty, was the giver of life. Khnum, at Elephantine, was the ram-headed potter who fashioned human beings from Nile clay. Osiris was sometimes identified with the river's inundation. To strike the Nile was not to vandalize infrastructure; it was to assault a pantheon. The Egyptian magicians — the *ḥarṭummîm* (חַרְטֻמִּים), a Hebrew loanword derived from Egyptian *ḥry-tp* ("chief lector priest") — were the Pharaoh's official ritual specialists. Jan Assmann and others have shown that such priests operated at the interface of state religion and apotropaic magic, their toolkit preserved in documents like the Harris Magical Papyrus and the Leiden Magical Papyrus I 348, which record precisely the kind of serpent-charming and water-manipulating rites that the biblical narrative evokes. When Aaron's staff turns into a *tannîn*, the court magicians are the plausible professional rivals — and the biblical narrator knows this.

The source-critical analysis of chapter 7 is more complicated than chapter 6 because the narrative is woven. Martin Noth, Brevard Childs, William Propp (*Anchor Bible Exodus 1–18*), and Richard Elliott Friedman (*The Bible with Sources Revealed*) generally assign vv. 1–13 and parts of vv. 19–22 to P and parts of vv. 14–18, 23–25 to J. The tells are vocabulary and pattern: P uses Aaron-and-the-staff (v. 19: "stretch out your hand over the waters"); J uses Moses-and-the-staff (v. 17: "with the staff that is in my hand I will strike"). P has Aaron's staff become a *tannîn* (sea monster); earlier in Exodus 4, E (or J) had Moses's staff become a *nāḥāš* (ordinary snake). P has the magicians also succeed by *lāṭ* (secret arts); J has a narrower focus on the Nile itself. Cassuto rejected the documentary reading and treated apparent doublets as rhetorical amplification. Either way, the final form presents a single cycle.

Hebrew and Egyptian terms worth attention: *tannîn* (תַּנִּין), the word used for what Aaron's staff becomes (v. 9), is not the garden snake of Exodus 4 but a much larger mythic creature — the same word used in Genesis 1:21 for "great sea creatures," in Isaiah 27:1 and Psalm 74:13 for the chaos-monster YHWH defeats, and in Ezekiel 29:3 and 32:2 specifically for Pharaoh himself as a dragon in the Nile. Propp and Sarna both note that this choice deliberately evokes cosmic combat; the staff is not just becoming a snake, it is invoking Leviathan imagery in the language by which Egyptian priests described Apep/Apophis, the chaos-serpent. *Ḥarṭummîm* (חַרְטֻמִּים) has already been noted. *Lāṭ* (לָט) and *kĕšāpîm* (כְּשָׁפִים), "secret arts" and "sorceries," are the narrator's technical vocabulary for magical performance. *Dām* (דָּם) — blood — carries cultic weight: blood on the altar atones; blood on the land defiles. The Nile becoming blood is simultaneously a wound to Egypt and an indictment.

## Lens 2 — The Literature

Exodus 7 opens the plague cycle, and the cycle's structure is famously elegant. Umberto Cassuto, Nahum Sarna, and Moshe Greenberg each argued, with small variations, for a 3+3+3+1 arrangement. Plagues 1, 4, 7 are announced in the morning at the Nile; plagues 2, 5, 8 are announced at the palace; plagues 3, 6, 9 come unannounced; and the tenth plague stands alone. The first plague of chapter 7 thus inaugurates the pattern — morning, Nile bank, explicit warning. Greenberg emphasized the pedagogical structure: Pharaoh is warned at the beginning of each triad; the pattern itself is a text he could read if he would.

The chapter also introduces the hardening vocabulary that will carry the whole cycle. Three Hebrew verbs do the work, and they do it differently. *Ḥāzaq* (חָזַק), "to be strong, firm," appears in v. 13 and v. 22 as a stative passive — Pharaoh's heart "was hard" (*wayyeḥĕzaq lēḇ par'ōh*). *Kāḇēd* (כָּבֵד), "to be heavy," will appear later in the cycle. *Qāšâ* (קָשָׁה), "to be stiff," is used less often. In chapter 7 itself, the active divine causative "I will harden" (*waʾănî ʾaqšeh*, v. 3) occurs in YHWH's opening speech, but the actual chapter-level narrations in vv. 13 and 22 are stative — Pharaoh's heart was hard. Propp's *Anchor Bible* commentary makes much of this: in the first five plagues the grammar consistently describes Pharaoh's own hardening; it is only beginning in 9:12 that God is the explicit subject. This grammatical asymmetry will become central to the theological debate.

Literarily, the staff-and-serpent sign (vv. 8–13) functions as a prologue. It is not one of the ten plagues but a demonstration piece. Its narrative function is to establish that the Egyptian magicians can imitate up to a point, that YHWH's sign is greater (Aaron's staff swallows theirs), and that Pharaoh is unmoved even by the greater. This last beat — Pharaoh is unmoved by an outright victory — is the book's literary prophecy of what the plague cycle will repeat, intensified, until the final blow.

Textually, chapter 7 shows interesting variation. The LXX in v. 12 has *drakontes* (dragons) for *tannînîm* and *ophis* (snake) for *nāḥāš*, preserving the distinction Hebrew makes. The Samaritan Pentateuch expands v. 18 with the content of the warning speech, a characteristic SP harmonizing tendency. 4QExod fragments confirm the basic MT text but preserve minor orthographic variants. The LXX plague sequence as a whole diverges from the MT in later chapters more than in chapter 7, but already the Greek translators have made interpretive choices — most notably, translating *lāṭ* consistently with words for secret or hidden arts, which gave the patristic tradition a lexical foothold for talking about demonic simulation.

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

In the canonical arc, Exodus 7 is the opening of a sustained argument: YHWH is sovereign over creation and over the gods the Egyptians take for creation's custodians. The plague cycle systematically contests Egypt's theological geography — its river, its air, its soil, its sky, its livestock, its firstborn. This is not scattered judgment; it is ordered disclosure. The first plague sets the terms: Hapi fails, the Nile bleeds, and the magicians can imitate but not heal.

**Jewish interpretive trajectory**. Josephus (*Antiquities* 2.293–314) reshapes the narrative for a Greco-Roman audience, emphasizing that the magicians' replicas demonstrate the gap between human craft and divine action. Philo (*Life of Moses* 1.96–146) reads each plague as judgment in the very element by which the Egyptians had sinned, drawing on the logic of Wisdom of Solomon 11–19 that punishment is *measure for measure* (μέτρον δικαιοσύνης). Wisdom itself, a Hellenistic Jewish text, is arguably the earliest surviving theological commentary on the plague cycle. The *Mekhilta de-Rabbi Ishmael* and *Exodus Rabbah* develop the measure-for-measure theme with specifically Egyptian crimes in view — the Nile that once received drowned Hebrew infants now runs red (Exodus Rabbah 9:10). The Talmud (b. Sotah 11a–12b) explores the magicians as Jannes and Jambres, figures named in Jewish tradition who appear later in 2 Timothy 3:8. Rashi on 7:17 emphasizes that Moses does not strike the Nile, because the Nile had sheltered him as an infant; Aaron strikes. Ibn Ezra offers naturalistic observations about Nile hydrology. Ramban reads the staff-serpent sign as a direct challenge to Egyptian cosmic-snake symbolism (he knows the Apep tradition indirectly). Maimonides (*Guide* 3.32) treats the plagues as divine pedagogy accommodated to Pharaoh's capacity. Zornberg's *Particulars of Rapture* reads the cycle psychologically — each plague addresses a different layer of Pharaoh's denial. Sarna's JPS commentary marshals the comparative evidence for Egyptian religion and the archaeological plausibility of the naturalistic substrate.

**Christian interpretive trajectory**. Origen's *Homilies on Exodus* 4 reads the Nile-to-blood typologically as a figure for the transformation of the world's waters into the blood of the new covenant — with careful attention to the point that the Egyptians cannot drink this blood (it is judgment for them, life only for those who belong to God). Augustine, especially in *Contra Faustum* and in sermons, treats the magicians' replica performance as a permanent figure for heretics whose imitations of true miracles deceive. Gregory of Nyssa, *Life of Moses*, reads the plagues as the soul's purgation of its attachments to material idolatry. Chrysostom's Homilies on 2 Timothy take up the Jannes-and-Jambres tradition. Aquinas (*ST* I q. 114 on angelic influence; I q. 110 on the difference between true and apparent miracles) draws on Augustine to argue that the magicians performed real but limited manipulations of nature by demonic assistance, which God permitted up to the point needed to expose them. Calvin (*Commentary on Exodus*) reads the magicians' success as God's deliberate escalation — Pharaoh would not have been accountable if the signs were trivially beyond imitation; God lets the imitators succeed precisely to remove every excuse. Luther in the *Lectures on Genesis* and scattered sermons reads the cycle as proof that Word overcomes sign: the decisive thing is that YHWH speaks, and Egypt's best specialists cannot match the word. Modern critical: Childs (*Exodus*) balances the historical-critical reading with the canonical-theological reading, refusing to reduce the cycle to either pure legend or straight reportage. Fretheim (*Exodus*) and Brueggemann (*Theology of the Old Testament*) emphasize the creation-unraveling pattern: the plagues are the cosmos coming undone under oppression, with the Nile blood as the first sign of ecological collapse. Propp (*Anchor Bible*) provides the closest textual analysis of the sources and the Egyptian background. Houtman's three-volume *Exodus* commentary is especially strong on the history of interpretation. Goldingay (*Old Testament Theology*) reads the cycle as YHWH's self-assertion against an imperial theology that had captured creation for its own legitimation.

**Convergence and divergence**. Jewish tradition reads the plague cycle primarily as a public demonstration and as pedagogy — Pharaoh is being taught, Egypt is being shown, Israel is being trained. The measure-for-measure principle governs the logic. Christian tradition overlays a typological reading: Pharaoh becomes Satan, Egypt becomes the world under sin, the plagues become eschatological judgments, and the exodus becomes baptism and resurrection. The typology is powerful but sometimes occludes the specific Egyptian referents the Jewish tradition preserves. Both traditions struggle with the magicians' partial success — Jewish commentary tends to say God permits it to expose Pharaoh's refusal; Christian commentary tends to say the same but adds a theory of demonic power that the Hebrew text itself does not assert. On the *tannîn*, both traditions sense that something larger than a snake is happening; Ramban and Propp and Levenson converge on cosmic combat, though the Christian tradition historically has been less sensitive to this reading because it had fewer ancient Near Eastern parallels to draw on.

## Lens 4 — The Theological Intent

**Tension One: Pharaoh's hardening.** The chapter opens with YHWH's announcement that he will harden Pharaoh's heart (v. 3) but narrates the first two instances of hardening as stative — Pharaoh's heart simply was hard (vv. 13, 22). The question is how these two claims relate.

- **Reformed (Calvin, Luther, Edwards, Piper)**: Calvin in *Institutes* 2.4 reads 7:3 as the hermeneutical key: Pharaoh's own hardening is the outworking of YHWH's decretive hardening. What appears as Pharaoh's self-determination is in fact God's sovereign action manifesting itself through Pharaoh's own disposition. Luther's *Bondage of the Will* 18 argues explicitly against Erasmus that the grammar of the passage does not permit softening: God hardens Pharaoh, and Pharaoh's hardening is real volition, but volition bound by its nature. Jonathan Edwards in *Freedom of the Will* sharpens this: Pharaoh does what he wants, and what he wants is fixed by who he is. Piper and Frame among contemporary Reformed thinkers hold the same position. Steelmanned: the reading honors the explicit divine announcement, refuses to attenuate the shock of 7:3, and takes seriously that God is genuinely sovereign over the event and not merely responding to a prior human decision.
- **Catholic (Aquinas, CCC 1037)**: Aquinas (*ST* I-II q. 79 a. 3–4) distinguishes between God willing evil and God permitting evil. God does not cause Pharaoh's sin; God withholds the grace that would have softened him, and Pharaoh's own corrupted will does the rest. This preserves divine goodness while accounting for the text's language. The *Catechism* 1037 states that God predestines no one to hell, and Catholic commentators accordingly read 7:3 as God's foreknowledge and permission rather than direct causation. Steelmanned: the reading preserves human moral responsibility and divine goodness, and it tracks the grammatical fact that chapters 7–9 describe Pharaoh's own hardening before God becomes the grammatical subject in 9:12.
- **Wesleyan/Arminian (Wesley, Olson, Walls)**: Wesley in his *Notes on Exodus* reads divine hardening as God confirming Pharaoh in the path Pharaoh himself has chosen, not as God originating Pharaoh's resistance. Prevenient grace was given and refused; subsequent hardening is judicial, not determinative. Roger Olson and Jerry Walls argue that the plague cycle itself is evidence: why repeated warnings and pedagogical escalation if the outcome were predetermined? Steelmanned: the reading accounts for the sequential character of the narrative, takes the warnings as real warnings, and fits the first-five-plagues grammar where Pharaoh hardens himself.
- **Jewish (Maimonides, Rashi, Levenson)**: Maimonides in *Hilchot Teshuvah* 6:3 gives the classic Jewish reading — Pharaoh's earlier sins were so grave that God withdrew the capacity for repentance as a judicial act; this does not violate free will because the earlier exercise of free will produced the condition. Rashi takes a similar line. Levenson (*The Death and Resurrection of the Beloved Son*) reads the sequence as public theater: Pharaoh has already chosen; the plagues are not trying to change him but to display judgment. Steelmanned: the reading respects the sequence (early plagues: Pharaoh hardens; later plagues: God hardens), accounts for the asymmetry without resorting to double predestination, and has deep roots in rabbinic exegesis.

**Where this leaves us**: the chapter's grammar genuinely complicates any single-position reading; the text seems to want both divine sovereignty and human agency held in tension without a scholastic resolution.

**Tension Two: The magicians' partial success.** Egyptian magicians imitate the staff-to-serpent sign and the blood-to-Nile sign. What theological weight does this carry?

- **Augustinian / Reformed**: The magicians perform real but ultimately constrained manipulations of nature, permitted by God as a test. The text's point is not to relativize YHWH's power but to establish that mere wonder-working is not diagnostic of truth. Steelmanned: this fits the narrative pattern — the magicians will fail at plague three, and their partial success amplifies rather than undermines the final asymmetry.
- **Rabbinic / Jewish**: The magicians are working with the same natural resources and ritual techniques the Egyptian religious establishment always deployed. God permits them to succeed because their success is precisely what exposes the emptiness of the system. The fact that Aaron's staff swallows theirs is the decisive narrative moment, not the surface parity. Steelmanned: this reading stays closer to the text's own vocabulary (*lāṭ*, "secret arts") and does not require a developed angelology to explain what is happening.
- **Critical / naturalistic (Hort, Fretheim)**: Greta Hort's 1957–58 studies proposed that the Nile-to-blood plague reflects a naturalistic phenomenon — a red algae bloom (*Pfiesteria* or a dinoflagellate) associated with unusual inundation conditions — which the magicians could superficially reproduce. Fretheim develops this in a theological direction: the plagues are creation responding to the disorder of oppression; nature itself participates in judgment. Steelmanned: the naturalistic substrate is genuinely plausible, correlates with known Nile phenomena, and does not require the miracles to be less but asks what "miracle" means when God acts in and through creation rather than in violation of it.

**Where this leaves us**: the text does not require readers to choose between miracle and natural process; it requires them to see that whatever the mechanism, YHWH is the agent and the Nile is the contested site.

**Unresolved question**: If the magicians could replicate the blood-plague, what did the Egyptians and Pharaoh actually witness that was supposed to convince them that YHWH, not their own priests, was the author of the event?

## The Thread

Three cross-references that do not usually appear together.

First, **Exodus 7:9 → Ezekiel 29:3 → Revelation 12**. The *tannîn* vocabulary of Aaron's staff-turned-sea-monster recurs in Ezekiel 29:3, where YHWH calls Pharaoh "the great *tannîn* that lies in the midst of his streams, that says, 'My Nile is my own; I made it for myself.'" Ezekiel is reading Exodus 7 as cosmic combat: the staff-serpent swallowing the magicians' staff-serpents is a foreshadowing of YHWH swallowing Pharaoh the sea-monster. Revelation 12 picks up the same dragon imagery and applies it to the final defeat of the adversary, binding the plague cycle's first sign to apocalyptic cosmology.

Second, **Exodus 7:11 → 2 Timothy 3:8**. The magicians are named Jannes and Jambres by Jewish tradition (Damascus Document CD 5:18, Targum Pseudo-Jonathan), and 2 Timothy 3:8 references them by name as a warning against impostors "who oppose the truth, men corrupted in mind and disqualified regarding the faith." The intertestamental afterlife of the *ḥarṭummîm* generates both a Jewish legendary complex and a New Testament exhortation, welding Exodus 7's court scene to early Christian pastoral theology.

Third, **Exodus 7:17 → Revelation 16:4**. "I will strike the water that is in the Nile, and it shall turn into blood" reappears in the third bowl of Revelation 16:4: "the third angel poured out his bowl into the rivers and the springs of water, and they became blood." The Johannine Apocalypse deliberately restages the plague cycle as eschatological judgment, with Exodus 7's local blow against a single river expanded to a cosmic register — the rivers and the sea. The first plague becomes the template for the third bowl, and Egypt becomes a figure for every imperial order that sets itself against the lamb.
