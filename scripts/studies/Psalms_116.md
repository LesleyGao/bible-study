# Psalm 116 — A Scholarly Study

## Lens 1: The Historical World

Psalm 116 is anonymous and stands without superscription in the Masoretic Text. It belongs to Book V (Pss 107–150) and to **the Egyptian Hallel** (*Hallel Mitzri*, Pss 113–118). In the rabbinic Passover Seder codified in Mishnah Pesahim 10 and discussed in b. Pesahim 117–118, **Psalm 116 is sung after the Passover meal**, as part of the second Hallel-block (Pss 115–118) that accompanies the third and fourth cups of wine. The synoptic note that Jesus and the disciples "sang the hymn" before going to the Mount of Olives (*hymnēsantes exēlthon*, Mark 14:26 / Matt 26:30) almost certainly refers to this post-meal Hallel. **Jesus and the disciples therefore likely sang Psalm 116 — with its line "I love the LORD because he has heard my voice," its "cup of salvation," and its astonishing v. 15, "precious in the sight of the LORD is the death of his saints" — within an hour or two of his arrest in Gethsemane and his death the next afternoon.** The historical-liturgical density of this psalm in Christian theological tradition is hard to overstate.

The **Greek-Hebrew numbering divergence** is especially important here. The LXX, followed by the Vulgate, splits Hebrew Ps 116 into two psalms: LXX/Vulgate Ps 114 (= Heb 116:1–9) and LXX/Vulgate Ps 115 (= Heb 116:10–19). Pre-Nova Vulgata Catholic Bibles (Douay-Rheims, the older Roman Breviary) followed the LXX numbering; modern Catholic editions revert to the Hebrew numbering, often retaining the Vulgate numbering parenthetically. The split is not arbitrary — there is a clear formal seam between v. 9 (closing the first thanksgiving movement) and v. 10 (opening the second with "I believed, even when I spoke"). But the Masoretic redactor evidently saw these as a single composition unified by recurring motifs (the *kos yeshu'ot*, the *vows*, the *call upon the name*, the *courts of the house of the LORD*).

In Hermann Gunkel's form-critical taxonomy, Ps 116 is the prototype of the **individual thanksgiving psalm** (*Danklied des Einzelnen*) — a votive composition offered by an individual delivered from danger, recounting (a) the danger encountered, (b) the cry to Yahweh, (c) the deliverance, and (d) the public vows of thanksgiving in the temple courts. Sigmund Mowinckel placed such psalms in the *todah* (thank-offering) liturgy of Leviticus 7:11–15. Erhard Gerstenberger (*Psalms 2*, FOTL, 2001) reads Ps 116 as a votive performance script — text spoken by an individual at the offering of the *todah* sacrifice in the temple precincts, amid the gathered community.

The psalm is dated to the post-exilic period (sixth to fourth centuries BCE) on the basis of (a) the Aramaic-influenced morphology in vv. 6–7 (the relative pronoun *she-* in *gamal alaykhi*, the second-person feminine address to *nafshi*, "my soul"), (b) the explicit reference to "the courts of the house of the LORD" (v. 19), implying the Second Temple, and (c) its placement within the post-exilic Hallel.

The opening line, *ahavti ki-yishma Yahweh et-qoli tachanunay* (אָהַבְתִּי כִּי־יִשְׁמַע יְהוָה אֶת־קוֹלִי תַּחֲנוּנָי, "I love the LORD because he has heard my voice and my pleas for mercy," 116:1), uses the verb *ahav* (אהב, "to love") in an unusual construction — without an explicit object — that has prompted endless commentary. The Targum supplies the object as Yahweh; the LXX renders ἠγάπησα ὅτι ("I have loved because"), preserving the Hebrew elision. *Tachanunim* (תַּחֲנוּנִים, "supplications, pleas for mercy"), from the root *chanan* ("to be gracious"), is technical liturgical vocabulary for cries from desperate need.

The vivid danger language of v. 3 — *afafuni chevlei-mavet u-metzarei she'ol metza'uni tzarah ve-yagon emtza* (אֲפָפוּנִי חֶבְלֵי־מָוֶת וּמְצָרֵי שְׁאוֹל מְצָאוּנִי, "the snares of death encompassed me; the pangs of Sheol laid hold on me") — uses *chevlei* (cords/snares — possibly with a wordplay on *chevlei yoledah*, birth-pangs) and *metzarei* (straits, narrow places) for Sheol. This is the lament-tradition's standard vocabulary of mortal peril, but compressed into one of the densest specimens in the Psalter.

The psalm's most theologically loaded image is the **cup of salvation** in v. 13: *kos-yeshu'ot essa u-ve-shem Yahweh eqra* (כּוֹס־יְשׁוּעוֹת אֶשָּׂא וּבְשֵׁם יְהוָה אֶקְרָא, "I will lift up the cup of salvation and call on the name of the LORD"). The plural *yeshu'ot* (salvations, deliverances) underscores the cumulative-comprehensive nature of Yahweh's saving acts. The "cup" is the libation cup of the *todah* sacrifice (cf. Num 28:7) — but in the Hallel context it is unmistakably the third cup of the Seder (the *kos shel berachah*, the cup of blessing — the same cup that Paul calls *to potērion tēs eulogias* in 1 Cor 10:16).

The famous v. 15, *yaqar be-einei Yahweh ha-mavtah la-chasidav* (יָקָר בְּעֵינֵי יְהוָה הַמָּוְתָה לַחֲסִידָיו, "precious in the sight of the LORD is the death of his saints"), uses *yaqar* (precious, costly) — the same root as in Prov 6:26 — and the unusual lengthened form *mavtah* (מָוְתָה) for "death." Whether the verse means God *grieves* the death of his faithful (so Calvin), or *honors* it, or *judges it costly and not lightly permitted* (so most Jewish commentators), is one of the great interpretive cruces of the Psalter.

ANE parallels: thanksgiving inscriptions are common in Egyptian and Babylonian votive contexts. The *todah* genre is an Israelite specialty within a wider ANE pattern. NT use is rich: 2 Corinthians 4:13 quotes 116:10 (LXX) — "I believed, and so I spoke" — as Paul's own apostolic *credo*. DSS evidence: 11QPs(a) and 4QPs(b) preserve portions of the Hallel cluster.

## Lens 2: The Literature

Psalm 116 is structurally a unified individual thanksgiving but bears a clear internal seam between vv. 9 and 10 (the basis for the LXX split). The composition unfolds in approximately four movements:

- **Movement 1 (vv. 1–4): The crisis recalled.** I love the LORD because he heard me; the snares of death encompassed me; I cried out.
- **Movement 2 (vv. 5–9): The deliverance celebrated.** Yahweh is gracious; he saved me; soul return to your rest; I will walk before the LORD in the land of the living.
- **Movement 3 (vv. 10–14): The thanksgiving formulated.** I believed, even when I spoke; what shall I render? — I will lift the cup of salvation, pay my vows.
- **Movement 4 (vv. 15–19): The vows performed.** Precious in the sight of the LORD is the death of his saints; I am your servant; in the courts of the house of the LORD; *halleluyah*.

Robert Lowth's parallelism categories appear throughout. Synonymous parallelism dominates: "the snares of death encompassed me / the pangs of Sheol laid hold on me" (v. 3); "you have delivered my soul from death / my eyes from tears / my feet from stumbling" (v. 8 — a tristich, "stair-step" or staircase parallelism); "I will pay my vows to the LORD / in the presence of all his people" (vv. 14, 18). Synthetic parallelism builds the closing strophe (vv. 17–19), each line adding to the location of the *todah* performance: "to you ... and call on the name ... pay my vows ... in the courts ... in your midst, O Jerusalem."

The psalm exhibits **internal refrains** that knit it together: the *call upon the name of Yahweh* (vv. 4, 13, 17), the *vows paid in the presence of all his people* (vv. 14, 18), the *courts of the house of the LORD* / *Jerusalem* as performance setting (v. 19). These refrains are the votive-liturgical signature: the psalm is a script for public performance, and the refrains mark the ritual gestures.

There is no *selah* in Ps 116, consistent with the Book V Hallel context.

The psalm exhibits a striking **first-person intensity**: the speaker uses *ani* and first-person verbal forms over twenty times, making this one of the most personal psalms in the Psalter. Yet the publicness is equally insistent: vows paid *neged-na le-khol amo* ("in the presence of all his people," vv. 14, 18), in *chatzrot beit Yahweh* ("the courts of the house of the LORD," v. 19). This individual-yet-public dialectic is the genius of the *todah* genre: my deliverance becomes our doxology.

The sequence of *ahavti* (v. 1, "I have loved") to *what shall I render?* (v. 12) to *the cup of salvation* (v. 13) is the psalm's emotional-theological arc. Love responds to being heard; gratitude searches for adequate expression; the cup is the answer. Robert Alter (*The Book of Psalms*, 2007) notes the careful escalation of intimacy: "I love" (v. 1) → "I will call on him as long as I live" (v. 2) → "return, O my soul, to your rest" (v. 7) → "I will walk before the LORD" (v. 9) → "I am your servant, the son of your maidservant" (v. 16) — each line drawing closer.

## Lens 3: The Larger Story

Within Wilson's canonical-shape reading of Book V (*The Editing of the Hebrew Psalter*, 1985), Pss 115–116 form the heart of the post-meal Hallel: Ps 115 establishes the trust-vs-idolatry frame, Ps 116 enacts a personal thanksgiving from death-deliverance within that frame. Hossfeld–Zenger (*Psalms 3*, Hermeneia, 2011) read Ps 116 as the lyric counterpart to the corporate trust-refrain of Ps 115: the community's trust is performed by the individual's testimony.

The **Jewish trajectory**. Mishnah Pesahim 10 prescribes Ps 116 over the third or fourth cup at the Seder. *Midrash Tehillim* identifies the speaker variously with Hezekiah (after his deliverance from Sennacherib and recovery from illness, 2 Kings 18–20), David (after his various deliverances), and the exilic community as a corporate "I." Rashi reads v. 15 as meaning that Yahweh does *not lightly permit* the death of his faithful — death must be "precious," costly to him before he allows it. Ibn Ezra emphasizes the votive-liturgical setting and the *kos yeshu'ot* as the libation cup. Radak (David Kimhi) develops the cup-of-salvation imagery in the context of temple liturgy. Modern Jewish thought engages Ps 116 as the lyrical heart of the Seder's second half: Buber on the *I-Thou* of vv. 1 and 16; Heschel on the death-of-the-faithful as God's grief, not God's celebration; Levenson (*Resurrection and the Restoration of Israel*, 2006) traces the Sheol-deliverance imagery; Sarna on the Hallel context. The Mishnah and Talmudic tractates devote substantial attention to the precise blessings recited over the *kos shel berachah* (third cup) at the Seder, and Ps 116:13 stands as one of the prooftexts.

The **Christian trajectory** is dominated by two reception streams. **First, the Eucharistic reading**. The third cup of the Seder, the *kos yeshu'ot* / *kos shel berachah*, is universally identified in Christian tradition with the cup of the Last Supper — the cup over which Jesus said, "this cup is the new covenant in my blood" (Luke 22:20; 1 Cor 11:25). Paul's "cup of blessing which we bless" (1 Cor 10:16) is precisely the third Seder cup, and Ps 116:13's *kos yeshu'ot* stands behind both the Pauline phrase and the Eucharistic theology of every subsequent Christian tradition. The Catholic Real Presence tradition reads the cup-of-salvation as the cup that contains the blood of Christ; the Lutheran tradition (Real Presence "in, with, and under" the elements) reads it sacramentally; the Reformed tradition (Calvin's spiritual presence) reads it as a means of mystical union; the Eastern Orthodox tradition reads it as the *poterion*, a sacramental bridge to the divine life.

**Second, the Pauline-credo reading**. Paul's quotation of Ps 116:10 LXX in 2 Corinthians 4:13 — *episteusa, dio elalēsa* ("I believed, therefore I spoke") — appropriates the psalmist's words as the apostolic motto of suffering proclamation. Paul reads his apostolic affliction as participation in the same pattern: the believer who has been delivered from death (or, in Paul's case, expects deliverance through resurrection) speaks because he believes.

Augustine (*Enarrationes in Psalmos*) reads Ps 116 christologically: Christ is the speaker of v. 1 ("I love the LORD"), and his death is the *yaqarah* (precious) death of v. 15. Cassiodorus develops the typology. Aquinas treats the cup of salvation as a figure of the Eucharist. Luther's reading emphasizes the *fides* of v. 10 — *Ich glaube, darum rede ich* became one of his rallying texts. Calvin's commentary on v. 15 famously reads it as God *grieving* the death of the faithful, not celebrating it (against a pious sentimentalism). The **Catholic and Anglican funeral liturgies** prominently feature v. 15 ("precious in the sight of the LORD is the death of his saints" / *pretiosa in conspectu Domini mors sanctorum eius*), often as an antiphon at the committal. The Anglican Book of Common Prayer's Burial of the Dead and the Catholic Office of the Dead both incorporate the verse. **Bach's Cantata BWV 102** weaves *Was kann ich doch dem Herren bezahlen für alle seine Wohltat* (Ps 116:12 in Luther's German) into a meditation on gratitude. The **St. Matthew Passion** invokes the Hallel context implicitly in its placement of the Last Supper before Gethsemane.

Among modern critical readers, Hans-Joachim Kraus (*Psalmen*, BKAT) treats Ps 116 as a model individual thanksgiving. John Goldingay (*Psalms 90–150*, BCOTWP, 2008) emphasizes the votive-public character of the *todah*. Marvin Tate, James Mays, Patrick Miller, and Beth Tanner all stress the integration of personal and communal worship. Walter Brueggemann (*The Message of the Psalms*, 1984) reads Ps 116 within the orientation–disorientation–new-orientation arc as a paradigmatic "new orientation" psalm. Hossfeld–Zenger emphasize the redactional placement: Ps 116 follows Ps 115's trust-refrain by *enacting* trust in personal testimony, and Ps 117 follows by extending that testimony to the nations.

## Lens 4: The Theological Intent

The central theological tension of Psalm 116 lies between the **personal intimacy of vv. 1–4** ("I love the LORD because he has heard my voice") and the **public-cultic performance of vv. 13–19** (the cup, the vows, the courts of the temple). How does private piety relate to public liturgy, and what does the famous v. 15 — *precious in the sight of the LORD is the death of his saints* — claim about God's relationship to the deaths of those who love him?

**Position 1: The Eucharistic reading (Catholic, Lutheran, Anglican, Eastern Orthodox traditions, with variations).** Ps 116:13's "cup of salvation" is the third cup of the Seder, identified by Christian tradition with the cup of the Last Supper, and therefore the deepest theological grounding of the Eucharist. The Catholic Real Presence reads the cup as containing the actual blood of Christ; the Lutheran reads sacramentally as Christ "in, with, and under" the elements; the Reformed reads as a means of spiritual presence and mystical union; the Eastern Orthodox reads as a sacramental theurgy. Steelman: the link between Ps 116:13 and 1 Cor 10:16 is exegetically secure; the Hallel context locates the psalm at the very meal at which the Eucharist was instituted; and Christian liturgical tradition has consistently received Ps 116 as a Eucharistic text. The disagreements among Christian traditions concern the *mode* of the cup's saving efficacy, not whether Ps 116:13 belongs in the conversation.

**Position 2: The Pauline-credo reading (2 Cor 4:13).** Ps 116:10 ("I believed, therefore I spoke") is appropriated by Paul as the apostolic motto. Steelman: Paul's reading is exegetically defensible — the psalmist did speak from belief amid affliction — and theologically generative, since it locates the speaking of the gospel in the same posture of suffering trust. Christian preaching is the apostolic enactment of the psalmist's *he'emanti ki adabber*.

**Position 3: The "precious death" reading (v. 15).** Three sub-positions are live in the tradition. **(3a) Calvin and Rashi's reading**: Yahweh does *not lightly permit* the death of his faithful — death is "precious" in the sense of "costly," weighed carefully in God's sight, never casually allowed. Steelman: this reading respects the Hebrew *yaqar* (precious, costly), avoids saccharine sentimentality, and honors the lament tradition's complaint against premature death. **(3b) The traditional liturgical reading**: God *honors* the death of his faithful, treating it as glorious. Steelman: the verse functions in funeral liturgy across Catholic, Anglican, Lutheran, Orthodox, and Reformed traditions, suggesting that the *yaqar* names a positive divine valuation, not a sorrowful divine reluctance. **(3c) The martyrological reading**: the verse is especially apt for those who die for the faith. Steelman: Maccabean and early Christian martyrologies cite the verse, and the structural placement after the Hallel (sung at Passover, the festival of deliverance through blood) reinforces the sacrificial reading.

**Position 4: The Gethsemane reading (Christological).** Jesus sang Ps 116 within hours of his arrest. The Christological force of every line is hard to ignore: "the snares of death encompassed me ... the pangs of Sheol laid hold on me" (v. 3); "I will lift up the cup of salvation" (v. 13 — the *cup* about which he would pray in Gethsemane); "precious in the sight of the LORD is the death of his saints" (v. 15 — sung the very night of his death). Steelman: this is not a forced typology but a historical-liturgical observation. Whatever Jesus believed about his own coming death, he sang Ps 116 with the words on his lips, and the early church's reception of the psalm Christologically (Augustine, Cassiodorus, Aquinas) is rooted in this fact.

**Where this leaves us:** Psalm 116 is the most personally intimate of the Hallel psalms and simultaneously the most publicly cultic. Its claim is that personal deliverance from death — "you have delivered my soul from death, my eyes from tears, my feet from stumbling" (v. 8) — issues necessarily into public testimony in the courts of the LORD's house. The cup of salvation lifted in v. 13 is a body-and-blood gesture: a cup raised in the presence of all the people, naming the Name, offering thanks for life that should have been lost. Christian tradition has read this cup as the cup of the new covenant; Jewish tradition has read it as the third cup of the Seder; the two readings rest on the same liturgical bedrock.

The closing question is what cup *we* are willing to lift, and whose name we are willing to call upon, when we have been delivered from death. Verse 15's *ha-mavtah la-chasidav* — the *death* of the faithful, *precious* to the LORD — is a verse that gains weight only after one has been close enough to death to feel the snares of v. 3. The psalm refuses to be read in detached calm. It was sung by Jesus on the night he was betrayed, and it is sung at funerals because it knows what funerals are. The *halleluyah* with which it closes is not a polite sigh but the exhale of someone who almost did not get to sing it.
