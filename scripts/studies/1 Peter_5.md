# 1 Peter 5: Fellow Elders, the Roaring Lion, and Babylon

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

The closing chapter of 1 Peter brings the letter to its epistolary conclusion with a charge to elders, a brief exhortation to the younger and to the whole community, a final eschatological framing of the readers' situation, and the conventional closing greetings (5:12-14) that have generated some of the most consequential historical-critical questions in the entire letter — questions about authorship, location, and ecclesiastical office.

The chapter opens with the address to the elders (*presbyteroi*, πρεσβύτεροι, 5:1, 5), the senior leadership figures of the Christian communities of Pontus, Galatia, Cappadocia, Asia, and Bithynia. The presbyterial office in late-first-century Christianity sat at a developmental moment well-mapped by Wayne Meeks (*The First Urban Christians*, 1983) and R. Alastair Campbell (*The Elders: Seniority within Earliest Christianity*, 1994). Campbell argued that "elder" in earliest Christianity functioned not as a fixed title but as a designation of senior, respected members of the community, often identified by household standing rather than by formal ordination. The terminology of *episkopountes* (ἐπισκοποῦντες, 5:2, "exercising oversight" — the participial form of the verb from which the noun *episkopos*, "overseer/bishop," derives) is significant: in 1 Peter 5 the elder *exercises oversight*; the noun *episkopos* and the office of bishop as distinct from elder are not yet in view. By the time of Ignatius of Antioch's letters (ca. 110), monepiscopacy — a single bishop in each city, distinct from and over the elders — is well-developed. 1 Peter sits prior to that development; the developmental moment is closer to 1 Clement (ca. 96) and the Didache (late first century), where elders and overseers are not yet sharply distinguished.

The author's self-designation as *sympresbyteros* (συμπρεσβύτερος, 5:1, "fellow elder") is the only New Testament occurrence of the term. Whether the author is Peter (Simon Peter) himself or a pseudonymous author writing in his name, the modesty of the self-designation has been read in two competing directions. Catholic interpreters from Joseph Fitzmyer through John Paul II's encyclical *Ut Unum Sint* (1995) have argued that Peter's modest self-presentation is consistent with — indeed expressive of — Petrine primacy understood as service rather than domination, in continuity with Matthew 16:18-19, Luke 22:31-32, and John 21:15-17. Protestant interpreters from Calvin through contemporary Reformed exegesis read 5:1 as flattening — Peter is *one elder among many*, with no hint of monepiscopacy or papal succession. The text itself does not adjudicate; it underwrites both the Catholic doctrine of Petrine office held humbly and the Protestant doctrine of presbyterial parity.

The "Babylon" of 5:13 ("she who is at Babylon, who is likewise chosen, sends you greetings") is almost universally read as code for Rome. The literal Mesopotamian Babylon was a virtually unpopulated site by the first century AD; no significant Christian community is attested there. The use of "Babylon" as code for Rome is a Jewish apocalyptic move: 4 Ezra 3:1-2 ("In the thirtieth year after the destruction of our city, I, Salathiel, who am also called Ezra, was in Babylon"), 2 Baruch, and Sibylline Oracles 5 all use Babylon as the cipher for Rome — the imperial capital that has destroyed Jerusalem (or, in the case of Sibylline Oracles, will). Revelation 17-18 deploys the same code in extended form. The use of "Babylon" for Rome appears to have crystallized in Jewish apocalyptic after AD 70, when the parallel between Babylon's destruction of the First Temple and Rome's destruction of the Second Temple became theologically pressing — though some have argued the code may have circulated earlier among diaspora Jews. If 1 Peter is pre-70 (the position of Karen Jobes, Richard Bauckham, Thomas Schreiner), then 5:13 is among the earliest attested uses of "Babylon = Rome." If post-70 (Paul Achtemeier, John H. Elliott), then it sits comfortably in the developed apocalyptic milieu.

Mark (John Mark, traditionally identified as the author of the Second Gospel) is greeted as "my son" (5:13), drawing on the early Christian tradition of intimate teacher-disciple kinship. The Mark of Acts 12:12 (whose mother's house was a meeting place for the Jerusalem church), Acts 13:5 (Barnabas's cousin who accompanied Paul and Barnabas on the first missionary journey), and Colossians 4:10 (in Rome with Paul) is the same figure. Papias of Hierapolis, ca. AD 130, transmitted by Eusebius (*Historia Ecclesiastica* 3.39.15), reports that Mark "became Peter's interpreter" and wrote down what he remembered of Peter's preaching — the foundational tradition for the Petrine character of the Second Gospel. 1 Peter 5:13 is one of the earliest pieces of evidence locating Mark with Peter, presumably in Rome.

Silvanus (Σιλουανός, 5:12), almost certainly the same Silas of Acts 15:22-18:5 who accompanied Paul on the second missionary journey, is identified as the letter's bearer or scribe ("by Silvanus... I have written"). The Greek phrase *dia Silouanou* (διὰ Σιλουανοῦ) can mean either "carried by Silvanus" (as letter-bearer) or "written by Silvanus" (as amanuensis). Defenders of Petrine authenticity (Bauckham, Jobes, Schreiner) have made much of the amanuensis reading: it accounts for the polished Greek of the letter (a register higher than one might expect from a Galilean fisherman) without sacrificing Petrine authorship.

Key Greek terms: *presbyteros* (πρεσβύτερος, 5:1, 5, "elder"); *sympresbyteros* (συμπρεσβύτερος, 5:1, "fellow elder," only NT occurrence); *episkopountes* (ἐπισκοποῦντες, 5:2, "exercising oversight"); *poimanate* (ποιμάνατε, 5:2, "shepherd," aorist imperative); *archipoimenos* (ἀρχιποίμενος, 5:4, "chief Shepherd," only NT occurrence); *Babylōn* (Βαβυλών, 5:13, code for Rome).

## Lens 2 — The Literature

The chapter is the letter's epistolary closing, with three structurally distinct units. First, 5:1-4 — the charge to elders, framed by the *sympresbyteros* self-designation and the *archipoimēn* eschatological promise. Second, 5:5-11 — exhortation to the younger and to the whole community, climaxing in the apocalyptic image of the prowling lion (5:8) and the doxology of 5:11. Third, 5:12-14 — the conventional epistolary closing with letter-bearer identification, greetings from Rome and Mark, and the kiss-of-love benediction.

The shepherd imagery of 5:2-4 (*poimanate to poimnion*, "shepherd the flock") draws on a deep Old Testament substrate. Ezekiel 34 — the prophetic indictment of the failed shepherds of Israel and the promise that God himself will be the shepherd — is the most important background. John 10 (Christ as the good shepherd) and Hebrews 13:20 (Christ as "the great shepherd of the sheep") show that the shepherd-Christology was widely diffused in early Christianity. The unique New Testament term *archipoimēn* ("chief Shepherd," 5:4) places the elders' shepherding work in a derivative relation to Christ's: they shepherd, but he is the chief Shepherd whose appearance will issue the "unfading crown of glory."

The *amaranthinos stephanos tēs doxēs* (ἀμαράντινος στέφανος τῆς δόξης, "unfading crown of glory," 5:4) borrows the Greco-Roman athletic-and-civic *stephanos* — the crown awarded to victors and benefactors — and inflects it eschatologically. The crown is *amaranthinos* — unfading, in contrast to the laurel and ivy crowns of the games, which withered.

The "roaring lion" (*leōn ōruomenos*, λέων ὠρυόμενος, 5:8) draws on apocalyptic imagery. Psalm 22:13 ("they open wide their mouths at me, like a ravening and roaring lion") is the Old Testament substrate. Daniel 6 (Daniel in the lions' den) and the rabbinic personification of the *yetzer hara* as a lion (Babylonian Talmud *Sukkah* 52a — "in the world to come, the Holy One will bring the *yetzer hara* and slaughter it... it will appear to the wicked as a hair, and they will weep") show the wider Jewish imaginative world. The image is not isolated allegory but participates in a deep Jewish-Christian tradition of evil personified as ravening predator.

The closing greeting "she who is at Babylon, who is likewise chosen" (5:13) uses the feminine *hē* — "she" — a personification of the Roman Christian community as a sister congregation greeting the Anatolian readers. The personification is consonant with the broader New Testament tradition of the church as bride or as the elect lady (2 John 1, "the elect lady and her children").

The "kiss of love" (*philēma agapēs*, φίλημα ἀγάπης, 5:14) is the Christian liturgical kiss attested in Romans 16:16, 1 Corinthians 16:20, 2 Corinthians 13:12, and 1 Thessalonians 5:26 — a ritual gesture of mutual recognition in the body of Christ that became a feature of the early eucharistic liturgy (Justin Martyr, *Apology* 1.65).

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

### Christian Interpretive Trajectory

The reception of 1 Peter 5 has been most contested over its ecclesiological implications — what does the chapter say about church order, the office of elder/bishop, and the Petrine office?

In the patristic period, the ambiguity of the elder/overseer terminology was already being noticed. 1 Clement (ca. 96), almost certainly written from Rome, uses "elders" and "overseers" interchangeably; the same is true of the Didache. By Ignatius of Antioch's letters (ca. 110), monepiscopacy is firmly established and the bishop is sharply distinguished from the elders. The Catholic tradition reads the trajectory from 1 Peter 5 to Ignatian monepiscopacy as legitimate development of apostolic seed; the Reformed tradition reads it as departure from the apostolic norm.

Catholic exegesis of 5:1 has consistently read the *sympresbyteros* self-designation as expressive of Petrine office held humbly. *Lumen Gentium* (1964) and *Ut Unum Sint* (1995) deploy the verse as evidence that the Petrine ministry, when faithfully exercised, is service to and among the elders, not domination. Joseph Fitzmyer's exegesis treats 5:1 as harmonious with Matthew 16:18-19, Luke 22:31-32, and John 21:15-17. Reformed and Protestant exegesis reads 5:1 in the opposite direction. Calvin's commentary (1551) treats the *sympresbyteros* designation as decisive — Peter is one elder among many; the verse is a proof-text for presbyterial polity. The Eastern Orthodox tradition reads 5:1 within conciliar episcopacy: each bishop is, with all other bishops, an *episkopos*, with no single bishop holding monarchical universal jurisdiction.

The roaring-lion passage (5:8) has generated its own reception history. Patristic writers — Augustine, Cyprian, John Chrysostom — read the verse with a robust personal demonology. Medieval monastic tradition (Bernard of Clairvaux) read the lion as the ever-present threat against which the cloister offers vigilance. The Reformation moderated the demonology somewhat — Calvin handles 5:8 with sober brevity — but retained the personal devil. Bultmann's twentieth-century demythologization program proposed reading the verse existentially, as the personification of inauthenticity rather than reference to a personal entity. Walter Wink's *Powers* trilogy (*Naming the Powers*, 1984; *Unmasking the Powers*, 1986; *Engaging the Powers*, 1992) developed an alternative reading — the "powers" as the inner spirituality of structures, institutions, and systems whose corrosive effects are real even if not reducible to discrete personal demons. Liberation theology's structural-evil reading (Sobrino, Boff) reads the lion as the systemic violence of empire and oppression.

### Jewish Scholarly Engagement

The shepherd imagery of 5:2-4 is densely Jewish. Ezekiel 34 is the central prophetic substrate, but the broader tradition — Psalm 23, Numbers 27:17 (Moses asking for a shepherd to lead the people lest they be "as sheep without a shepherd"), Zechariah 11 — places the chapter inside the Jewish prophetic-pastoral tradition. The application of shepherd-of-Israel language to Christian elders, with Christ as the *archipoimēn*, is a christological transposition of an originally Jewish ecclesial vision.

The "Babylon" code (5:13) is a Jewish apocalyptic move that the Christian text adopts. 4 Ezra and 2 Baruch — Jewish apocalyptic texts probably composed in the late first or early second century, after the destruction of the Second Temple — use Babylon as the cipher for Rome. Sibylline Oracles 5, a Hellenistic-Jewish work, does the same. The Christian use of the code in 1 Peter 5:13 and Revelation 17-18 is a borrowing from contemporaneous Jewish apocalyptic.

The "roaring lion" image (5:8) draws on the rabbinic personification of the *yetzer hara* — the evil inclination — as a lion (Babylonian Talmud *Sukkah* 52a). Geza Vermes and Daniel Boyarin have noted the close exchange between Jewish and Christian demonological idioms in the late Second Temple and early rabbinic periods. The lion-image is not an exotic Petrine flourish but participates in a shared Jewish-Christian imaginative ecology.

## Lens 4 — The Theological Intent

What God is doing in this chapter is constituting a community ordered for endurance. The shepherding elders, the humility-clothed congregation, the watchful resistance against the prowling adversary, the eschatological certainty that "after you have suffered a little while" the God of all grace "will himself restore, confirm, strengthen, and establish you" (5:10) — these are not separate themes but the integrated architecture of a community shaped to outlast the trial. The closing of the letter is the placing of that ordered community into the hand of "a faithful Creator" (4:19), with leadership vested not in domination but in the participatory shepherding pattern of the *archipoimēn*.

### Tension 1: Elder/Episkopos and the Petrine Office

**Position A: Catholic — apostolic Petrine office.** Peter's *sympresbyteros* self-designation in 5:1 is the modest expression of an office grounded in Matthew 16:18-19, Luke 22:31-32, and John 21:15-17 — an office held humbly among brethren but real and continuing. The Petrine office, exercised in succession by the bishops of Rome, is the visible center of unity for the universal church. *Lumen Gentium* and *Ut Unum Sint* develop the doctrine.

**Position B: Eastern Orthodox — conciliar episcopacy.** Each bishop is, with all other bishops, an *episkopos* in succession from the apostles; the Petrine ministry is exercised collegially in the apostolic college, not monarchically. The bishop of Rome is *primus inter pares* in honor but holds no universal monarchical jurisdiction. 1 Peter 5:1 is read consistently with this collegial structure.

**Position C: Reformed presbyterian.** 5:1 is read as flattening: Peter is one elder among many. The polity of the church is presbyterial — governance by a council of elders, with no monepiscopal or papal layer. Calvin and the Westminster Standards develop this position.

**Position D: Congregational / free-church.** Each local congregation is autonomously governed by its own elders or by the membership directly; there is no translocal ecclesial hierarchy. 5:1-3 supports a leadership style of shepherding and exemplification rather than juridical authority over churches beyond the local body.

**Position E: Anglican / Lutheran moderate episcopal.** Bishops, presbyters, and deacons compose a threefold ordained ministry that emerged from apostolic seed and represents a faithful (if not strictly mandated) development. The Petrine office is acknowledged historically but not granted the universal jurisdictional claim of post-Vatican-I Catholic doctrine.

**Steelman for Position A (Petrine office) — the position most foreign to the Protestant assumptions in which much of contemporary Anglo-American biblical scholarship is located:** the Catholic reading is not an arbitrary imposition on 5:1 but a careful reading of the verse within the broader apostolic witness. Peter's self-designation as *sympresbyteros* expresses the *form* of his ministry — service among brethren — without erasing its *substance* — the apostolic and Petrine office grounded in the dominical commissions. The text supports both the substance (Peter writing authoritatively to communities far beyond his immediate physical presence, exhorting their leadership) and the form (modest fellow-elder language). The Catholic doctrine of Petrine ministry as service rather than domination, articulated in *Ut Unum Sint*, claims neither more nor less than the verse permits when read in concert with the rest of the Petrine corpus. The Protestant worry that this trespasses on Christ's headship is met by the Catholic insistence that the Petrine ministry is wholly derivative, sacramentally and authoritatively, from Christ.

**Where this leaves us:** the disagreements among the five positions are not resolvable by exegesis of 5:1 alone. The verse is consistent with all five readings; the disagreements turn on broader ecclesiological and historical-theological commitments. What 5:1-4 unmistakably asserts is that whatever leadership exists in the Christian community is shepherding leadership patterned on the *archipoimēn*, not domination — a claim that all five traditions can affirm and that judges all of them.

### Tension 2: The Devil as "Roaring Lion" (5:8) and Christian Resistance

**Position A: Patristic and contemporary literal demonology.** The devil is a personal entity, fallen angel and adversary; 5:8 is straightforward warning of personal demonic threat. Augustine, Cyprian, Aquinas, and most premodern Christian theology hold this position. Contemporary evangelical theology (Sam Storms, Peter Williams) has reasserted it.

**Position B: Reformation moderate demonology.** Calvin's commentary on 5:8 reads the devil as personal and real but warns against speculative demonology that distracts from the believer's central vocation. The Reformed tradition holds personal demonology with sober restraint.

**Position C: Bultmannian demythologization.** Rudolf Bultmann's *Kerygma and Myth* (1941) proposed reading New Testament demonology existentially: the "devil" is the dramatic personification of the threat of inauthenticity, meaninglessness, or fallenness, not a discrete personal entity. 5:8's image is rhetorical, not ontological.

**Position D: Walter Wink — the Powers as inner spirituality of structures.** Wink's *Powers* trilogy reads the New Testament's "principalities and powers" — and 5:8's "adversary" — as the inner spirituality of institutions, systems, and corporate structures. Real, dangerous, but not reducible to discrete personal demons. Engagement is structural-political-spiritual at once.

**Position E: Liberation theology — structural evil.** Sobrino, Boff, Gutiérrez read 5:8 in light of the systemic violence of empire, poverty, and oppression. The "lion" devours the poor; resistance is solidarity with the suffering body of Christ in history.

**Steelman for Position D (Walter Wink's *Powers*) — the position most often rejected by both literalists (as evasive) and demythologizers (as half-measure):** Wink's reading is not a soft compromise between literal demonology and Bultmannian demythologization but a third way grounded in close reading of New Testament *exousiai* and *archai* language. The "powers" in Paul (Romans 8:38, Ephesians 6:12, Colossians 1:16) are language for institutional, structural, and systemic realities — empire, the law, slavery, market, principality — that have an *inner spirituality* (a corporate ethos and impetus) as well as an outer manifestation. The "roaring lion" of 5:8 names a real and ravening adversary against which the believer must be vigilant; reducing it either to a discrete personal demon (which underplays the structural reach of evil) or to existential inauthenticity (which underplays its agency) misses the mark. Wink's framework allows the reader to take the text's image with full seriousness — the devil is real, agential, ravenous, prowling — without flattening it into either premodern personalism or modern psychologism.

**Where this leaves us:** the five positions are not equally compelling but they each preserve something important. The personal-demonology tradition preserves the agency of evil. Wink's reading preserves its institutional reach. Liberation theology preserves its concrete victims. Calvin's restraint preserves the believer's central vocation. Bultmann preserves the existential demand of the text. A pastorally serious reading of 5:8 will refuse to choose only one — the lion is personal, structural, and existential, and the resistance ("firm in your faith," 5:9) is correspondingly multidimensional.

**Closing question:** if the readers' brotherhood throughout the world is enduring "the same kinds of suffering" (5:9) and the "Babylon" from which Peter writes is the same imperial city whose persecution he is addressing, what does it mean to "stand firm" inside the very structure that is devouring you — and what kind of letter, written from inside Babylon, can hope to fortify a flock under the same lion?

## The Thread

- **Ezekiel 34** — the shepherd-of-the-flock prophecy that Peter applies to Christian elders, with Christ as the *archipoimēn* who fulfills Ezekiel's promise that God himself will shepherd his people.
- **John 21:15-17** — Peter's own commissioning by the risen Christ to "feed my sheep," the dominical charge that 5:2's "shepherd the flock of God" enacts derivatively.
- **Acts 20:17-38** — Paul's parallel charge to the Ephesian elders, with the same overseer/shepherd vocabulary and the same warning against ravening adversaries (Acts 20:29, "fierce wolves will come in among you").
- **Revelation 17-18** — the contemporaneous Christian use of "Babylon" as code for Rome, the apocalyptic counterpart to Peter's brief greeting from "Babylon" (5:13).
