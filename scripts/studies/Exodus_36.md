# Exodus 36: Enough, and More Than Enough

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

Exodus 36 begins with a proclamation that has no close ANE parallel: the people bring so much material for the sanctuary that the builders ask Moses to stop them (vv. 5-7). Royal building inscriptions from the ancient Near East typically boast of the *quantity* extracted by the king from provinces, tribute, booty, and forced labor. The Annals of Sennacherib record the materials conveyed to Nineveh from conquered lands; the building inscriptions of Nebuchadnezzar II at Babylon (the "East India House Inscription") detail the silver, gold, cedar, and lapis brought by royal command. Egyptian temple-building inscriptions from Ramesses II at Karnak and Abydos tell the same story. The normal ANE pattern is: the king extracts, the people supply under compulsion, the temple is built. Exodus 36 inverts this: the people volunteer beyond what the project requires and are restrained by proclamation.

The contrast with Israel's preceding experience is sharper still. Exodus 5:7-19 is the "bricks without straw" narrative: Pharaonic extraction, impossible quotas, corporal punishment of foremen. In Egypt, extraction without end. At Sinai, production to sufficiency and then halt. The chapter enacts a literal reversal of Pharaonic political economy. Brueggemann's *The Prophetic Imagination* and Fretheim's *Exodus* both read this inversion as the theological core of P's tabernacle narrative.

Material culture anchors the chapter's detail. The dimensions given (curtains 28 x 4 cubits, frames 10 x 1.5 cubits, the tabernacle proper roughly 30 x 10 cubits with standard cubit conversions) are architecturally coherent for a Late Bronze or Iron Age portable shrine. Richard Friedman's reconstruction in *The Bible with Sources Revealed* and Menahem Haran's *Temples and Temple Service in Ancient Israel* both argue that the tabernacle's architecture, however stylized, draws on genuine memory of a portable desert sanctuary — not sheer post-exilic invention. The tent-shrine at Timna excavated by Beno Rothenberg (Site 200, late thirteenth century BCE) preserved post-holes, a copper serpent, textile remnants, and cult vessels in a tent structure plausibly analogous to the tabernacle on a smaller scale. The Midianite origin of the Timna shrine and its proximity to the biblical Sinai traditions have kept it a focus of debate; even skeptical scholarship concedes the general architectural plausibility of the tabernacle as a portable Bronze Age desert sanctuary type.

The technical vocabulary is period-appropriate. The "frames" of v. 20 (*qĕrāšîm*) are acacia uprights standing 10 cubits tall, overlaid with gold — a construction technique attested in New Kingdom Egyptian carpentry. The goats'-hair over-covering (v. 14) is the functional equivalent of the Bedouin black tent, which expands when wet and seals against rain — a point Carol Meyers and Jacob Milgrom have both made against sheer literary fabrication.

Chapter 36 is P-source execution narrative (so Wellhausen, Noth, Friedman, Dozeman), paralleling Exodus 26 almost verbatim. LXX ordering for chapters 35-40 diverges significantly from MT: the LXX is shorter and rearranged. Wevers and Aejmelaeus document the divergences and argue in different directions about whether LXX reflects an older Hebrew Vorlage or translator choice. Dating of P: Wellhausen post-exilic (after 538 BCE), Milgrom and Knohl First Temple period with later H-school editing, Haran pre-exilic priestly. Genuinely unresolved.

Key Hebrew terms: *mĕlāʾkâ* (מְלָאכָה) — "work, task, craft" (v. 1, 2, 3, 4, 5); *nĕdābâ* (נְדָבָה) — "freewill offering" (v. 3); *mišpĕṭê hāʾōhel* (משפטי האוהל) — tabernacle specifications; *qĕrāšîm* (קְרָשִׁים) — "frames, planks" (v. 20); *pārōket* (פָּרֹכֶת) — "veil" of the inner sanctuary (v. 35).

## Lens 2 — The Literature

The chapter operates on two distinct literary registers. Verses 1-7 are narrative: the people bring, the craftsmen report, Moses restrains. Verses 8-38 are technical construction description: curtains, frames, bases, veil, screen. The narrative register is dense with theological weight; the technical register carries the repetition-with-fulfillment structure that is P's signature.

Verses 5-7 are one of the most arresting moments in the Pentateuch. The craftsmen come to Moses and say *marbîm hāʿām lĕhābîʾ* — "the people bring too much." Moses proclaims, *ʾîš wĕʾiššâ ʾal-yaʿaśû-ʿôd mĕlāʾkâ litĕrûmat haqqōdeš* — "let no man or woman do any more work for the holy contribution." The text's specific note "man and woman" echoes the gender-inclusive contribution language of chapter 35 (vv. 22, 29); Carol Meyers highlights this as one of the Pentateuch's clearest acknowledgments of women's labor as constitutive of collective holy work. The phrase "the people were restrained" (*wayyikkālēʾ hāʿām*) uses a rare passive construction emphasizing external limitation of an internal drive.

Umberto Cassuto's *Commentary on Exodus* and Nahum Sarna's JPS volume both note how literarily shocking this moment is against the background of Exodus 32 (the golden calf) and Exodus 5 (the bricks-without-straw). Against both foils, chapter 36 stages an extraordinary reversal: the same people who gave gold for the calf now give gold for the tabernacle; the same people who suffered Pharaonic extraction now out-produce their own project. Zornberg's *The Particulars of Rapture* makes this the chapter's theological centerpiece — Israel has been *redirected*, not repressed. The interior motion of the heart is the same (it still "lifts up" — *nĕśāʾô libbô*), but the object of offering has changed.

The technical description of vv. 8-38 is structured by the refrain-skeleton that runs through chapters 36-40: "he made" (*wayyaʿaś*) recurs dozens of times, each clause reporting the faithful execution of a commanded element. Childs reads this as the literary inverse of "and God saw that it was good" in Genesis 1: where God's creation narrative closes each day with divine approval, the tabernacle construction closes each element with faithful fulfillment. The parallelism is intentional. Jon Levenson's *Creation and the Persistence of Evil* argues this is P's deliberate claim: the tabernacle is a *micro-cosm*, a built cosmos, and the language of its construction echoes the original creation. Samuel Balentine's *The Torah's Vision of Worship* builds this theological architecture out.

The specific literary device is what scholars call *ausführliche Gehorsamsbeschreibung* — extended obedience description. Every detail matches what was commanded in chapter 26, with verbal parallelism often exact. The technique asserts that the tabernacle is *exactly what was commanded* — not approximately, not creatively, not with priestly improvisation. The theological claim encoded in the literary form is that sacred form is not improvised. Calvin's regulative principle locates its textual warrant here (and in 1 Chron 28:19, David passing the "pattern" to Solomon).

The naming conventions vary significantly in the LXX. Wevers and Aejmelaeus document that the LXX abbreviates and rearranges. Some modern commentators (including Anneli Aejmelaeus) argue the LXX variant is older; others argue it is translator simplification. The MT text as we have it preserves maximum detail.

Textual note: verse 8's "all the craftsmen among the workmen made the tabernacle" — the Hebrew is *wayyaʿăśû kol-ḥăkam-lēb* ("all the skilled-hearted ones made"). The singular "he made" later in the chapter (vv. 10, 11, 14) refers to Bezalel as the lead artisan, whose name will be repeated at the start of chapter 37. The text's oscillation between plural ("all the craftsmen") and singular ("he made") has exercised commentators; Rashi reads the singular as referring to Bezalel throughout.

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

**Jewish interpretive trajectory.** Second Temple sources engage the chapter's material content. Philo's *Life of Moses* 2.71-97 allegorizes the tabernacle as a microcosm: the curtains as elements, the cherubim as divine powers, the whole as the cosmos built small. Josephus' *Antiquities* 3.123ff similarly cosmologizes. Philo's allegorical move becomes standard in Alexandrian Jewish and early Christian readings.

Rabbinic tradition develops the restraint-from-giving narrative into a central teshuvah text. Exodus Rabbah 48-49 makes the comparison with the golden calf explicit: "the same gold that was used for sin was used for forgiveness." Midrash Tanchuma on Pekudei reads the overflow of contribution as the measure of teshuvah's completeness — not only is the debt paid, but it is overpaid. The Talmud Bavli Megillah 10b-11a discusses the relationship between the tabernacle pattern and the First Temple. The Mishnah Middot maps out the architectural continuities and differences between the tabernacle and the Temple.

Rashi (on vv. 5-7) draws out the psychology of restraint: the people, having found the joy of giving, now have to be stopped — a remarkable inversion of the usual human inclination. Ibn Ezra works grammatically and architecturally. Ramban reads the chapter theologically as the tabernacle embodying God's indwelling with Israel, with the voluntary overflow evidencing Israel's changed heart. Maimonides' *Hilchot Beit HaBechirah* codifies the architectural tradition into normative law for future sanctuary building. Bahya ibn Pakuda (*Duties of the Heart*) reads the chapter as evidence that true service is interior motion finding exterior form. Judah Halevi's *Kuzari* uses the tabernacle theology to argue for Israel's unique covenantal calling.

Moderns continue the line. Nahum Sarna's JPS Commentary and Jeffrey Tigay's work on architectural description handle the material technically. Abraham Joshua Heschel's theology of the Sabbath and of sacred space treats the tabernacle as the template of holiness embodied. Jonathan Sacks in *Covenant and Conversation* reads the restraint-from-giving moment as the counter-image of consumerism: a society defined not by accumulation but by sufficiency. Jon Levenson's temple theology sees the tabernacle-cosmos link as P's signature theological claim. Michael Fishbane traces the intertextual work — chapters 36-40 are reused later in Scripture (esp. Chronicles).

**Christian interpretive trajectory.** Patristic readings consolidate around two moves. First, allegorical: Origen's *Homilies on Exodus* 13 treats each element of the tabernacle as a spiritual reality — the curtains as teachings, the frames as disciplines, the cherubim as angelic protection. Gregory of Nyssa's *Life of Moses* 2 reads the tabernacle as the soul's ascent architecture. Augustine's *City of God* 10.5-6 treats the tabernacle worship as prefigurative. Second, typological: the tabernacle prefigures Christ's body (John 2:19-21, Heb 9-10). Chrysostom, Cyril of Alexandria, and Jerome all develop this line.

Bede's *On the Tabernacle* (early eighth century) produces the most extended Patristic-medieval commentary, treating every element as a spiritual allegory and the construction as the building of the Church. Bede's reading dominates monastic reception for centuries. Hugh of St. Victor continues the tradition. Aquinas (*ST* III q. 61) uses the tabernacle to work out how Old Testament ceremonies prefigure Christ; the tabernacle is preparatory, fulfilled in the body of Christ and extended sacramentally in the Church.

The Reformation fractures the reception. Luther treats the tabernacle typologically and relatively lightly — it is abrogated in Christ. Calvin's *Commentary on Exodus* reads chapters 35-40 as establishing two enduring principles: first, that sacred form follows divine command exactly (the regulative principle); second, that faithful execution in accordance with commanded pattern is the substance of worship. The chapter's repeated "as the LORD commanded Moses" is Calvin's textual anchor. Reformed iconoclasm in the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries (the Scottish Reformation, the Puritan revolution) draws explicit authority from this chapter: the tabernacle's cherubim were permitted because commanded, but images not commanded were prohibited — the regulative line runs through here.

Critical scholarship opens new angles. Wellhausen reads the chapter as priestly retrojection of Second Temple practice. Noth and von Rad refine the redactional picture. Brevard Childs' *Exodus* treats chapters 35-40 as canonical complement to 25-31, the execution fulfilling the command in literarily deliberate form. Terence Fretheim's *Exodus* reads the chapter as theology of human participation in divine creation. Thomas Dozeman's *Commentary on Exodus* develops the source-critical and theological picture. Samuel Balentine's *The Torah's Vision of Worship* treats chapters 35-40 as the Torah's architectural vision of worship. Walter Brueggemann reads the restraint-from-giving as the anti-Pharaonic political economy of the tabernacle. Christopher Wright integrates the ethical implications for communal economy. Carol Meyers brings the material-cultural and gender-inclusive reading forward.

**Where they converge and diverge.** Convergence: both traditions treat the restraint-from-giving (vv. 5-7) as theologically extraordinary, and both take the technical specification as theologically meaningful. Jewish tradition makes the tabernacle pattern normative for the Temple and for halakhic categories (the thirty-nine labors, sanctified space, priestly architecture). Christian tradition makes the tabernacle typological of Christ's body and the Church. Divergence: the Reformed regulative principle reads the chapter as establishing a permanent form principle for worship, while Catholic and Orthodox traditions read it as preparatory to fuller revelation whose developed form is the liturgy. The Reformed iconoclastic tradition and Catholic iconographic tradition both appeal to the chapter: Reformed readers to the strict limit of commanded form, Catholic readers to the sanctioned cherubim (permitted images when commanded).

## Lens 4 — The Theological Intent

**Tension 1: form and freedom in worship.** The chapter is the clearest Old Testament locus for a tension that has structured centuries of Christian worship debate. Is the form of worship strictly limited to what Scripture commands (the regulative principle), or is it freely constructed within the bounds of what Scripture does not prohibit (the normative principle)?

- *Reformed reading (Calvin, Westminster Confession 21, Horton)*: chapter 36 establishes that every element of worship's form was commanded. Bezalel made *exactly* what was specified. Cherubim were not iconoclasm's exception but commanded embroidery. The form is divinely revealed, not humanly invented. In the new covenant, the form has changed (Christ replaces the tabernacle), but the principle that worship follows command has not. Worship elements not prescribed by God are not permitted.
- *Lutheran reading (Luther, Book of Concord)*: the tabernacle establishes that God cares about worship's form, but the new covenant church is not bound to Old Testament form or its exactness. Adiaphora — indifferent matters — are permitted under pastoral and aesthetic judgment. Scripture prescribes the *elements* (Word, sacrament, prayer) but not all the *circumstances*.
- *Anglican / normative principle (Hooker's Laws of Ecclesiastical Polity)*: what is not forbidden by Scripture is permitted, subject to edification and order. The tabernacle's detail does not bind new-covenant worship's form; it establishes that God attends to worship, not that He continues to specify each detail.
- *Catholic reading*: Aquinas (*ST* I-II q. 101-103; III q. 61) reads the tabernacle as preparatory ceremonial law, fulfilled in Christ. The Church's sacramental and liturgical form extends, does not abrogate, the principle that worship has divinely revealed structure. The Second Vatican Council's *Sacrosanctum Concilium* reaffirms that the liturgy is received, not constructed.
- *Orthodox reading (Lossky, Schmemann, *For the Life of the World*)*: the liturgy is the Spirit-guided continuation of the tabernacle-Temple-body-of-Christ trajectory. Its form is received from the apostles and fathers, organically developing but fundamentally given. The detailed specification of chapter 36 is the template for understanding how Christian liturgy is received, not invented.
- *Jewish reading (Maimonides, Hilchot Beit HaBechirah)*: the tabernacle's detailed specification is permanently binding halakhic precedent for sacred space. The Temple was built to the same pattern with development. The precise detail of chapter 36 is not merely descriptive but prescriptive for all subsequent sanctuary construction.
- *Critical reading (Balentine, Fretheim)*: chapter 36 is a theological vision of ordered worship, not a legal code for subsequent practice. Its detail encodes the claim that worship is taken seriously enough to specify — but the text does not adjudicate between regulative and normative later theologies.

*Steelman the least intuitive — the regulative principle.* Non-Reformed Christians often read the regulative principle as restrictive legalism: worship reduced to a cold command-and-execute. The principle's charitable reading is different. It claims that worship is never humanly neutral — what is not commanded is invariably seized by the worshipers' preferences, by clerical innovation, by cultural taste, by aesthetic pressure. The regulative principle is *anti-clerical* in that sense: it protects the worshiping assembly from being colonized by the tastes of those who run the worship. Chapter 36's detailed specification is the historical anchor: when God cared to give a pattern, He gave a specific one, and the worshipers did *exactly that* — not approximately, not with elaboration. The regulative reader claims this is the permanent pattern: divine command plus exact execution.

**Where this leaves us:** chapter 36 establishes that sacred form is not indifferent and not improvised. Traditions differ on the scope of this principle — whether it binds detail-for-detail in the new covenant, or only establishes the general point that worship's elements are divinely given. Reading the chapter charitably, neither the strict regulative reader nor the normative reader can dismiss the other without distorting the text: form matters, and freedom matters.

**Tension 2: sufficiency and economy.** The chapter's most theologically loaded moment is the restraint-from-giving (vv. 5-7). What does it mean that Israel is restrained from further offering because there is "enough and more than enough"?

- *Jewish reading (Sacks, Heschel)*: the episode enacts an anti-Pharaonic economy. The voluntary contribution exceeds the project's need; the proclamation halts extraction before it becomes exploitation. The Sabbath logic of sufficiency — there is enough; one may stop — governs sacred labor as it governs manna-gathering in Exodus 16. Sacks in *The Dignity of Difference* reads this as the theological charter of an economy defined by sufficiency rather than accumulation.
- *Catholic reading*: the episode evidences the principle of sufficient-not-excessive in Catholic social teaching (from *Rerum Novarum* through *Laudato Si'*). Worship's material support should be adequate to its purpose, not maximized. The proclamation of restraint prefigures the Church's teaching against both poverty in worship (neglect) and excess in worship (gilding).
- *Reformed reading*: the sufficiency-restraint points to stewardship — Israel's generosity is godly because it is bounded. Calvin on this verse reads the restraint as divine wisdom in setting limits on well-intentioned but potentially disordered zeal.
- *Lutheran reading (Luther)*: the episode is the paradigm of gratitude-driven works — Israel gives from joy in forgiveness, not from compulsion. The restraint is evidence of contentment rather than anxiety-driven accumulation.
- *Wesleyan reading (Wesley, *Use of Money*)*: the episode illustrates Wesley's principle of giving all that one can but within ordered limits — the church's proper work is bounded, and giving beyond its need becomes disordered. Wesley distinguished earnings, saving, and giving; this chapter speaks to the giving-with-limits principle.
- *Orthodox reading (Schmemann)*: the sufficiency is eucharistic — enough is what grace gives; excess is the sign of anxiety that grace has not been received. The restraint proclaims grace as provision.
- *Critical reading (Brueggemann, Fretheim)*: the episode is an explicit anti-Pharaonic political economy. Pharaoh demanded bricks without straw; YHWH proclaims rest from bringing more than enough. The same material economy (labor, raw material, production) is structured by radically different theologies of power and distribution.

*Steelman the least intuitive — the critical anti-Pharaonic reading.* To readers suspicious of political-economic readings of the Pentateuch, Brueggemann's framing can sound anachronistic, importing twentieth-century political categories into a Bronze Age text. The charitable reading takes the structural-theological claim seriously: Exodus has just narrated Israel's liberation from an extractive economy (chs. 1-14). The tabernacle construction is Israel's first communal production project as a free people. The text's unmissable detail — that production halts because material is sufficient — is a structural theological claim. It is not smuggling in modern categories; it is articulating what differentiates a covenantal economy from a Pharaonic one.

**Where this leaves us:** the restraint-from-giving is not an incidental literary flourish. It is the text's clearest statement that covenantal sacred economy is bounded — enough is enough, and more than enough calls for proclamation to cease. Every major tradition reads some version of this.

*Unresolved:* If the tabernacle construction ends because materials were sufficient, what should we make of later biblical narratives (1 Kings 6-7; 1 Chron 22-29; the Second Temple) where royal authority and centralized extraction *do* produce the sacred space? Does the Davidic-Solomonic temple-building economy represent a fall from the tabernacle's anti-Pharaonic pattern, a legitimate development, or a theological problem the canon itself preserves as tension?

## The Thread

Three non-obvious cross-references.

First: Exodus 16:16-18. The manna narrative — Israel gathers, each according to his need, and "whoever gathered much had nothing left over, and whoever gathered little had no lack." The logic is the same as chapter 36's restraint. Paul will explicitly connect the two in 2 Corinthians 8:15, using the manna verse to justify the collection for Jerusalem. The structural theology of sufficiency runs from manna through tabernacle construction to the apostolic collection.

Second: 1 Kings 8:27 — Solomon at the Temple dedication: "But will God indeed dwell on the earth? Behold, heaven and the highest heaven cannot contain you; how much less this house that I have built!" The irony against Exodus 36 is sharp. The tabernacle, for all its detail, never prompts a question about whether God can be contained. The Temple, far more grand, does. The shift from portable sufficiency to monumental extraction raises the theological question Solomon asks — whether centralized temple-building has silently crossed a line the tabernacle did not.

Third: Acts 2:44-47 and 4:32-35 — the early Jerusalem community "had all things in common… there was not a needy person among them." Luke's language echoes the manna and tabernacle patterns: voluntary contribution, sufficiency, restraint from accumulation. The apostolic community is Luke's attempt to instantiate chapter 36's economic theology in the age of the Spirit. The reading-back-into the Gentile Christian mission (in Paul's collection, 2 Cor 8-9) makes the thread one of the Bible's most persistent.
