# Luke 6: Sabbath, the Twelve, and the Sermon on the Plain

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

Luke 6 opens with a halakhic dispute that only makes sense inside the texture of first-century Galilean Sabbath practice. Plucking grain (***tillontes***, τίλλοντες, "plucking" — Luke alone adds "rubbing them in their hands," ***psōchontes tais chersin***, ψώχοντες ταῖς χερσίν), within the casuistic framework codified later in the Mishnah's tractate *Shabbat* (chapter 7's thirty-nine *avot melakhot*, principal forbidden categories of work), would constitute "reaping" and "threshing." The dispute is not whether Torah forbids work on Sabbath — every Jewish party agreed — but how to define work, and whose interpretive authority counts. **Geza Vermes** and **E.P. Sanders** have demonstrated that this kind of intra-Jewish halakhic argument is exactly what we should expect in the first century; the Pharisees were not "legalists" against "grace" but practitioners of a particular school of Sabbath interpretation. Jesus's appeal to David eating the showbread (1 Samuel 21) is a properly rabbinic move: cite a precedent where the literal letter yields to a higher principle.

The grain itself is significant. By late spring in Galilee, barley and wheat ripened in fields adjacent to the network of roads connecting Sea-of-Galilee villages — Capernaum to Bethsaida, Magdala to Tiberias. Disciples walking through such fields and snacking on heads of grain (allowed by Deut 23:25) was unremarkable on any other day of the week. The synagogue scene that follows (6:6-11) places us in the architecture excavated at Capernaum, Magdala, Gamla, and the more recently published synagogue at Khirbet Wadi Hamam: typical first-century *bet kenesset* with stone benches around a central reading floor, a Torah cabinet, and seating that would have made the man with the withered (***xēra***, ξηρά, "dried-up") right hand publicly visible to the watching scribes.

The mountain scene of 6:12 — "he went out to the mountain (***eis to oros***, εἰς τὸ ὄρος) to pray, and all night he continued in prayer to God" — moves us into a different topographic register. The mountain in Luke is theological space, drawing on Sinai, Horeb, and the prophetic tradition of mountaintop encounter. Yet the historical Galilee provides plausible candidates: the heights above the lake on the eastern (Golan) side, or the Mount of Beatitudes traditionally identified by Byzantine pilgrims with the bluffs above Tabgha. Luke's "**level place**" (***topon pedinon***, τόπον πεδινόν, 6:17) where Jesus then comes down to teach is not a contradiction of Matthew's "mountain" but a distinct setting — perhaps a plateau on a mountainside, where a teacher could stand below the summit and a great crowd could gather on the slope.

The crowd's geography is striking: "from all Judea and Jerusalem and the seacoast of Tyre and Sidon" (6:17). This is the first explicit Gentile-territory reference in the Lukan narrative. Tyre and Sidon, the Phoenician coastal cities, lay outside Herodian Jewish territory; their inclusion among the listeners signals Luke's programmatic Gentile-inclusion theme. Archaeological work on first-century Phoenician cities by **John Grainger** and others has documented their commercial integration with the Galilean and Judean economies; the trade routes ran inland through Caesarea Philippi and the Hula Valley.

Three Greek terms anchor the chapter's social-theological texture. ***Apostolos*** (ἀπόστολος, 6:13) — the Synoptic Gospels' first use of "apostle" as a fixed title rather than a generic term for a sent messenger; **Joel Green** notes that Luke is consciously establishing the institutional vocabulary that will dominate Acts. ***Ptōchoi*** (πτωχοί, 6:20) — not the working poor (***penēs***, πένης) but the destitute, the cringing-poor who must beg; the Septuagint's standard rendering of Hebrew *anawim*, the pious-oppressed. ***Eleēmōn*** (ἐλεήμων, 6:36) — "merciful," the Lukan climax-word that replaces Matthew's "perfect" (***teleios***, τέλειος, Matt 5:48); the difference is theologically loaded and we will return to it.

## Lens 2 — The Literature

Luke 6 is the chapter where the Synoptic Problem becomes unavoidable. The Sermon on the Plain (6:17-49) has obvious overlap with Matthew's Sermon on the Mount (Matt 5–7) — same beatitudes (in part), same love-of-enemies, same Golden Rule, same two-builders parable. But the differences are substantial: Luke has four blessings and four woes; Matthew has eight or nine beatitudes and no woes. Luke's blessings address the second person ("Blessed are you who are poor"); Matthew's are third-person ("Blessed are the poor in spirit"). Luke's are economic-physical ("poor," "hungry now," "weep now"); Matthew's are spiritual-ethical ("poor in spirit," "hunger and thirst for righteousness," "mourn"). Luke's sermon runs about thirty verses; Matthew's runs over a hundred.

The classical **Two-Source Hypothesis** (Streeter, articulated through the twentieth century by **Joachim Jeremias** and **Norman Perrin**, and still dominant today in the work of **John Kloppenborg's** International Q Project) holds that both evangelists are drawing on a common sayings-source Q, which contained a sermon block that each redacted. Luke is conventionally taken to preserve Q's wording more closely (the second-person "you," the economic register), while Matthew has expanded and spiritualized. **Mark Goodacre's** *The Case Against Q* — and the broader Farrer-Goulder-Goodacre hypothesis — argues for a leaner solution: Mark first, then Matthew uses Mark, then Luke uses both Mark and Matthew. On this reading, Luke is shortening and reorganizing Matthew's sermon, not preserving an earlier Q. The implications are theologically substantial: if Luke is later than Matthew and depends on him, then Luke's economic-radical edge ("blessed are the poor" without Matthew's "in spirit") is a deliberate redactional move — Luke is *de-spiritualizing* Matthew, not faithfully preserving an earlier tradition that Matthew has already spiritualized.

**Joel Green** (NICNT) maintains the Two-Source view but reads Luke's redaction sympathetically: Luke is not contradicting Matthew but correcting the spiritualizing-drift that even the earliest tradition was prone to. **John Nolland** (WBC) is more open to Goodacre's hypothesis but holds Q as the more economical solution. **Bock** (BECNT) keeps the Two-Source position; **Marshall** (NIGTC) is also conventional. **Bovon** (Hermeneia) is meticulous about the textual decisions.

Three Lukan compositional fingerprints govern the chapter:

First, the **night-of-prayer before key decisions** (6:12). Luke alone places Jesus in all-night prayer before choosing the Twelve. This is the Lukan signature — at the baptism (3:21), at the choosing of the Twelve (6:12), before Peter's confession (9:18), at the Transfiguration (9:28-29), in Gethsemane (22:41-46), on the cross (23:34, 46). **Conzelmann** identified this prayer-pattern as a structural marker of Luke's Spirit-Christology.

Second, the **first-Synoptic-use of "apostle" as title** (6:13). Mark calls them disciples; Matthew uses "the twelve" or "the twelve disciples"; only Luke writes "the twelve, whom he named apostles." **Tannehill** in *Narrative Unity* sees this as Luke programmatically establishing the institutional category that will dominate Acts 1–15. The list itself (6:14-16) has its variants — Luke's "Judas the son of James" appears as Mark's "Thaddaeus" and Matthew's "Lebbaeus," generating reception-historical confusions about an apostle named Jude. **Richard Bauckham** (*Jesus and the Eyewitnesses*) argues these are alternate names for a single person, a phenomenon paralleled in other ancient name-lists.

Third, the **economic-physical register of the Beatitudes**. The redactional verdict matters here. **Levine** in *The Misunderstood Jew* and *Short Stories by Jesus* makes the case that Luke's "blessed are you who are poor" recovers the genuine prophetic register of Isaiah 61:1 ("good news to the poor" — *anawim*) that the Matthean spiritualization risks softening. **Bauckham**, in essays on the Beatitudes, similarly favors the economic reading. The Lukan woes (6:24-26), absent from Matthew, drive the prophetic-justice edge home: woe to the rich, the full, the laughing, the well-spoken-of — language that draws directly from prophetic indictments (Amos 6, Isaiah 5).

Two-Builders parable: Luke's distinctive addition is "**dug deep**" (***eskapsen kai ebathynen***, ἔσκαψεν καὶ ἐβάθυνεν, 6:48) — the wise builder excavates to bedrock. **Kenneth Bailey** in *Through Peasant Eyes* points out that this is the realistic Mediterranean construction practice for stone-masonry on bedrock, whereas Matthew's version (with its sand-and-rock contrast) emphasizes the type of soil. Both versions converge theologically; their differences reflect different cultural-architectural imaginations.

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

The **Jewish interpretive trajectory** finds in Luke 6 deep continuity with Hebrew prophetic and rabbinic ethics, and also genuine tensions. **Levine** notes that the love-of-enemies command, often presented in Christian polemic as Jesus's distinctive innovation against a Jewish "love your neighbor, hate your enemy" ethic, is in fact already present in the Hebrew Bible (Exodus 23:4-5, Proverbs 25:21) and developed in rabbinic teaching (Avot 1:12, "be of the disciples of Aaron, loving peace and pursuing peace"). The "hate your enemy" half of Matthew 5:43 has no Jewish source; it is rhetorical foil. **Pinchas Lapide** in *The Sermon on the Mount: Utopia or Program for Action?* argued that Jesus's love-of-enemies command is intelligible within Hellenistic-Jewish ethics and is closer to Hillel than commonly recognized. **David Flusser** noted parallels in the Dead Sea Scrolls (1QS 10:17-18 forbids returning evil for evil within the community) while acknowledging the genuine novelty of Jesus's universalization.

The Sabbath disputes are intra-Jewish halakha, not Jesus-vs-Judaism. **E.P. Sanders** in *Jewish Law from Jesus to the Mishnah* showed that Pharisaic Sabbath rulings were a matter of school-debate, not codified law in Jesus's day; the dispute over plucking grain has no clear Pentateuchal violation, and rabbinic literature later registers debates on what counts as "reaping." **Daniel Boyarin** in *Border Lines* argues that Jesus's "Son of Man is lord of the Sabbath" is not an abolition but a Christological claim within Jewish messianic categories.

The **Beatitudes' Jewish reception**: **Sanders** and **Vermes** both place the Lukan "blessed are the poor" within the prophetic *anawim* tradition — Psalms 37, 72, 146; Isaiah 11, 41, 61. The pious-poor are God's beloved; God will vindicate them. The woes against the rich draw on Amos 6:1-7, Isaiah 5:8-23, James 5:1-6. Luke's sermon is not anti-Jewish but radically prophetic-Jewish.

The **Christian interpretive trajectory** is vast. **Origen's** *Homilies on Luke* read the Sermon on the Plain as the moral-mystical instruction of the Christian life, with the Beatitudes mapping the soul's ascent. **Ambrose's** *Expositio Lucam* engaged the divergence with Matthew explicitly, suggesting that Luke's sermon was a different (later, on a plain) sermon, distinct from Matthew's mountain-sermon. This "two-sermon" harmonization persisted through medieval tradition. **Augustine** initially held the two-sermon view in his early *De sermone Domini in monte* but later acknowledged in *De consensu evangelistarum* that the two might be variant traditions of a single sermon.

**Calvin** in his *Harmony* favored the single-sermon view, arguing that Matthew expanded what Luke recorded more concisely. **Luther** preached extensively on Matthew's Sermon and treated Luke's as the same material; his lectures emphasize the "blessed are the poor" as economic-physical poverty that becomes spiritual blessing only through faith. **Bonhoeffer's** *The Cost of Discipleship* exposits Matthew's Beatitudes but his prison letters and *Ethics* engage Luke's economic radicalism more directly, particularly the woes.

The **liberation theological reading** finds Luke 6 paradigmatic. **Gustavo Gutiérrez** in *A Theology of Liberation* and *The God of Life* takes Luke's "blessed are you poor" without "in spirit" as the textual ground for the preferential option for the poor. **Justo González** *Luke (Belief)* reads the woes as the necessary obverse of the blessings — the Sermon proclaims a great reversal that requires both the lifting up and the bringing down. **Leonardo Boff** and the broader Latin American liberation tradition use Luke 6 as central scripture. **Richard Horsley** (*Jesus and the Spiral of Violence*) reads the love-of-enemies command in its first-century context as a strategy for non-violent resistance against Roman imperial occupation, not as quietist passivism.

**Feminist scholarship** (Schüssler Fiorenza *In Memory of Her*, **Seim** *Double Message*, Beverly Gaventa) notes that the apostle-list of 6:14-16 is exclusively male, reinforcing Lukan gender-structures even as Luke gives unusual narrative space to women. **Catherine LaCugna's** *God for Us* engaged the Trinitarian-ethical implications of "be merciful as your Father is merciful": the imitation of God in mercy is constitutive of Christian identity in a way the Matthean "be perfect" does not as fully capture. **Mary Healy** and other Catholic theologians have developed the mercy-theme through Pope Francis's *Misericordiae Vultus*.

**Kenneth Bailey's** *Through Peasant Eyes* and *Jesus Through Middle Eastern Eyes* read the Sermon's commands in Mediterranean honor-shame terms: "turn the other cheek" is not passivity but a refusal to be drawn into the spiral of honor-violence; "give to everyone who asks" is the radical hospitality-ethic of village culture; "love your enemies" is a public renunciation of clan-vengeance. Bailey's contextual readings have been influential across mainstream and evangelical commentary.

**Conzelmann** *Theology of St. Luke*, **Tannehill** *Narrative Unity*, **Fitzmyer** AB, **Joel Green** NICNT, **Nolland** WBC, **Bock** BECNT, **Marshall** NIGTC, **Johnson** Sacra Pagina, **Bovon** Hermeneia all weigh in extensively on the Sermon's structure. The dominant scholarly consensus treats it as a coherent literary unit (not a random sayings-collection), structured chiastically around the love-command, with the beatitudes-and-woes as proem and the two-builders parable as peroration.

## Lens 4 — The Theological Intent

**Tension One: Luke's "blessed are you who are poor" — economic destitution, prophetic *anawim*, or both?** Three positions. The **strict-economic reading** (Gutiérrez, González, Horsley, **John Howard Yoder** in *The Politics of Jesus*) holds that Luke means literal poverty: the destitute are blessed because the kingdom of God reverses social hierarchies, not because poverty is spiritually meritorious in itself. Steelman: the parallel woe ("woe to you who are rich") makes no spiritual-condition sense; it requires literal economic reference. The **prophetic-*anawim* reading** (Fitzmyer, Marshall, Bock, much patristic-Reformation tradition) holds that Luke's "poor" carries the Hebrew *anawim* meaning — the pious-oppressed who trust in God, who happen also to be economically poor but whose blessedness is theological, not merely sociological. Steelman: Isaiah 61:1, programmatically cited at Luke 4:18, defines the term theologically; the destitute who are not pious are not blessed. The **dialectical reading** (Joel Green, Levine, Bauckham) holds that the spiritual-economic distinction is itself an artifact of later spiritualizing readings; for Luke, the *anawim* are precisely the pious-poor whose poverty and piety are inseparable in the prophetic tradition. Steelman: Hebrew prophets refused to disjoin material and spiritual conditions; Luke is recovering that integrated vision against the spiritualizing tendency that Matthew already shows. **Where this leaves us:** the dialectical reading is most adequate to Luke's prophetic-Jewish context. The economic and the spiritual are not two readings between which to choose but two abstractions of a single reality the prophets refused to separate. The pastoral-implicational rub is that comfortable readers are tempted to adopt either extreme — purely-economic to apply only to other people, or purely-spiritual to evacuate the woes.

**Tension Two: "Be merciful as your Father is merciful" (Luke) versus "be perfect as your heavenly Father is perfect" (Matthew). Are these the same teaching? Different teachings? Different translations of the same Aramaic original?** Three positions. The **same-teaching, different-translation reading** (Fitzmyer, traditional Two-Source) holds that Aramaic *gemīrā* underlies both, with Matthew rendering it "perfect" (with the Greek philosophical resonance of *teleios*, completion) and Luke rendering it "merciful" (with the Hebrew-prophetic resonance of *raḥamim*, mercy). Steelman: the immediate context in both Gospels concerns the love-of-enemies; the theological substance (the disciple imitates God's posture toward enemies) is identical. The **deliberately-different-redactions reading** (Goodacre, **John Nolland**) holds that Luke is consciously redacting Matthew, replacing "perfect" with "merciful" because Luke's whole gospel theme is divine mercy (***eleos***, ἔλεος, the Lukan key-word from Mary's Magnificat onward). Steelman: Luke's distinctive vocabulary throughout the Gospel makes this the natural reading; Luke knows what he is doing. The **complementary-readings, irreducibly-different reading** (LaCugna, some patristic strands) holds that "perfect" and "merciful" are theologically distinct: Matthew calls the disciple to ethical completion in imitation of God's holiness; Luke calls the disciple to compassionate-action in imitation of God's mercy. Steelman: the theological content of Christian discipleship is enriched by holding both, and impoverished by reducing one to the other. **Where this leaves us:** all three positions can be true simultaneously — same Aramaic original, different Greek translations, theologically complementary emphases. The pastoral truth is that the modern Western imagination tends to default to "perfect" (a moral-perfectionist reading) and needs Luke's "merciful" as corrective. Mercy is what divine perfection looks like when it acts toward the ungrateful and the evil.

**Unresolved question:** What does Luke do with the rich? The woes of 6:24-26 are unflinching, and Luke will return to the theme repeatedly (the rich fool of 12:16-21, Lazarus and the rich man of 16:19-31, Zacchaeus of 19:1-10). Yet Luke also has wealthy patrons in 8:3 (Joanna), wealthy disciples in Acts (Lydia, Cornelius), and the entire community-of-goods experiment in Acts 2 and 4. Is Luke calling for renunciation of wealth (the Franciscan reading)? For redistribution (the liberation reading)? For radical generosity within a continuing economy (the moderate reading)? The text refuses to settle.

## The Thread

Three cross-references frame this chapter's thread through the canonical and Lukan story. **Isaiah 61:1-2** stands behind the Beatitudes as it stood behind the Nazareth synagogue scene of Luke 4:18-19: "the Spirit of the Lord is upon me, because he has anointed me to bring good news to the poor." Luke 6 is the programmatic exposition of what Luke 4 announced; the Sermon on the Plain is Jesus teaching the Isaianic program. **Deuteronomy 28** and the covenant-blessings-and-curses pattern stand behind the structure of blessings-and-woes; Luke's sermon is delivered in the cadence of covenant renewal, with Jesus in the prophetic-Mosaic role. **Acts 2:42-47 and 4:32-37** show the Sermon's economic-radical instructions enacted in the early Jerusalem community: "all who believed were together and had all things in common." Watch also for **Luke 16:19-31** (Lazarus and the rich man) — the woes of 6:24-26 will be narratively dramatized; and for **Acts 5:1-11** (Ananias and Sapphira) — the Sermon's warning against hypocrisy will receive its starkest application.
