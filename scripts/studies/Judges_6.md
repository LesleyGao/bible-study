# Judges 6: Threshing Wheat in a Winepress

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

The opening verses describe an oppression with a particular logistical signature: the Midianites and Amalekites and "people of the East" cross the Jordan at harvest, encamp, devour the standing produce as far west as Gaza, and disappear. They come "with their livestock and their tents" and their camels "could not be counted." This is not the territorial conquest of an Egyptian or Assyrian power; it is seasonal raiding by a confederated mobile-pastoralist coalition exploiting Israel's harvest cycle. The Israelite response — fleeing to "dens that are in the mountains and the caves and the strongholds" — is the predictable behavior of a sedentary peasantry that lacks chariot forces and cannot defend open fields.

The camels are the crux of the historical-critical debate. Lawson Younger (in his Anchor Yale History of the Arameans and his commentary on Judges) takes the text at face value and reads Iron Age I northern Arabia as a region where camel use for raiding had begun, citing the Tell Jemmeh deposits and the gradual shift in Negev pastoralism. Israel Finkelstein and Benjamin Sass have argued in the opposite direction: large-scale, militarily significant camel domestication is an eighth- to seventh-century BCE phenomenon, and the Midianite camel hordes are a literary anachronism reflecting the time of the writers, not of Gideon. Older critical scholarship (Albright, then later Soggin) split the difference and read the camel-economy detail as a memory of Late Bronze / early Iron caravan activity inflated by tradition. The archaeology of "Midianite ware" — the painted bichrome pottery from the Hijaz, Timna, and Qurayyah — confirms a real Midianite material presence in the southern Levant during the Late Bronze / early Iron transition; the question is the saddle and the mass mobility.

The material details inside Israel are sharp. Gideon is "beating out wheat in the winepress" (Hebrew *bagat*, a hewn rock-cut basin) — the irony lands only if you know the agronomy. Wheat is normally threshed on an open, elevated *goren* (threshing floor) so the wind can carry off the chaff after the oxen or sled break the heads. The winepress is a sunken, walled pit in bedrock used to crush grapes underfoot in autumn. Threshing wheat there is inefficient, secretive, and physically miserable: there is no breeze for winnowing, the floor is wrong, and the man is hiding. The text's first portrait of Gideon is a portrait of an economy in collapse. The "ephah of flour" he later prepares for his guest — roughly 22 liters — is enormous, the better part of a household's reserve grain; it signals both desperation and lavishness.

The site is Ophrah of the Abiezrites, a Manassite clan settlement (not the Benjaminite Ophrah of Joshua 18:23). The "terebinth" (*ʾelah*) is a sacred tree, of a piece with the oak at Shechem (Genesis 35:4; Joshua 24:26) and the terebinths of Mamre. Sacred-tree shrines belonged to the local landscape long before the centralizing reforms of Hezekiah and Josiah. The presence in Joash's own household of an altar to Baal and an Asherah — the wooden cult-pole representing the goddess — is unremarkable for a Manassite Iron I village. The household-religion finds at Tirzah, Taanach, and the Megiddo cult corners, with their Astarte plaques and incense stands, give the texture: this is not heretical Israelite practice in the eyes of Joash's neighbors, it is normal religion. That is what makes Gideon's nighttime demolition shocking and dangerous to him.

A handful of Hebrew terms repay attention. **בַּעַל (Baʿal)**, "lord/master," is both the Canaanite storm-god of the Ugaritic cycle and a generic title; the wordplay in v. 31 turns on whether Baal is a person who can defend himself. **אֲשֵׁרָה (ʾasherah)** is both the goddess and the wooden cult object. **יְרֻבַּעַל (Yerubbaʿal)**, Jerubbaal, is etymologized in v. 32 as "let Baal contend," but the form *yrb-bʿl* almost certainly means "Baal contends/multiplies/is great" in its native Canaanite sense; the narrator inverts the theophoric. **גִּדְעוֹן (Gidʿon)** comes from *gdʿ*, "to hew down" — a name that prophesies the altar-felling. **מַלְאַךְ יְהוָה (malʾak YHWH)**, the "messenger of YHWH," appears and is then equated with YHWH himself (vv. 12, 14, 16); the ambiguity is theological, not careless. **יְהוָה שָׁלוֹם (YHWH shalom)**, the altar's name, etiologizes the cult site Gideon founds; it remains "to this day" — the editorial signature of a writer addressing readers who can still see the stones.

## Lens 2 — The Literature

Judges 6 is a textbook biblical call narrative, and Robert Alter, Yairah Amit, and Susan Niditch have all mapped it against the type-scene whose other canonical instances are Moses at the burning bush (Exodus 3–4), the call of Saul (1 Samuel 9–10), Jeremiah's commissioning (Jeremiah 1), and Isaiah 6. The pattern has set elements: a divine confrontation in an unexpected place; a commissioning word; the recipient's protest of unworthiness ("my clan is the weakest in Manasseh, and I am the least in my father's house" — almost verbatim Saul in 1 Sam. 9:21 and Moses in Exodus 3:11); a divine reassurance of presence ("I will be with you"); a sign. Gideon hits every beat. The signs, however, are unusually intense: the consuming fire on the rock, the demolition-and-renaming, and then the doubled fleece — wet then dry. Where Moses gets three signs given by God, Gideon demands the signs himself, and demands them after he has already received one. The narrator is letting the reader notice.

Within the call frame, the chapter is built as a triptych. The prophetic oracle (vv. 7–10) sets the theological terms; the angelic commissioning at Ophrah (vv. 11–24) gives the personal call; the iconoclasm at the family altar (vv. 25–32) makes the call public. Then a coda (vv. 33–35) deploys Gideon militarily, and the fleece pericope (vv. 36–40) re-opens his hesitation — a structural surprise, since the type-scene should be closed by v. 24. That re-opening is the literary fingerprint of a man whose faith has not finally been settled, and it sets up the further reductions of chapter 7.

The text is dense with irony and verbal play. The "mighty man of valor" (*gibbor he-ḥayil*) is hiding in a winepress. The clan's "weakest" man tears down the strong man's altar. The name Jerubbaal mocks the very god who cannot defend himself. The bull (vv. 25–27) does double work: the second bull is both wrecking machine (pulling down the altar) and burnt offering. The doubled fleece structurally mirrors the doubled bull — Gideon's pattern is to test, then test again.

The most important textual variant in the chapter — and one of the most important in all of Judges — is at vv. 7–10. The fragment 4QJudg-a, edited by Julio Trebolle Barrera in DJD XIV, lacks these verses entirely; the text moves from v. 6 directly to v. 11. This omission is not a scribal accident — the seam is clean and the narrative reads coherently without the prophet. The standard inference, defended by Trebolle, Eugene Ulrich, Emanuel Tov, and Richard Nelson, is that vv. 7–10 are a deuteronomistic insertion: the unnamed prophet's speech recapitulates the deuteronomic covenant frame (Exodus deliverance, the warning against "the gods of the Amorites in whose land you dwell") in language indistinguishable from Judges 2:1–5 and 10:11–14. Reading 4QJudg-a alongside MT, you can watch the deuteronomistic editor at work, splicing his interpretive theology onto an older Gideon tradition. This is among the strongest text-critical cases anywhere in the Hebrew Bible for the reality of redactional layers.

LXX A and LXX B diverge in the chapter at smaller points; the Old Greek of Judges is famously a textual mess, and Rahlfs printed both recensions in parallel. The variants are mostly stylistic, not theological — the 4QJudg-a omission is the one that carries weight.

In the larger argument of the book, Judges 6 marks the central turn. The sequence of judges has been moving in a direction: Othniel is heroic and clean; Ehud is heroic and bloody; Deborah and Barak are mixed; Gideon will be ambivalent; Abimelech will be a disaster; Jephthah and Samson will be tragedies. Daniel Block and Barry Webb both treat Gideon as the hinge: through chapter 6 the book is still capable of presenting deliverance as a real good; from chapter 8 forward, every "deliverer" is a problem. The literary placement of the doubled fleece at the end of chapter 6 — already too much hesitation for a hero — prepares the reader for what is coming.

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

The canonical arc that Judges 6 opens is the long arc of Israel's struggle to be governed without becoming like the nations. The deuteronomistic frame reads the chapter as part of the cycle that finally requires monarchy as a divine concession (1 Samuel 8); the Chronicler ignores Judges almost entirely; the New Testament appropriates Gideon by name only once, in Hebrews 11:32, where he stands in a list of those who "through faith conquered kingdoms." That single citation has done enormous work in subsequent Christian exegesis.

**Jewish Interpretive Trajectory.** Pseudo-Philo's *Liber Antiquitatum Biblicarum* (chs. 35–36, late first century CE) retells the Gideon material with substantial expansion: Pseudo-Philo invents speeches for the angel and supplies an extended explanation of why God chose so unlikely a man, framing Gideon's hesitations as appropriate humility rather than weakness. Josephus (*Antiquities* 5.6) sanitizes Gideon further, presenting the call as straightforward and the iconoclasm at Ophrah as an act of pure piety. The Babylonian Talmud (*Rosh Hashanah* 25a–b) lists Gideon, along with Jephthah and Samuel, as a judge whose authority was definitive in his generation — the pragmatic rabbinic point being that the leadership God gives is the leadership you must accept, however small the man may seem. Rashi on 6:11 treats the threshing-in-a-winepress detail as a sign of Israelite poverty and Gideon's filial piety (he is hiding the grain to feed his family). Radak and Ralbag, more philosophically inclined, focus on the angel: Radak treats the *malʾak YHWH* as a created intermediary (consistent with Maimonidean negative theology); Ralbag reads the appearance as a prophetic vision. The fleece, in the Jewish trajectory, is read as Gideon's pragmatic skepticism — not unbelief, but an honest test consistent with Deuteronomy 13's command to evaluate signs. Yair Zakovitch's literary readings emphasize the etymological play on Gideon's name and on Jerubbaal as a polemic against the cult center at Shechem. Jon Levenson's broader work on divine accommodation gives a Jewish theological frame for the fleece pericope: the God of Israel, unlike the gods of the philosophers, condescends to negotiate with his servants.

**Christian Interpretive Trajectory.** Origen's *Homilies on Judges* (Homily 8, on Gideon) reads almost everything in Gideon's story typologically. The fleece, dry then wet, is Christ's incarnation: the wool of the fleece (Israel) receives the dew of the Spirit while the world is dry; then in the church age, the world is moistened with the gospel and the fleece (Israel) is dry. Ambrose of Milan, in *De Spiritu Sancto* and *De Virginibus*, took Origen's typology a decisive step further: the fleece is Mary, dewed by the Holy Spirit while the world remained barren of God's presence. This Marian reading of the fleece becomes the standard medieval iconography — paintings and tapestries of the Annunciation routinely include a fleece motif — and is cited approvingly by Bernard of Clairvaux, by Thomas Aquinas (*Summa* III, q. 27), and (with reservations) by Calvin. Augustine, in *Quaestiones in Heptateuchum* and various sermons, treated Gideon's iconoclasm at Ophrah as the model for Christian destruction of pagan altars in his own North African setting; he also worked the doubled fleece into a meditation on grace and human weakness. Theodoret of Cyrus follows Origen on the fleece. Aquinas, asking whether Gideon sinned in demanding a sign, answers no: he distinguishes between testing God in unbelief (forbidden, Deut. 6:16) and seeking confirmation of a genuine call (permitted, even commendable when the cost of obedience is high). Calvin's sermons on Judges (preached 1561, published posthumously) read Gideon as an exemplum of weak faith made strong by God's patient accommodation; Calvin emphasizes the doubled fleece as evidence that "God deals with us not as we deserve but as our weakness requires." Luther, characteristically, makes Gideon an emblem of *sola fide*: the man who knew himself the weakest is the man God uses, because faith and not strength saves. In the modern critical tradition, Wellhausen treated the chapter as a composite of an old Ophrah cult etiology and a deuteronomistic frame; Noth integrated this into his Deuteronomistic History thesis; Boling (Anchor Bible) and Soggin gave it a careful philological treatment; Webb and Block read it canonically as the start of the book's downward trajectory; Brueggemann and Polzin emphasize the political theology, the warning that even a good deliverer accumulates the symbols of kingship; the feminist readings of Exum and Bal, more on chapters 8–9 and 11 than 6, attend to the all-male iconoclasm and the cost of "saving" a society that will then be governed by warrior shame.

**Where the traditions converge and diverge.** Both Jewish and Christian readers agree that Gideon's hesitations are real and theologically significant; the patristic and medieval Christian tradition reads them allegorically (the fleece as Mary, as church, as Christ), while the Jewish tradition reads them in the plain sense as legitimate skepticism within a covenantal framework. The Christian Marian reading of the fleece is among the clearest cases of allegorical exegesis becoming a feature of liturgical and visual culture: a piece of Iron Age textile divination becomes a Marian icon. The Jewish tradition, by contrast, makes the fleece a teaching about how to seek confirmation of a calling without violating the prohibition on testing God — a halakhic, not a typological, reading. Both traditions agree, against any flattening modern reading, that the fleece is theologically significant rather than psychologically embarrassing.

## Lens 4 — The Theological Intent

What the deuteronomistic editor believed God was doing in Judges 6 is, on the surface, straightforward: punishing covenant-breaking, hearing the cry of his people, raising up a deliverer. But the chapter as it stands also says things the editor did not need to say, and those excesses are where the theology is.

**Tension One: Divine accommodation versus the cost of weak faith.** Gideon asks for sign upon sign — the meal, the fleece wet, the fleece dry — and God gives him each one. Calvin and Aquinas read this as God's pastoral patience: the work of saving Israel cannot wait for Gideon to grow up, and so God meets him where he is. The Catholic and Lutheran traditions broadly affirm this as the nature of grace; God's accommodation is not a concession to sin but an expression of fatherhood. The Reformed-confessional reading keeps Calvin's pastoral note but adds, with Webb and Block, a darker counterpoint: every accommodation is also a delay, and a man who needs three signs to cross a river will, by chapter 8, build a golden ephod and accept the substance of kingship while denying its name. The Jewish reading (Levenson, Zakovitch) presents the signs as Gideon's appropriate response to a genuinely high-stakes call — not weakness at all, but covenantal due diligence. The critical reading (Niditch, Soggin) treats the doubled fleece as a folkloric duplication, a literary marker of an old oracular practice that the writer has preserved without endorsing. The position least intuitive to most modern readers is probably the Calvin-Aquinas one — that God's willingness to be tested is itself a high doctrine of grace, not a deflation of God's transcendence. Steelmanned: a God who refuses to be approached by the weak is not a God you can preach to actual humans; the fleece is the doctrinal foundation of every later Christian and Jewish account of how a finite creature negotiates a real relationship with the infinite without thereby leveling the asymmetry. **Where this leaves us:** the chapter is comfortable with a faith that arrives in pieces, but it is not naive about the cost of arriving slowly.

**Tension Two: Iconoclasm and the religion of one's own house.** Gideon is commanded to tear down his father's altar to Baal and the Asherah beside it. He does it at night, because he is afraid of his family and his neighbors. The town wakes and demands his death. The deuteronomistic theology is unambiguous: there is one God and the local gods are nothing, and any cult that competes with YHWH must be destroyed. The Augustinian and Reformed traditions inherit this directly; Augustine cites Gideon when his bishops break down pagan altars in fifth-century North Africa, and Calvin's reformers cite him when they whitewash medieval cathedrals in sixteenth-century Geneva. The Catholic tradition is more cautious: Aquinas affirms the principle (false worship is destroyable) but limits the practice (the prince and not the private man wields the sword of cult-correction). The Orthodox tradition, with its high doctrine of liturgical place, is uneasy with the cleanliness of the deuteronomistic line; the holy is mediated through material, and the line between an idol and an icon is a line drawn by the church, not by individual zealots. The Jewish tradition (Rashi, Radak) accepts the iconoclasm as right but preserves the texture of the family's distress: this was Joash's altar, and the violation is real even when the cause is just. The critical and feminist readings (Bal, Exum, Brettler) press a further question: whose religion gets called false? The household religion of Iron I Israel — the Asherah pole, the standing stones, the bread offerings to the queen of heaven that Jeremiah 44 describes the women of Judah defending — was the religion of women and ordinary families; the centralizing prophetic reform was the project of male elites in Jerusalem. The position least intuitive to a Protestant reader is the Orthodox/feminist one: that the destruction of a household altar is not only a defense of monotheism but also a violence against a domestic and matrilineal religious life that the centralizing project could not absorb. Steelmanned: the deuteronomistic editor's confidence that his theology is identical with God's may itself be the kind of certainty against which the prophets warn. **Where this leaves us:** the chapter wants its iconoclasm celebrated, but it also preserves enough of the family's grief — Joash's defense of his son is also a grief over his altar — that a careful reader cannot pretend the violence was costless.

The chapter ends with a question that the rest of the book will not let go: if a man needs the dew tested twice before he will fight, what will he be like once the fight is won?

## The Thread

The Spirit-clothing in v. 34 — *ruaḥ YHWH lavshah et-Gidʿon*, "the Spirit of the LORD clothed Gideon" — uses a verb that recurs only twice more in the Hebrew Bible: of Amasai in 1 Chronicles 12:18 and of Zechariah son of Jehoiada in 2 Chronicles 24:20, who is then murdered for his prophecy. The metaphor is an inversion of the usual: the Spirit is not poured into the man but wraps around him from outside, as a garment. When you reach the Pentecost narrative in Acts 2 with its tongues "as of fire" resting on each, the inversion runs the other way and the metaphor changes again — but the verb stays in the background.

The Marian fleece reading reaches its full development in medieval iconography, not in any single biblical text. When you encounter the *Hortus Conclusus* tradition, or the Annunciation panels of Jan van Eyck, the dewed fleece is in the iconography for a reason; you are looking at Origen and Ambrose painted onto a board.

When you get to 1 Samuel 8 and Israel's demand for a king, remember that Gideon has already declined the title in name while building the house of a king in fact (chapter 8 will spell this out); the deuteronomistic editor has been preparing the reader to think of the monarchy as a relapse, not an innovation.

When you get to the prophet's speech in Judges 10:11–14, where YHWH tells Israel to go cry to the gods they chose, hold the unnamed prophet of 6:7–10 next to it; the parallel diction is the deuteronomistic editor's signature, and 4QJudg-a's omission of the earlier passage is the textual evidence that he was a real editor working on real material.
