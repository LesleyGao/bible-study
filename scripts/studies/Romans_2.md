# Romans 2: The Trap and the Inward Jew

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

Romans 2 is composed in the same Corinthian winter of 56–57 CE as the rest of the letter, but its rhetorical posture takes us deeper into the mixed Jewish-Gentile reality of the Roman house-churches. After Claudius's expulsion edict of 49 CE (Suetonius, *Life of Claudius* 25.4; Acts 18:2) lapsed with the emperor's death in 54 CE, Jewish believers returning to Rome found Gentile-led congregations whose leaders had reorganized worship, governance, and Sabbath practice along non-Torah-observant lines. The tension was not abstract. Whose interpretation of the Mosaic covenant counted? Whose ritual claims defined the community? The diatribe partner of Romans 2—introduced abruptly at 2:1 with the vocative *ō anthrōpe* ("O man")—stands in for one or another voice in this conversation.

The historical question of whom Paul is addressing across chapter 2 is unsettled. Cranfield, Moo, Schreiner, and the dominant Reformation tradition read the interlocutor as a Jew, with the moralizing judgment of 2:1–11 functioning as a setup for the explicit Jewish address at 2:17. **Stanley Stowers** in *A Rereading of Romans* (1994), and **Runar Thorsteinsson** in *Paul's Interlocutor in Romans 2: Function and Identity in the Context of Ancient Epistolography* (2003), argue instead that 2:1–16 addresses a *Gentile* moralizer—a Greek philosophical pretender of the kind Epictetus and Musonius Rufus excoriated—and that Paul does not turn to the Jewish interlocutor until 2:17. The case rests on rhetorical convention: in the Hellenistic diatribe, the *ō anthrōpe* address is generic, and the philosophical critique of moralizing hypocrisy targets pretenders to wisdom across ethnic lines. The chapter then unfolds as a deliberate two-part diatribe: first the Gentile moralist (2:1–16), then the Jewish boaster (2:17–29), with both reduced to the same standing under God's judgment.

Material context matters. The Roman world Paul is writing into was a culture of intense ethnic boundary-marking. The Mishnah Avodah Zarah, redacted later but reflecting earlier traditions, codifies elaborate rules for keeping separate from Gentile food, drink, and household objects; conversely, Roman elite literature (Tacitus, *Histories* 5.4–5; Juvenal, *Satires* 14) records sneering Gentile descriptions of Jewish *separatism*. **Erich Gruen**'s *Diaspora: Jews amidst Greeks and Romans* and **John Barclay**'s *Jews in the Mediterranean Diaspora* document how circumcision, Sabbath, and food laws functioned as the most visible markers of Jewish identity in Greco-Roman cities. When Paul writes about circumcision in 2:25–29, he is not raising a private religious question but striking at the most public and contested marker of ethnic boundary in his world.

The textual situation for chapter 2 is stable. **𝔓⁴⁶** (c. 200 CE), **Codex Vaticanus**, **Codex Sinaiticus**, and the major Greek witnesses preserve a uniform text without significant variants. The chapter's text-critical issues are minor—a few articles and conjunctions—rather than substantive. There is, however, a long tradition of considering whether 2:16 ("on that day when . . . God judges the secrets of men") interrupts the flow from 2:15 to 2:17 and might be a parenthesis or even a later marginal gloss; Bultmann famously argued for excision, but the manuscript evidence does not support it.

Three Greek terms reward attention. **Krima** (κρῖμα, 2:2, 3) is "judgment" in the forensic sense, the sentence handed down. **Metanoia** (μετάνοια, 2:4) is "repentance" but more literally a "change of mind"—the cognitive-volitional reorientation that God's *chrēstotēs* (kindness) is meant to elicit. **Peritomē** (περιτομή, 2:25–29) is "circumcision," the chapter's climactic concept; Paul's argument in 2:28–29 distinguishes peritomē *en sarki* (in the flesh) from peritomē *kardias* (of the heart) *en pneumati* (in/by the Spirit), drawing on Deut 10:16, Deut 30:6, Jer 4:4, and Ezek 44:7, 9. The phrase **erga tou nomou** (ἔργα τοῦ νόμου, "works of the law") does not appear in chapter 2 in the technical sense it carries in 3:20, 28, but its conceptual ground is laid here.

## Lens 2 — The Literature

Romans 2 is the most rhetorically virtuosic chapter in the letter and the clearest demonstration of Paul's diatribe technique. The chapter opens with one of the New Testament's most famous rhetorical traps: 2:1, "*Therefore* you have no excuse, O man, every one of you who judges." The *therefore* (*dio*) connects abruptly back to 1:32, where Paul has just said that those who do such things "deserve to die" and yet "give approval to those who practice them." The reader who has nodded along through the indictment of pagan disorder in 1:18–32 finds himself suddenly indicted at 2:1 for the very act of approving Paul's diagnosis. This is *prosōpopoiia*—speech-in-character constructed to expose the listener's own complicity.

**Stanley Stowers**, *Diatribe and Paul's Letter to the Romans* (1981), traced the form back to Bion of Borysthenes, Teles, and the Stoic-Cynic preachers; in Paul's own century, Epictetus's *Discourses* (preserved by Arrian) provide the closest stylistic parallels. The diatribe's signature features all appear in Romans 2: direct address to an imagined interlocutor (2:1, 3, 17), rhetorical questions (2:3, 4, 21–22, 26–27), the false-objection-and-correction pattern, and the *prosōpopoiia* that voices the imagined speaker's position. Reading Paul as if these were neutral assertions of his own settled views misreads the literary form.

The chapter divides clearly into two diatribe units. The first (2:1–16) addresses an unnamed moralizing judge—Gentile in the Stowers/Thorsteinsson reading, Jewish or generic in the traditional reading—and unfolds the principle of impartial judgment "according to works" (2:6). The second (2:17–29) explicitly addresses someone "who calls yourself a Jew" (2:17) and dismantles the boast in Torah possession with a brutal series of rhetorical questions: "you who teach others, do you not teach yourself? While you preach against stealing, do you steal?" (2:21). The structural symmetry between the two units is deliberate: each ends with a paradoxical reversal in which the apparent insider is shown to be excluded and the apparent outsider included. In 2:13–16, Gentiles "who do by nature things required by the law" turn out to "show that the work of the law is written on their hearts." In 2:25–29, the circumcised Jew who breaks Torah becomes effectively uncircumcised, while the uncircumcised Gentile who keeps Torah's precepts is reckoned as circumcised.

This pattern of paradoxical reversal is itself a literary inheritance from the Hebrew prophets, particularly Jeremiah and Ezekiel, both of whom proclaim that physical circumcision without circumcision of the heart is no circumcision at all (Jer 4:4; 9:25–26; Ezek 44:7, 9). Paul's 2:28–29—"a Jew is one inwardly, and circumcision is a matter of the heart, by the Spirit, not by the letter"—is a deliberate compression of Jeremiah and Ezekiel's prophetic critique. **Richard Hays**, *Echoes of Scripture in the Letters of Paul*, traces the intertextual layering carefully: Paul is not abolishing the prophetic critique but extending it.

The chapter also contains one of Paul's most striking pieces of natural-theology vocabulary at 2:14–15. Gentiles "by nature do what the law requires" and "are a law to themselves," demonstrating that "the work of the law is written on their hearts." The phrasing draws on Stoic natural law discourse familiar from Cicero (*De Legibus*, *De Re Publica*) and from Hellenistic Jewish writers like Philo (*De Specialibus Legibus*). Whether Paul is making a *theological* claim about an actual Gentile righteousness or a *rhetorical* claim within the diatribe (i.e., setting up the reversal he is about to spring) is one of the chapter's interpretive cruces—we will engage it under Lens 4.

The textual variant question worth raising for chapter 2 is the ongoing debate about whether 2:6—"He will render to each one according to his works"—reflects Paul's settled doctrine or whether it functions within the diatribe as a hypothetical. The wording is verbatim from LXX Psalm 62:13 and Proverbs 24:12; it is one of the most uncomfortable verses in Paul's corpus for the Reformation tradition because it sounds straightforwardly works-righteousness. We engage this under Lens 4.

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

Romans 2 has been read in radically different ways by the four major modern interpretive schools, and the differences are sharper here than perhaps anywhere in the letter.

The **Old Perspective** (Augustine, Luther, Calvin, Cranfield, Moo, Schreiner) reads 2:1–16 as a hypothetical argument: *if* anyone could perfectly do the law, *then* God would justify them; the entire passage functions to show that no one in fact does this, setting up the universal verdict of 3:9, 19–20, 23. Cranfield's careful argument is that 2:6, 13—"render to each according to his works" / "the doers of the law will be justified"—is *theoretically true* but *practically void* because no one is in fact a doer. Augustine reads 2:14–15 as describing not unconverted Gentiles but Gentile *Christians* who have the law written on their hearts by the Spirit (a position Augustine works out in *De Spiritu et Littera*, his most important Romans-engaged treatise). The chapter therefore prepares the indictment of universal sin without offering any actual category of righteous-by-works exception.

The **New Perspective on Paul** (Sanders, Dunn, Wright) reads chapter 2 as targeting **Jewish covenant boasting**—not Jewish "works-righteousness" in the abstract sense the Reformation supposed, but Jewish ethnic privilege, the assumption that physical descent and circumcision alone secure covenant standing. Wright reads 2:17–29 as the dismantling of *kavod-Israel* boasting and 2:25–29 as the redefinition of "true Jew" around heart-circumcision (compare Phil 3:3). Wright's argument runs that Paul is not saying "Jews must abandon Jewishness" but "the marks of being God's people have been redrawn by the Spirit, and Gentiles can now be included on the same terms." Dunn's analysis of "works of the law" as boundary markers (circumcision, Sabbath, food laws) finds its preparation in chapter 2's explicit focus on circumcision (2:25–29).

The **Apocalyptic Paul** school (Käsemann, Martyn, Gaventa, **Douglas Campbell**) reads chapter 2 differently still. Käsemann insists that 2:6 must mean what it says—judgment according to works is part of Paul's eschatology, even alongside justification by faith. Campbell, in *The Deliverance of God* (2009), reads 1:18–3:20 as Paul's elaborate ventriloquy of a Jewish-Christian "Teacher" whose theology Paul is *demolishing*, not endorsing; on this reading, the harsh judgment-according-to-works framework of chapter 2 is what Paul is *parodying*, not preaching. Campbell's thesis is controversial—Cranfield and the mainstream commentaries reject it as exegetically unsustainable—but it has provoked productive engagement from Wright, Beverly Gaventa, and others. Gaventa's own apocalyptic reading is gentler: chapter 2 establishes the universal scope of human disorder under the powers, against which God's apocalyptic invasion through Christ will provide the only rescue.

The **Paul Within Judaism / Radical New Perspective** (Nanos, Eisenbaum, **Matthew Thiessen**, Fredriksen) reads chapter 2 as a continuation of the *Gentile-only* argument begun in chapter 1. The Jewish interlocutor of 2:17ff is a *teacher of Gentiles*, not a representative of Judaism in general; Paul's critique is of Jewish-Christian missionaries who would impose Torah observance on Gentile converts, not of Judaism's core conviction that Israel's covenant remains operative. Thiessen's *Paul and the Gentile Problem* and *A Jewish Paul* read 2:25–29 as redefining what counts as covenant standing *for Gentiles*—Spirit-circumcision is the new mode of Gentile inclusion, not a universal abrogation of Jewish flesh-circumcision. Israel's covenant remains. Fredriksen's *Paul: The Pagan's Apostle* makes the same case at greater length.

The story of how Romans 2 has been read tracks the broader interpretive arc. **Origen**'s *Commentary on Romans* reads 2:14–15 as evidence of natural law and Gentile capacity for moral response—a position consistent with his moderate anthropology. **Augustine**'s reading hardens against any optimistic reading of Gentile righteousness; in *De Spiritu et Littera*, he insists that Paul's "Gentiles who do by nature what the law requires" must refer to Gentile Christians under grace, not to natural Gentiles—the first major ecclesiastical narrowing of the chapter's apparent scope. **Pelagius**, in his *Commentary on Romans* (recovered by Souter 1922–31), reads 2:14–15 as describing actual Gentile capacity, against Augustine's Christianizing reading—and this is the precise textual point at which the Pelagian controversy turns. **Chrysostom**'s 32 *Homilies on Romans* take a middle path: Gentiles do have a natural moral capacity, but it is insufficient for salvation without Christ.

**Aquinas**'s *Lectura* on Romans reads 2:14–15 within his developed natural-law framework: the *lex naturalis* written on the heart is genuine, real, and provides the rational ground for moral discernment, though it does not constitute saving grace. Aquinas's reading became the standard Catholic position. **Luther**'s *Lectures on Romans* (1515–16) read chapter 2 as a thoroughgoing indictment of human pretension, with 2:13—"the doers of the law shall be justified"—as a hypothetical that exists only to expose the universal failure to be such doers. Calvin's *Commentary on Romans* (1540) follows Luther closely.

The 20th and 21st centuries have argued about chapter 2 with unusual intensity. **Krister Stendahl**'s "Apostle Paul and the Introspective Conscience of the West" (1963) argued that Western readings of chapter 2 had imported a late-medieval anxiety about the conscience that Paul did not share; Paul's question is corporate-historical (what about Israel? what about the Gentiles?) not introspective-individual (am I righteous?). Stendahl's reframing prepared the ground for Sanders. **E. P. Sanders**'s *Paul and the Law and the Jewish People* (1983) wrestled directly with the apparent contradiction between 2:13 ("doers of the law will be justified") and 3:20 ("by works of the law no one will be justified") and argued that Paul's argument is incoherent if read as a single forensic schema and coherent only if read as multiple arguments to multiple problems. **N. T. Wright**'s *Paul and the Faithfulness of God* offers the most extensive recent New Perspective treatment. **Douglas Campbell**'s *The Deliverance of God* (2009) and his shorter *Paul: An Apostle's Journey* (2018) press the apocalyptic ventriloquy thesis. **Matthew Thiessen**'s *A Jewish Paul* (2023) is the most accessible contemporary Paul-Within-Judaism statement of how the chapter targets *Jewish-Christian missionaries to Gentiles*, not Judaism itself.

The four schools differ irreducibly. The Old Perspective hears chapter 2 as a setup for universal indictment; the New Perspective hears it as a critique of Jewish ethnic boasting; Apocalyptic hears it as part of Paul's exposure of human bondage to the powers; Paul Within Judaism hears it as a Gentile-targeted argument that does not abrogate the Jewish covenant. Each is doing real exegetical work; none is straightforwardly wrong; the chapter is doing more than any single school can capture.

## Lens 4 — The Theological Intent

**Tension 1: How are we to read 2:13—"the doers of the law who will be justified"?**

The **Reformed / Old Perspective** position (Cranfield, Moo, Schreiner) reads 2:13 as a *hypothetical*. The verse states the principle—judgment is according to actual obedience, not mere possession of the law—but the principle is theoretically true and practically void, because no one in fact perfectly obeys. The verse is part of the prosecuting argument that drives toward 3:9, 19–20, 23. Justification by works of the law is *theoretically possible but empirically impossible*; therefore justification must come by another route, namely faith (3:21–26).

The **Catholic** position (Fitzmyer, Brendan Byrne, Joseph Ratzinger) reads 2:13 as expressing Paul's actual eschatological doctrine: God will judge according to works, and the genuine doers of the law (under grace, energized by the Spirit) will indeed be justified. The verse is consistent with James 2:24 and with the broader Catholic tradition that justification is both gift and transformation. The Council of Trent's Decree on Justification (Session VI, 1547) reads 2:13 in this register.

The **Wesleyan-Arminian** position (Wesley, Adam Clarke; in modern form Ben Witherington's *Paul's Letter to the Romans: A Socio-Rhetorical Commentary*) reads 2:13 as describing the genuine outcome of God's *gracious enablement*—those whom God's prevenient grace has reached and who have responded in obedient faith will indeed be doers and will indeed be justified. The verse is not hypothetical but real-but-grace-empowered.

The **New Perspective** position (Wright, Dunn) reads 2:13 within the framework of *eschatological judgment according to a life lived*. Wright distinguishes "present justification" (which is by faith alone, in the sense of declaring covenant membership in advance of the verdict) from "future justification" (which is according to "the totality of the life lived"—Wright's phrase—and is a real eschatological judgment on the whole pattern of obedience produced by the Spirit). The two are not in contradiction; they are two moments of the same judgment, anchored in two stages of God's saving action.

The **Apocalyptic** position (Käsemann, Gaventa) takes 2:13 as part of Paul's eschatological framework: God's final judgment is real and is according to works, and only the new creation in Christ provides the foundation for confident standing.

**Steelmanning**: Cranfield's hypothetical reading rightly hears that Paul's argument in chapters 1–3 drives toward universal indictment and that 2:13 cannot be read in isolation from 3:20. Fitzmyer's Catholic reading rightly hears that Paul does not appear to *bracket* 2:13 as merely theoretical—he writes it as a positive principle of eschatological judgment. The Wesleyan reading rightly hears that grace and obedience are not mutually exclusive in Paul. Wright's two-moment framework rightly hears that Paul has both an inaugurated and a final justification in view.

**Where this leaves us**: The Reformed hypothetical reading is exegetically weakest at 2:6–10, which states the principle of judgment according to works in positive terms without obvious bracketing. The Catholic and Wesleyan readings are stronger here, though they bear the interpretive cost of having to reconcile 2:13 with 3:20 across a unified Pauline soteriology. Wright's framework is the most ambitious attempt at integration—a present declaration anchored in faith and a final verdict according to the life lived—and it has the merit of taking 2:13 seriously without sacrificing 3:21–26. Whichever reading we adopt, we cannot pretend Paul did not write 2:6 and 2:13. The chapter resists a flat *sola fide* reading; it also resists a flat works-righteousness reading. The relation between faith and the obedient life is one of the unresolved tensions of Pauline theology.

**Tension 2: Who is a Jew? (2:17–29 and especially 2:28–29)**

The **traditional Christian / supersessionist** reading (much of the patristic and medieval tradition; in modern form, parts of the Reformed and dispensational traditions) reads 2:28–29—"no one is a Jew who is merely one outwardly . . . a Jew is one inwardly, and circumcision is a matter of the heart"—as the *redefinition* of "Jew" around Christian identity. The church has *replaced* ethnic Israel as the people of God. Whoever has heart-circumcision is a Jew, and whoever does not (whatever their ethnic identity) is not.

The **Reformed / covenantal** reading (Cranfield, Moo) softens this: Paul is not abolishing the Jew/Gentile distinction but redefining who within Israel is a true Israelite (cf. 9:6, "not all who are descended from Israel are Israel"). The argument is *internal to Israel*—a critique of Jews who possess the covenant signs without the covenant heart—and prepares the way for the inclusion of Gentile believers as full members of the covenant family without abolishing Israel's ongoing role.

The **New Perspective** reading (Wright, Dunn) reads 2:28–29 as the eschatological redefinition of God's people around the Spirit-given heart, not around ethnic descent. Wright argues that Paul is not creating a Christian/Jewish binary but redrawing the lines of the covenant family: the Spirit's circumcision of the heart is the eschatological reality both Old Testament prophecy (Deut 30, Jer 31, Ezek 36) and Christian experience attest. Gentile believers therefore enter the renewed covenant on the same terms as Jewish believers—through the Spirit, not through ethnic descent or boundary-marker observance.

The **Paul Within Judaism** reading (Nanos, Thiessen, Fredriksen, Eisenbaum) reads 2:28–29 as redefining what counts as covenant standing *for Gentiles*. Paul is not telling Jews they are no longer Jews if they fail to have heart-circumcision; he is telling *Gentiles* that they can be full members of God's eschatological people through Spirit-circumcision without becoming proselytes (without flesh-circumcision). On this reading, ethnic Jews remain ethnic Jews, Torah remains the proper way of life for Jewish followers of Jesus, and the Gentile inclusion happens *alongside* rather than *instead of* the continuing Jewish covenant.

The **Eastern Orthodox** reading (Theodoret, John Chrysostom; in modern form, Fr. Theodore Stylianopoulos, Fr. John Romanides) emphasizes the *theosis* dimension: heart-circumcision by the Spirit is the inward transformative work that constitutes participation in the divine life. Less polemical against Jewish identity, more focused on the pneumatological reality.

**Steelmanning**: The supersessionist reading rightly hears that Paul *does* relativize the boundary markers and redefines covenant identity in a way that opens to Gentiles. But it overreads the text into a replacement theology that Romans 9–11 will explicitly repudiate (11:1, "Has God rejected his people? By no means!"). The Reformed/covenantal reading rightly hears that the redefinition is internal to Israel's story rather than its abolition. The New Perspective rightly hears that the chapter's whole argument is about who counts as God's people in the eschatological present. The Paul Within Judaism reading rightly hears that Paul did not in fact tell Jewish-Christians to abandon Torah (cf. Acts 21:20–24, where Paul affirms the Jerusalem Jewish-Christians' continued Torah observance) and that the chapter's argument is plausibly aimed at a Gentile inclusion question rather than a Jewish identity question.

**Where this leaves us**: After Auschwitz, no honest Christian reader can return to the supersessionism that dominated 1500 years of Romans interpretation. Whether we go with Wright's eschatological renewal of the one covenant family, with the Paul Within Judaism reading's preservation of two parallel covenant identities, or with some mediation, the chapter requires us to think more carefully than the older tradition did about what Paul is and is not saying. He is not saying ethnic Jewishness no longer matters; he is saying that *something more than ethnic Jewishness* is the condition of belonging to God's renewed people.

**Unresolved question**: Does Romans 2:14–15—Gentiles who "by nature do what the law requires"—describe (a) actual unconverted Gentiles displaying genuine natural moral capacity (Pelagius, the Reformed natural-theology tradition, Aquinas), (b) Gentile *Christians* in whom the Spirit has written the law on the heart (Augustine, Cranfield), or (c) a rhetorical category serving the diatribe's reversal logic without making a settled anthropological claim (Stowers)? Where you stand here shapes much of how you read Pauline natural theology, the Catholic/Protestant divide on grace and nature, and contemporary debates about the church's engagement with non-Christian religious and moral traditions.

## The Thread

Romans 2 sets several threads that run through the rest of the letter:

**The principle of impartial judgment** (2:11, "God shows no partiality") is taken up at 3:22–23 ("there is no distinction; for all have sinned") and at 10:12 ("there is no distinction between Jew and Greek; the same Lord is Lord of all").

**Heart-circumcision by the Spirit** (2:28–29) finds its full development at 8:1–17 (life in the Spirit, the Spirit's witness with our spirit) and at 9:6 ("not all who are descended from Israel are Israel"). The eschatological heart-renewal promised by Jeremiah 31 and Ezekiel 36 becomes the pneumatological reality of chapter 8.

**The boast in God / boast in the law** (2:17, 23) is the negative pole that 3:27 ("then what becomes of our boasting? It is excluded") and 5:11 ("we also rejoice [boast] in God through our Lord Jesus Christ") will rework. Paul will allow boasting *in God*; he will not allow boasting *in the markers of ethnic privilege*.

**Judgment according to works** (2:6–11) finds a complex echo in 14:10–12 ("we will all stand before the judgment seat of God"). Paul holds together justification by faith and judgment according to works in a way that has resisted every neat schematization.

Paul's use of Isaiah at this critical juncture anticipates the heavy Isaiah engagement of chapters 9–11, where Israel's failure and restoration drive the argument.]
