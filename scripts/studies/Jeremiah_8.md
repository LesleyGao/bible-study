# Jeremiah 8: The Lying Pen of the Scribes and the Balm in Gilead

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

Jeremiah 8 continues the temple-sermon material that began at chapter 7 and runs through 8:3, then transitions into a new oracular cycle from 8:4 through 9:25. The chapter belongs, on the most plausible dating, to the early years of Jehoiakim (609–605 BC) — the period after Josiah's death at Megiddo, when the reform party had lost its royal patron, the Egyptian-installed Jehoiakim was reasserting older syncretistic practices, and the Babylonian threat was beginning to coalesce north of the Euphrates. Verse 16's "snorting of their horses is heard from Dan" places the foe-from-the-north imagery at the very northernmost limit of Israelite memory — Tel Dan, where Avraham Biran's excavations (1966 onward) recovered the Tel Dan Stele (1993) with its "House of David" reference and confirmed the city's role as Israel's traditional north-frontier marker.

The desecration of the dead in vv.1–3 — "the bones of the kings of Judah, the bones of its officials, the bones of the priests, the bones of the prophets, and the bones of the inhabitants of Jerusalem shall be brought out of their tombs… they shall be spread before the sun and the moon and all the host of heaven" — is the ultimate covenant-curse of the ancient Near East. Assyrian royal inscriptions (Ashurbanipal's Babylon campaign, 648 BC) brag explicitly about the desecration of royal tombs as ultimate humiliation. The Hebrew Bible's parallels at 2 Kings 23:16 (Josiah burning bones on the Bethel altar) and 2 Kings 9:33–37 (Jezebel's body becoming "as dung on the surface of the field") use the same vocabulary. Recent excavation of the Silwan necropolis south of Jerusalem and of First Temple period burial caves at Ketef Hinnom (Gabriel Barkay's excavations, 1979 onward, including the famous silver amulet scrolls of Numbers 6:24–26) has confirmed the high cultural value placed on intact burial in Iron II Judah; the threat at v.1 is calibrated against that value.

The "host of heaven" (*tseva' ha-shamayim*, v.2) cult is well-attested archaeologically. The Lachish Letter II references Yahwistic piety in the same period; standing stones at numerous late Iron II sites (Tel Arad's two-room shrine, Tel Beersheba's altar) and household figurine assemblages (the "Judean Pillar Figurines," over 1,000 examples from Iron II Judean homes, plausibly identified with Asherah) all witness the syncretistic practice Jer 8:1–2 indicts.

The chapter's most archaeologically suggestive image is *the balm of Gilead* (v.22). Gilead lay east of the Jordan, in the Transjordanian region across from Samaria. The medicinal "balm" (Hebrew *tzori*, צֳרִי) is most plausibly identified with the resin of *Commiphora gileadensis* (the Mecca myrrh tree, also called the Balsam of Gilead) — a small tree that produces aromatic resin valued in Egyptian, Phoenician, Greek, and Roman medicine. Pliny the Elder (*Natural History* 12.111–115) and Theophrastus (*Historia Plantarum* 9.6) describe the balm of Gilead trade in detail; ANE medical texts from Mesopotamia (the Diagnostic Handbook, the Sakikku) reference resin-based salves for wounds. By Jeremiah's time the trade was well-established; the rhetorical question "is there no balm in Gilead?" assumes the audience knows Gilead was *the* place to source medicinal resin.

Standard commentaries: Lundbom's Anchor (the maximalist Jeremianic-authorship reading), Holladay's Hermeneia (the careful philological reading, with its meticulous handling of MT/LXX divergences), Carroll's OTL (the redaction-critical reading), McKane's ICC (the rolling-corpus theory), Brueggemann's Eerdmans volumes, J.A. Thompson's NICOT (the steady evangelical-historical reading), Stulman, Kathleen O'Connor (*Jeremiah: Pain and Promise*, 2011, the trauma-informed reading whose contribution is most evident in chapter 8's lament-material at vv.18–22).

Key Hebrew terms: *meshuvah* (מְשׁוּבָה, "backsliding/turning away," v.5) — the noun-form of the Jeremianic leitwort *shuv*. *Mo'adim* (מוֹעֲדִים, v.7, "appointed times") — the same word used for Israel's pilgrimage festivals (Lev 23), here applied to migratory birds. *'Et soferim sheker* (עֵט סוֹפְרִים שָׁקֶר, v.8, "lying pen of the scribes") — the chapter's most contested phrase. *Tzori* (צֳרִי, v.22, "balm") — the resin of *Commiphora gileadensis*. *Bath-'ammi* (בַּת־עַמִּי, "daughter of my people," vv.19, 21, 22) — Jeremiah's signature term of grief for Judah, used eight times in chapters 8–9 alone, the densest concentration in the book.

## Lens 2 — The Literature

Form-critically, chapter 8 is more varied than chapters 6–7. Verses 1–3 close out the temple-sermon prose unit. Verses 4–13 are poetic oracles of judgment (with Mowinckel's A characteristics — short lines, vivid imagery, the leitwort *shuv*). Verses 14–17 are a dialogue-oracle alternating between the people's voice (vv.14–15, "why do we sit still?") and YHWH's voice (vv.16–17, "the snorting of their horses is heard from Dan"). Verses 18–22 introduce the prophet's lament — the genre that will dominate chapters 8:18–9:3, often called the first of "Jeremiah's confessions" by older scholarship though more recent work (Mark Smith's *The Laments of Jeremiah and Their Contexts*, 1990) prefers "laments" to distinguish them from formal individual lament psalms.

The MT/LXX divergence is more significant in this chapter than in chapters 6–7. The LXX is roughly 80 words shorter overall and rearranges some material. Most consequentially, the LXX of v.10–12 omits material that closely parallels Jer 6:13–15; whether this is a Septuagint shortening or a Masoretic doubling remains debated (Holladay defends the MT; Janzen and Tov tend toward the LXX as original).

Devices accumulate. The migratory-bird simile of v.7 — *the stork knows her mo'ed* — is one of the most subtle in the prophetic corpus: the technical festival-vocabulary applied to creation reverses the natural-religion / revealed-religion polarity and indicts Israel by analogy with what should have been beneath them. The poisoned-water of v.14 (*me-rosh*, מֵי־רֹאשׁ) puns on the Hebrew word for both "poison" and "head" — the cup of judgment as cup of doom that runs through Jer 25:15–17, Isa 51:17, and forward to Mark 14:36's "let this cup pass." The *harvest is past, the summer is ended, and we are not saved* (v.20) is the most concentrated single verse of late-season despair in the prophetic corpus; the temporal markers (*qatzir*, harvest; *qayitz*, late summer) point to the autumn liturgical horizon (Tabernacles is in early autumn) and to the agricultural year's closing without rescue.

The lament-section at vv.18–22 marks a structural shift. The voice modulates from divine wrath (vv.4–17) to prophetic grief (vv.18–22), with the "is the LORD not in Zion?" of v.19 playing as a dialogue between the despairing people and the indicting God. Kathleen O'Connor's 2011 reading takes the lament-passages as the chapter's emotional core and argues that the rhetoric of judgment in vv.4–17 only makes pastoral sense in light of the lament that grounds it.

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

Chapter 8 is the bridge from the temple-sermon material into the lament-cycle that runs from 8:18 through chapter 20. Without chapter 8's tonal pivot — from prosecution to grief — the laments would land as mere personal complaint; with chapter 8 as their introduction, the laments are read as the prophet's interior dimension of the public catastrophe.

In Jewish reception, chapter 8 has multiple deep tributaries. The Tisha B'Av liturgy uses Jer 8:18–9:1 extensively — the *bath-'ammi* lament becomes the structural core of the medieval *kinot* (lamentation poems) for the destruction of the temple. Lamentations Rabbah cites the chapter repeatedly. **Rashi** reads v.8 ("the lying pen of the scribes") as referring to scribes who falsified specific halakhic rulings to please the powerful; the verse becomes a touchstone in medieval Jewish polemics about scribal authority. **Radak** generalizes Rashi's reading. **Abrabanel** reads v.8 as a polemic against the syncretistic priestly faction that interpreted Torah to legitimize idolatrous practice. The Hasidic tradition reads vv.18–22 (the lament) through the lens of *galut ha-shekhinah* — the exile of God's presence with the people — making the chapter a touchstone of mystical-pastoral theology.

In Christian reception, **Origen's *Homilies on Jeremiah*** treats 8:22 (*is there no balm in Gilead?*) typologically: the absent physician of v.22 is the not-yet-arrived Christ, the balm is the gospel, and the chapter's despair is fulfilled in the Incarnation. **Jerome's *Commentary on Jeremiah*** develops this. The patristic and medieval reading of "balm in Gilead = Christ as physician" runs through nearly the entire pre-Reformation tradition. **Calvin's *Lectures on Jeremiah*** (1563) reads v.8 (the lying pen) as a Reformation polemic against scholastic-medieval glossing that had buried the plain sense of Scripture; the verse becomes a key Reformation proof-text for the principle of *sola scriptura* against medieval canonical interpretation. Luther preached on the verse in similar terms. The Puritan tradition (John Owen, the Westminster divines) used v.8 against Roman Catholic glossing.

The most consequential cultural reception of v.22 outside the seminary is the African American spiritual *There Is a Balm in Gilead* — the spiritual that *answers* Jeremiah's question affirmatively from the depths of slavery and Reconstruction-era suffering. The spiritual was first published in 1854 in *The Christian Lyre*, was widely sung in Black congregations through Reconstruction, and entered the broader American hymnody through the 20th century. The spiritual's theological move is striking: it takes Jeremiah's despairing question and answers it with a confidence forged in the experience of slavery — *"there is a balm in Gilead, to make the wounded whole; there is a balm in Gilead, to heal the sin-sick soul."* Howard Thurman, Martin Luther King Jr., and the Black Christian tradition more broadly have read the spiritual as evidence that the most decisive reception of biblical lament is by communities who know the actual conditions the text describes.

The critical reception of v.8 — *the lying pen of the scribes* — has produced one of the most contested readings in modern Jeremiah scholarship. **Karel van der Toorn's *Scribal Culture and the Making of the Hebrew Bible* (Harvard, 2007)** argues that the verse is evidence that Jeremiah accused the late-Judean scribal establishment of *forging* Torah text — that the verse is the prophet's accusation that the very texts being cited as "the law of the LORD" had been textually corrupted by the scribes who controlled them. Van der Toorn's reading has been resisted by more conservative scholars (Lundbom, Thompson) who read v.8 as indicting *misinterpretation* rather than *forgery*. The medieval-Jewish and Reformation readings (Rashi through Calvin) are generally on the misinterpretation side; the modern critical reading is more sympathetic to the forgery thesis. Bernhard Duhm's 1901 commentary tilts toward the radical reading; Mowinckel's 1914 source theory leaves the question open.

Where the traditions diverge: the Christian patristic-medieval tradition reads v.22 Christologically (the absent physician fulfilled in Christ); the Jewish tradition reads it as the diagnosis of exile (the question still unanswered, awaiting messianic resolution); the African American spiritual tradition reads it as a question already answered in the spiritual experience of suffering communities; the critical tradition reads it as pharmacological irony (the resin trade actually existed; the failure was political, not material). Likewise on v.8: Reformed *sola scriptura* reads it against medieval glossing; Catholic tradition reads it against bad-faith interpretation rather than against tradition itself; the radical critical reading (van der Toorn) reads it as accusation of textual forgery; the rabbinic reading reads it as halakhic-moral failure of specific scribes.

## Lens 4 — The Theological Intent

**Tension 1: The lying pen of the scribes — interpretation or forgery?** Verse 8's *'et soferim sheker* — *"the lying pen of the scribes has made it into a lie"* — sits at the intersection of textual criticism, scribal authority, and the theology of Scripture. The standard reading from Rashi through Calvin to most contemporary commentators is that the scribes have *misinterpreted* genuine Torah — produced false halakhic rulings, glossed bad-faith readings, applied the law selectively to protect the powerful. **Karel van der Toorn's *Scribal Culture and the Making of the Hebrew Bible*** (2007) takes the more radical reading: the scribes have *forged* the very texts they cite, and the prophetic voice (Jeremiah) is accusing them of textual corruption. The two readings have radically different implications. The misinterpretation reading is compatible with traditional doctrines of scriptural authority — the text is fine, the interpreters are corrupt. The forgery reading destabilizes the very textual base on which traditional scriptural authority rests — and yet, paradoxically, it *also* sits inside Scripture (Jeremiah 8:8 *is* Scripture, accusing other Scripture or the textual transmission of Scripture). The Catholic Magisterial reading takes the verse seriously but constrains it: scribes *can* misinterpret, but the *integrity* of Scripture is preserved by the Magisterium and the Spirit; v.8 critiques the human reception of the text, not the text itself. The Reformed tradition reads v.8 as a charter for *sola scriptura* against ecclesial glossing — but the verse cuts harder than Calvin acknowledged, since it suggests that even the scribes who *produce* Scripture can corrupt it. The Lutheran reading sits adjacent to the Reformed but presses the law-gospel distinction: the law-as-Torah remains good; the scribes' *use* of it has become law-as-condemnation. The Jewish tradition (Rashi, Radak, Abrabanel) reads v.8 within an authoritative-tradition frame: certain scribes failed, but the institution of scribal authority remains valid. The radical reading (van der Toorn, some critical scholars) is harder than any of these traditions can comfortably absorb. The steelman of the radical reading: v.8 explicitly says the scribes have *made the Torah into a lie* (*'asahu lasheqer*) — not "interpreted it falsely," but "made it into a lie." If one takes the Hebrew at face value, the verse describes textual production, not just hermeneutical malpractice. **Where this leaves us:** any high doctrine of Scripture must reckon with the canonical fact that Scripture itself contains the accusation that Scripture has been falsified by its scribes. The verse does not abolish scriptural authority, but it forbids any naive identification of the text in your hand with the unmediated voice of God. The transmission was carried out by humans, including humans who lied.

**Tension 2: The balm in Gilead — material, theological, or Christological?** Verse 22's *is there no balm in Gilead? Is there no physician there?* is one of the most reception-rich verses in the Old Testament. The literal-pharmaceutical reading takes the verse as *ironic*: there *is* balm in Gilead — the medicinal resin trade was thriving — but the political-spiritual disease is one no resin can heal. The chapter's medical-metaphor structure (*the wound of my people*, vv.21, 11; *the health of the daughter of my people not restored*, v.22) presses this reading: the prophet is using the well-known efficacy of Gileadite balm as the contrast against which to measure the depth of the present crisis. The patristic-medieval Christological reading (Origen, Jerome, Aquinas) takes the verse typologically: the absent physician is the not-yet-incarnate Christ, the balm is the gospel; v.22's question is rhetorical only because the answer is still future at Jeremiah's moment. The African American spiritual tradition takes the verse as a question to which the Christian community can give an experiential answer: *yes, there is*, and the balm is what sustains the church through the long affliction. The Jewish tradition reads the verse as the question still open in unredeemed history — the messianic balm awaiting messianic time. The critical reading tends toward the pharmacological-ironic and resists the Christological. The pastoral-theological question: which is the more theologically serious reading — the one that treats the verse as a question still open (Jewish, critical) or the one that treats it as a question already answered in Christ (patristic-medieval, Reformed, the spiritual)? **Where this leaves us:** if the verse's question has been answered, the answer must be lived in such a way that suffering communities can say, with the African American spiritual, *there is a balm in Gilead*. If it has not been answered, then the chapter's grief stands as the perpetually open wound of a creation not yet healed. Both traditions agree the wound is real. They differ on whether the physician has arrived.

The unresolved question that lands hardest: at v.20 — *the harvest is past, the summer is ended, and we are not saved* — Jeremiah voices a kind of theological lateness that most religious literature avoids. There are seasons in the agricultural year, in personal life, in the life of nations and churches, when the time of possibility has passed. The verse refuses the standard religious move that there is always more time, always another harvest, always another opportunity for *teshuvah*. Some moments are over. The grain that was not gathered is not gathered. What does it mean to confess God's sovereignty over history when some of the lateness is real and final? The chapter does not soften this. Neither does Lamentations.

## The Thread

Several cross-references run forward and backward from chapter 8. First, Jer 6:14 (*they have healed the wound of my people lightly, saying "peace, peace," when there is no peace*) is repeated almost verbatim at 8:11, binding chapters 5–8 into a single false-peace indictment that culminates in the temple-sermon prose (7:1–8:3) and resolves into the prophet's lament (8:18–9:3). The repetition is not accidental: the *shalom shalom* has now been *sung* — institutionalized by the scribes whose lying pen has made Torah into a lie. Second, Deuteronomy 28's curses (especially 28:26 — "your dead bodies shall be food for all birds of the air and for the beasts of the earth, and there shall be no one to frighten them away") sit directly behind both Jer 7:33 and Jer 8:1–3; the bone-desecration of vv.1–2 is Deut 28:26 operationalized as imminent threat. Third, Jer 31:31–34's new covenant promise — *I will put my law within them, and I will write it on their hearts* — is the structural answer to 8:8's lying pen; the new covenant is necessary precisely because the Torah-as-text has been falsified by the scribes whose monopoly the new covenant abolishes. Fourth, forward to Lamentations 4:3–4 — *"even jackals offer the breast and nurse their young, but the daughter of my people has become cruel… the tongue of the nursing infant sticks to the roof of its mouth for thirst"* — picks up exactly the *bath-'ammi* lament-vocabulary of Jer 8:18–22 and confirms it as historical reality. And fifth, the Christological trajectory: Jesus weeps over Jerusalem at Luke 19:41–44 (*"if you, even you, had only known on this day the things that make for peace! But now they are hidden from your eyes…"*) — the Lukan scene is structurally Jer 8:18–22 redivivus, with Jesus standing in the line of the prophet who wept for the city he announced doomed. Read 8:20's *the harvest is past, the summer is ended, and we are not saved* through the Lukan tears, and the chapter stops being merely Judean. It becomes the question every prophetic tradition keeps asking on every threshold of catastrophe: is this lateness the final lateness, or is there yet time?
