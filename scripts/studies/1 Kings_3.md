# 1 Kings 3: The Listening Heart at Gibeon

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

The chapter opens with three quick notations that the dtr will later expand into central themes: a foreign-royal marriage (the Pharaoh's daughter), a building-program timeline, and a critical comment about high-place worship. Each is data-dense for ANE history.

The marriage to "Pharaoh's daughter" (v. 1) is the chapter's first historical puzzle. Egyptian royal practice in the 21st Dynasty (c. 1069-945 BCE), the period of Solomon's reign on conventional chronology, did not normally export princesses to foreign kings. The Amarna correspondence two centuries earlier records Egyptian kings refusing such matches even to major Mesopotamian monarchs (EA 4, the famous Kadashman-Enlil exchange — "from time immemorial no daughter of the king of Egypt has been given to anyone"). Kenneth Kitchen (The Third Intermediate Period in Egypt) and Donald Redford (Egypt, Canaan, and Israel in Ancient Times) note that the late 21st Dynasty was politically weak and might plausibly have departed from earlier practice. The candidate pharaoh is most often identified as Siamun (c. 978-959 BCE), whose Tanis stela depicting him smiting an enemy holding a double-axe (sometimes interpreted as a Philistine or Aegean weapon) has been speculatively connected to the Solomonic-era reference at 1 Kgs 9:16 (Pharaoh capturing Gezer and giving it as dowry). Mordechai Cogan (Anchor Bible) and Marvin Sweeney (OTL) note that the historicity is plausible if disputed; the narrative function — foreshadowing Solomon's foreign wives and his eventual apostasy at 1 Kgs 11 — is unambiguous.

The building program (v. 1) describes Solomon's three projects: his palace (bayit), the Temple (bet YHWH), and the city wall (chomat yerushalayim). Yigael Yadin's identification of "Solomonic" six-chambered casemate gates at Megiddo (Stratum IVB-VA), Hazor (Stratum X), and Gezer (with R.A.S. Macalister's earlier excavations re-examined by William Dever) anchored a maximalist Solomonic-era architectural reconstruction. Israel Finkelstein's "Low Chronology" redates these structures to the Omride era (9th c. BCE), which would push the impressive monumental architecture of these sites out of Solomon's reign. The Stepped Stone Structure and Eilat Mazar's "Large Stone Structure" in the City of David have been controversially proposed (Mazar 2006) as part of David's or Solomon's palatial complex; Finkelstein, Lily Singer-Avitz, and others contest both the dating and identification. The 2007-2013 excavations at Khirbet Qeiyafa by Yosef Garfinkel claim a fortified 10th-century Judahite border city contemporary with Solomon, providing maximalist support; Finkelstein contests it. The state of the question is genuinely open.

Gibeon (v. 4) is a key archaeological site. The "great high place" (ha-bamah ha-gedolah) was excavated by James B. Pritchard from 1956-1962, identifying it with el-Jib, c. 9 km northwest of Jerusalem. Pritchard found a substantial Iron Age city, a remarkable rock-cut water system (the "pool of Gibeon" of 2 Sam 2:13, with a stepped tunnel), and dozens of jar handles inscribed g-b-‘-n in paleo-Hebrew. The cultic significance of Gibeon as a major sanctuary before the Jerusalem Temple is independently attested in the Saul narrative (1 Sam 22 — Nob may be Gibeon-region) and the Joshua-Gibeonite covenant (Joshua 9-10). Pre-monarchic and early-monarchic Gibeon was a major regional cult center; 1 Chronicles 16:39 places the Mosaic tabernacle there (a probable Chronicler harmonization). Solomon's choice to sacrifice at Gibeon is historically plausible as a major-shrine pilgrimage; the dtr's discomfort with high-place worship (v. 2-3) reflects later Josianic-era polemic against bamot (high places), grounded in Deuteronomy 12's centralization theology.

Hebrew terms worth tracking: lev shomea (לֵב שֹׁמֵעַ, v. 9), "a hearing/listening heart" — Solomon's request, more literally a "heart that hears," with shomea drawing on the prophetic-Mosaic auditory tradition (cf. Shema, Deut 6:4); chen (חֵן) — implicit in the "favored one" framing of v. 10; chokhmah (חָכְמָה) — actually not in the chapter explicitly, but assumed; the related havin (הָבִין, v. 9, "to discern") is the operative word; bamah (בָּמָה, "high place," v. 2-4), the technical term for the elevated outdoor cultic site, attested in Mesha Stele (line 3) and in archaeology at Tel Dan, Megiddo, and elsewhere; zonot (זוֹנוֹת, v. 16), "prostitutes" — the social location of the case-law petitioners, the lowest rung of formal female personhood in ancient Israel.

## Lens 2 — The Literature

Genre: the chapter combines three units of distinct genre. Verses 1-3 are dtr editorial introduction, with characteristic "did right/wrong only X" formula in compressed form. Verses 4-15 are a dream-theophany report, a recognized ANE genre with parallels in the Mari archives (the prophetic dreams of Šibtu and others), the Egyptian Sphinx Stela of Thutmose IV, and the Akkadian Cuthean Legend. Robert Gnuse (The Dream Theophany of Samuel) and A. Leo Oppenheim (The Interpretation of Dreams in the Ancient Near East) have mapped the conventions: a king sleeps at a sanctuary (incubation, Greek enkoimēsis), receives a divine offer or warning, makes a request, and rises with a divine endowment. The Solomon dream is structurally classic — Gibeon as the sanctuary, the "ask what I shall give you" formula matching Mesopotamian dream-prayer patterns. Verses 16-28 are a wisdom-court narrative, of the genre Walter Brueggemann and Carole Fontaine have called "wisdom case-law" — a story exhibiting the king's discernment through a hard case. There are parallels in Egyptian (the Eloquent Peasant), Mesopotamian (sham-trial topoi), and even later cross-cultural Indian and Greek folklore (the Buddhist Jataka tale of the disputed child shows striking parallels, though the direction of dependence — if any — is disputed; David Daube and Hugo Gressmann have explored these comparisons).

Devices: chiasm and irony. Solomon's prayer (vv. 6-9) chiastically frames the chapter — looking back to David and forward to the people. The dream-narrative shows the Hebrew narrative technique of free indirect discourse: God's speech includes God's evaluation of Solomon's request (v. 10, "it pleased the Lord"). The two-prostitutes case (vv. 16-28) is structurally a perfect exposure-by-contrast: the false claimant accepts division, the true mother surrenders her claim. Note the gap: the text never tells us which of the two women is which; both speak, both are unnamed, the narrator never identifies the narratological "first" with the true mother explicitly until the verdict. Robert Alter (The Art of Biblical Narrative) and Meir Sternberg (The Poetics of Biblical Narrative) have made this case a model of biblical narrative ambiguity rendered into wisdom outcome.

Place in the book argument: 1 Kings 3 inaugurates the Solomon Source proper. The previous chapter ended with Solomon's brutal political consolidation; this chapter inaugurates his reign with the dream-theophany at Gibeon and the demonstration of judicial wisdom. The juxtaposition is theologically fraught — the dtr is letting the wisdom-tradition source speak after letting the Court History source speak, with no editorial reconciliation. Brueggemann reads this as deliberate dtr theological dissonance; the king who has just executed Adonijah, Joab, and (eventually) Shimei is the same king who will pray for a "listening heart." The Solomon Source frames Solomon as the wisdom king (proverbs, songs, wide-as-Egypt, ch 4-5), but this framing sits uneasily next to the Court History's just-completed political ledger.

Burke Long (FOTL 1 Kings) maps the genre boundaries carefully, distinguishing the dream-theophany unit from the wisdom-narrative unit. Jerome Walsh (Berit Olam) reads the chapter as the Solomon Source's overture, providing the theological vocabulary that the rest of the source will exploit. Simon DeVries (WBC) tracks the form-critical layers and their composition history. Jon Levenson, in Sinai and Zion, has read the Gibeon dream as a Solomonic counter-Sinai event — a personal theophany endowing the king with discerning power, a sort of Mosaic transmission to the king.

The two-prostitutes story (vv. 16-28) is, as Brueggemann notes, a deliberately scandalous choice for the demonstration of royal wisdom. Two zonot — outside any patriarchal household, with no male advocates, with no legal standing in any normal sense — bring their case to the king's court. Solomon hears them. The story is teaching that royal wisdom serves the most marginal of subjects; the king's competence is measured not on grand state cases but on a domestic dispute between two women whose social position is legally negligible. Phyllis Trible, Tikva Frymer-Kensky, and Cheryl Exum have written extensively on this scene as a counter-patriarchal moment within a patriarchal framework. Frymer-Kensky in particular notes that the surviving mother's willingness to surrender her child rather than see him divided is the moment at which biblical motherhood-language becomes theologically operative — the love that yields rather than divides.

Textual variants: 1 Kings 3 has notable MT-LXX divergence. The OG places verse 1 (the Pharaoh's daughter notice) much later in its text — the OG of Reigns has a different sequencing of the early Solomon material, with the marriage notice located near the building completion notice (ch 7-9 region). Adrian Schenker, James Trebolle Barrera, and Frank Cross have argued that this OG sequencing reflects an alternative Hebrew Vorlage. Whether the MT or the OG order is more original is debated; the MT placement at 3:1 is theologically pointed (foreshadowing 11:1-8) and may be a later editorial relocation. The Gibeon-dream sequence (vv. 4-15) is stable across traditions. The two-prostitutes case is also stable, though the OG occasionally varies in the dialogue formulas.

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

Canonical arc: 1 Kings 3 looks back to Genesis 28 (Jacob's dream at Bethel — pre-Temple-era theophany at a sanctuary), Numbers 12:6-8 (the prophetic dream-revelation framework), and Exodus 18 (Moses learning to delegate judicial authority — the wisdom-judgment frame). It looks forward to Solomon's wisdom productions (1 Kgs 4:29-34), the Queen of Sheba's visit (1 Kgs 10), the New Testament's "something greater than Solomon" sayings (Matt 12:42, Luke 11:31), and the deuterocanonical Wisdom of Solomon — which makes Solomon's prayer for wisdom the framework for sustained theological reflection on God's wisdom personified.

Jewish interpretive trajectory: Josephus (Antiquities 8.21-49) treats the Gibeon dream as straightforward divine endowment and the prostitutes case as a pure demonstration of wisdom; he tends to elevate Solomon. Rashi reads the lev shomea request through a tradition that distinguishes between intellectual wisdom (chokhmah) and discerning wisdom (binah) — Solomon asks for the latter, the kind required for judgment. Radak (Kimhi) and Ralbag are similar but more attentive to the political context. The Talmud (b. Berakhot 55a-57b) discusses dream-revelation in extensive halakhic and aggadic terms; the Solomon dream is a major reference point. Genesis Rabbah and Pesikta de-Rav Kahana both treat the chapter, with the prostitutes case becoming a midrashic seed for sustained reflection on hidden-mother and revealed-mother as theological types (the rabbis read the surviving mother as Israel; the false mother as the nations or, in some versions, as Pharaoh's daughter herself, whom Solomon has just married).

Don Isaac Abarbanel reads the chapter as a study in royal education. Solomon, having just secured his throne by violence, is given the opportunity to ask for any gift; he asks for wisdom because he knows — Abarbanel argues — that he has just taken the throne by means that no Torah-licensed king should have used, and only by becoming the kind of king who hears wisely can he redeem his accession. Abarbanel's reading is striking because it directly connects ch 2's executions to ch 3's prayer: the prayer is theologically required precisely because the executions happened. Avivah Zornberg has written on the Gibeon dream as a moment of receptive consciousness — the listening heart as the Hebrew counterpart to the Greek noetic intellect. Yair Zakovitch and Moshe Garsiel parse the chapter's wordplay (the multiple meanings of shomea, the double senses of binah).

Christian interpretive trajectory: Origen reads the Gibeon dream Christologically — the wisdom Solomon receives is Christ-as-Wisdom (1 Cor 1:24), pre-figured in the Solomonic gift. Augustine, in De Doctrina Christiana and elsewhere, treats Solomon's wisdom as a participation in divine wisdom that nevertheless fails (citing 1 Kgs 11), making the typology bittersweet. Theodoret of Cyrus (Quaestiones in Reges) handles the chapter in question-and-answer form: why did God appear at a high place if high places are forbidden? Theodoret answers that the Temple did not yet exist, so God accommodated to the available cultic site. The two-prostitutes case is treated patristically as a moral exemplum on the discernment of true motherhood. Ambrose, Chrysostom, and Gregory the Great all preach on the Solomon-prayer as a model of priestly humility — particularly Gregory, who in Pastoral Rule explicitly names Solomon's prayer as the model for any pastor or bishop's self-understanding.

Calvin's sermons on 1 Kings (Geneva, 1561-62) treat the chapter as a model of right prayer — asking for wisdom rather than long life or wealth. Calvin reads the Gibeon high-place worship pragmatically (the Temple did not yet exist) but does not exonerate it. The two-prostitutes case Calvin treats as evidence of the divine wisdom granted to Solomon. Luther's Christmas sermons sometimes invoke Solomon's prayer as the king's appropriate posture before God.

Critical scholarship: the chapter is recognized as the prologue to the Solomon Source. Mordechai Cogan's Anchor Bible commentary handles the dream-theophany with extensive ANE parallels (especially the Egyptian Sphinx Stela of Thutmose IV — a young king at a sacred place receives a divine commission). Marvin Sweeney (OTL) emphasizes the dtr framing of vv. 1-3 as theologically critical of Solomon's foreign-marriage and high-place practices. Simon DeVries (WBC) tracks the form-critical layers. Jon Levenson and James Kugel both read the Gibeon dream as a programmatic statement of Solomonic theology — the king is the locus of divine wisdom in the kingdom. Brueggemann (1 & 2 Kings) reads with characteristic suspicion: the Solomon Source is presenting its preferred theology, and the dtr's framing is critical of it. Polzin treats the chapter as a moment of moral reset that the rest of Kings will undermine. Robert Alter handles the prostitutes case in his commentary on Kings (in The Hebrew Bible: A Translation with Commentary) as a virtuoso piece of biblical narrative.

Contemporary: Eugene Peterson on the lev shomea as the foundational virtue of pastoral leadership. Ellen Davis (Opening Israel's Scriptures) on the listening heart as a counter-formation to the technocratic mind. Goldingay's accessible reading. Iain Provan's harmonistic but textually attentive treatment. Feminist readings: Frymer-Kensky's reading of the prostitutes case as a moment in which the unnamed and socially marginal women become the bearers of the chapter's deepest moral content. Trible on the language of yearning (rachamim, "compassion," from rechem, "womb") underlying the surviving mother's plea. Brenner and Exum on the structural use of female bodies in royal-narrative legitimation.

## Lens 4 — The Theological Intent

Tension One: Gibeon's high place — accommodation or compromise? Position one (traditional/harmonistic, e.g., the Chronicler at 2 Chr 1, Provan): God meets Solomon at Gibeon because the Temple does not yet exist, and the high place at Gibeon was an acceptable provisional sanctuary (per the Chronicler, the Mosaic tabernacle was there). The dtr's mild discomfort at v. 3 ("only he sacrificed at high places") is editorial overlay, but the theology is permissive. Position two (dtr-source-critical reading, Cross/Nelson, Brueggemann): the chapter is showing how even Solomon's reign begins with theological compromise. The dream-theophany at Gibeon is real, but the Gibeon worship is critiqued. God can meet Solomon there — but the meeting itself signals that Israel's worship is not yet centralized, and the dtr is prefiguring the critique that will sharpen across Kings. Position three (Catholic and Orthodox sacramental tradition): God meets people where they are; the high places are not inherently wrong, only inferior to the Temple, which itself is inferior to the Christological reality.

Steelman of position two: the dtr at v. 2 makes the high-place practice conditional on the absence of the Temple ("because no house had yet been built"). This is exactly the structure of "permitted but not preferred." The prophetic critique of high-place worship across Kings (the recurring "but the high places were not removed" formula) signals that even Judahite kings who do right are graded down for tolerating bamot. Solomon's worship at Gibeon is the historical inflection point at which Israel's most powerful king sets the precedent for the unfinished worship-centralization that the dtr will mourn.

Where this leaves us: the chapter is teaching a layered theology. God meets Solomon — really meets him, with a real theophany and a real endowment of wisdom — at a worship-site that the dtr will later critique. The theological lesson is that divine encounter is not contingent on perfect cultic conditions, but that the conditions still matter for the long-term direction of the worshipping people. God is generous beyond the cult; the cult still shapes formation. Both positions hold simultaneously.

Tension Two: The two-prostitutes case and the nature of judicial wisdom. Position one (traditional Jewish and Christian, Rashi, Calvin, most patristic readings): Solomon's wisdom is pure divine endowment manifesting in unimpeachable judgment. The case is a demonstration of supernatural insight. Position two (literary-critical, Alter, Brueggemann): the case is a demonstration of psychological-rhetorical skill. Solomon's "divide the child" is a stress-test designed to expose the false claimant by maternal compassion. The wisdom is pragmatic and observable, not magical. Position three (feminist-theological, Frymer-Kensky, Trible): the case demonstrates that royal wisdom is most truly itself when it serves the most marginal. The two prostitutes have no other advocate; the king must be theirs. The wisdom demonstrated is solidarity-wisdom, not merely cleverness.

Steelman of position three: the chapter is at pains to specify that the petitioners are zonot, prostitutes, with no household and no male representative. This is not incidental detail. The narrator is showing that the king's wisdom is for the people who have no one else. If Solomon had judged a noble's case, the wisdom-demonstration would be less theologically loaded; that the case involves two women without standing makes the king's competence into an act of judicial mercy. Frymer-Kensky reads the surviving mother's renunciation as the chapter's deepest theology — the love that prefers the beloved's life over the lover's claim is the love that defines true motherhood and, by extension, true sovereignty.

Where this leaves us: the prostitutes case is teaching that wisdom in service of the powerless is the chapter's working definition of sovereign virtue. The two-prostitutes scene becomes the lens through which we should read every later judicial encounter in Kings — and find them, mostly, falling short.

Unresolved question: How does the Solomon of 1 Kings 3 — the listening heart, the mother-discerning judge — become the Solomon of 1 Kings 11, the apostate polygamist? The chapter's gift of "wisdom and discerning mind, none like you before or after" appears to be permanent (vv. 12-14, conditioned only on continued Torah-walking, which Solomon does not do). What is the relationship between divine endowment of wisdom and human capacity to use that wisdom faithfully? The text does not answer; later reflection (Wisdom of Solomon, the rabbinic tradition, Christian wisdom-Christology) takes up the question in different ways.

## The Thread

Cross-references: Genesis 28 (Jacob at Bethel — dream-theophany at sanctuary), Exodus 18 (Moses learning to judge), Deuteronomy 17:18-20 (the law of the king, including the requirement to study Torah for discerning judgment), Proverbs 1:7 ("the fear of the LORD is the beginning of knowledge"). Flag forward: 1 Kings 4:29-34 (the wisdom proverbs and songs, the wisdom kingdom-administration), 1 Kings 8:22-53 (Solomon's Temple-dedication prayer — the rich theological elaboration of this chapter's theology), 1 Kings 11:1-13 (Solomon's apostasy, conditioned on the chapter's "if you walk in my ways" warning at v. 14); Matthew 12:42 (Christ as one greater than Solomon — the wisdom-king typology pointed forward); James 1:5 (asking for wisdom — the New Testament's most direct echo of the Gibeon prayer); 1 Corinthians 1:18-31 (Christ as the wisdom of God, the Christological elaboration of the Solomonic theology).
