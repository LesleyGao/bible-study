# Psalm 122 — Going Deeper

## Lens 1: The Historical World Behind the Text

Psalm 122 is the third in the **Songs of Ascents** (*Shir HaMa'alot*, שִׁיר הַמַּעֲלוֹת) — the fifteen-psalm pilgrim collection (Pss 120–134) within Book V of the Psalter (Pss 107–150). Its superscription is more elaborate than most of the Songs of Ascents: *shir ha-ma'alot le-David* (שִׁיר הַמַּעֲלוֹת לְדָוִד, "a song of ascents. Of David"). Four of the fifteen Songs of Ascents bear a Davidic attribution (122, 124, 131, 133), and one is attributed to Solomon (127). The Davidic attribution is contested: most modern critical scholars (Gunkel, Mowinckel, Kraus, Hossfeld–Zenger) read it as a post-exilic editorial assignment that gathers Davidic associations around the Temple-pilgrimage tradition rather than a literal claim of Davidic authorship; some traditional and conservative readers (Delitzsch, more recently Wenham, with caveats) defend at least a Davidic milieu for the underlying material. The reference to "thrones of the house of David" (v. 5) presupposes a functioning Davidic dynasty in some form, which fits either a pre-exilic original (Solomonic or later) or, more plausibly, a post-exilic memorial poem that recalls the Davidic-throne tradition as part of the city's sacred geography. The Songs of Ascents as a collection are most likely **post-exilic** in their final shape — the editorial logic of Book V argues for a Persian-period anthology of pilgrim materials.

The pilgrimage referenced here is the *aliyah la-regel* (עֲלִיָּה לָרֶגֶל, "going up by foot"), the Deuteronomic obligation (Deut 16:16) for every adult male Israelite to appear before the LORD three times a year — at Passover (*Pesach*), Pentecost (*Shavuot*), and Tabernacles (*Sukkot*). The opening verse, *samachti be-omrim li beit Yahweh nelech* (שָׂמַחְתִּי בְּאֹמְרִים לִי בֵּית יְהוָה נֵלֵךְ, "I was glad when they said to me, 'let us go to the house of the LORD'", 122:1), is the **arrival psalm** — the pilgrim, having journeyed up from exile (Ps 120) and lifted eyes to the hills (Ps 121), now stands at Jerusalem's gate. Verse 2 makes the moment explicit: *omdot hayu ragleinu bi-she'arayikh Yerushalayim* (עֹמְדוֹת הָיוּ רַגְלֵינוּ בִּשְׁעָרַיִךְ יְרוּשָׁלִָם, "our feet have been standing in your gates, O Jerusalem").

Gunkel's form-critical genre is **Song of Zion** (*Zionslied*) — the same category as Pss 46, 48, 76, 84, and 87 — psalms that celebrate Jerusalem as the city of the Great King, the site of the Temple, the navel of the world. Sigmund Mowinckel's cult-functional reading places these in the actual liturgy of Israel's autumn festivals; Hans-Joachim Kraus (*Theologie der Psalmen*) develops a comprehensive Zion-theology rooted in the Jerusalem royal cult.

The architectural language is precise. *Yerushalayim ha-benuyah ke-ir she-chubrah-lah yachdav* (יְרוּשָׁלִַם הַבְּנוּיָה כְּעִיר שֶׁחֻבְּרָה־לָּהּ יַחְדָּו, "Jerusalem — built as a city that is bound firmly together," v. 3) uses the verb *chavar* (חָבַר, to join, bind, connect) — the same root behind the Hebrew word *chaver* (companion, associate, member of a *chavurah*). The participial passive *chubrah* describes Jerusalem as masonry skillfully fitted, stones knit into a single unified structure. Some commentators see here a reference to the post-exilic rebuilding under Nehemiah (cf. Neh 4:6 — *va-tiqasher kol-ha-chomah*, "and all the wall was joined together"), where the community physically and politically reconstituted itself by reconstituting the wall. The Hebrew *chubrah-lah yachdav* — "joined to itself together" — emphasizes the city's wholeness against fragmentation.

Verse 4 names the **tribal pilgrimage**: *she-sham alu shevatim shivtei-Yah eidut le-Yisrael lehodot le-shem Yahweh* ("to which the tribes go up, the tribes of the LORD, as a testimony for Israel, to give thanks to the name of the LORD"). The phrase *eidut le-Yisrael* (עֵדוּת לְיִשְׂרָאֵל, "a testimony for Israel") recalls the legal language of the Sinai covenant — the *edut* is the testimony deposited in the ark (Exod 25:16). The plural *shivtei Yah* (tribes of YH) uses the short divine name; pilgrimage is itself the visible covenant-keeping of the people. By post-exilic times, of course, "the tribes" no longer functioned as the political reality they had in pre-exilic Israel, but the language preserves the memory.

Verse 5 invokes the **Davidic judicial tradition**: *ki shamah yashvu khis'ot le-mishpat kis'ot le-veit David* ("for there thrones of judgment were set, the thrones of the house of David"). Jerusalem was not only the cultic center but the legal-political center; the Davidic kings sat as judges at the city gate (cf. 2 Sam 8:15; 1 Kgs 3:16–28). The plural *khis'ot* (thrones) likely refers to the multiple judicial seats — the king and his councilors, or the dynastic succession of thrones across generations.

The peace-of-Jerusalem motif occupies vv. 6–9 and turns on a dense **wordplay**. The Hebrew *sha'alu shelom Yerushalayim* (שַׁאֲלוּ שְׁלוֹם יְרוּשָׁלִָם, "ask for / pray for the peace of Jerusalem," v. 6) plays on the consonance and shared *sh-l-m* root in *shelom* (שָׁלוֹם, peace) and the *Yerushalayim* place-name (whose etymology has been popularly though not certainly linked to *shalem*, "wholeness, peace"; the name appears in the 14th-century BCE Amarna letters as *Urusalim*, possibly "foundation of Shalim," the Canaanite god of dusk). The verb *sha'al* (to ask) further alliterates with *shalom*. The line is virtually untranslatable: roughly, "ask-shalom of Jeru-shalem" — three *sh-l-m*-rooted resonances in one phrase. Verses 7–8 continue: *yehi shalom be-cheilekh shalvah be-armenotayikh ... adabberah-na shalom bakh* — three more *shalom* repetitions. The whole final strophe is built on this single word.

The Septuagint numbers Ps 122 as Ps 121 (LXX runs one behind MT) and translates the pilgrimage opening straightforwardly. The **Dead Sea Scrolls** preserve Ps 122 in 11Q5 (the Great Psalms Scroll); the Qumran community placed great theological weight on Jerusalem and the Temple, and the Songs of Ascents tradition was important to their piety even as they remained alienated from the Second Temple's actual priestly leadership.

## Lens 2: The Literature Itself

The psalm divides into **three strophes**: arrival (vv. 1–2), description (vv. 3–5), prayer (vv. 6–9). The first names where the pilgrim is; the second names what makes the city what it is; the third names what is to be prayed for the city.

Robert Lowth's parallelism appears throughout. **Synonymous parallelism** in v. 4: "the tribes go up / the tribes of the LORD"; v. 5: "thrones for judgment / thrones of the house of David"; v. 7: "peace within your walls / security within your towers"; v. 8: "brothers and companions." **Synthetic parallelism** governs the strophe transitions: each statement extends and intensifies the previous. **Antithetic parallelism** is largely absent — this is a psalm of unified celebration, not contrast.

The most distinctive literary feature is the **paronomasia** (sound-play) on the *sh-l-m* root. The chain runs through vv. 6–8: *sha'alu shelom Yerushalayim* (v. 6) → *yishlayu* (may they prosper, "be at ease," sharing root resonance though from a different stem *sh-l-h*) → *shalom be-cheilekh* (peace within your walls, v. 7) → *shalvah be-armenotayikh* (security in your towers, v. 7, with *shalvah* sharing the *sh* but a different root) → *adabberah-na shalom bakh* (let me speak peace within you, v. 8). Five or six *sh-l-m* (or near-homophone) repetitions, plus the city name itself, in three verses. The psalm performs what it prays: the saturation of Jerusalem with peace.

The **inclusio** structure binds the psalm. It opens at the gates ("our feet standing in your gates," v. 2) and closes with the speaker still inside, addressing the city in second person ("for the sake of the house of the LORD our God, I will seek your good," v. 9). The pilgrim arrives, beholds, and prays — and the prayer is the leaving-gift that the pilgrim deposits at the gate as he departs.

The pronoun shifts are deliberate. Verse 1's "they said *to me*" is the pilgrim's pre-departure memory; v. 2's "*our* feet" is the corporate arrival; v. 6 turns to direct address of the city ("pray for the peace of Jerusalem... may they be secure *who love you*"); vv. 8–9 close in first-person resolve ("*I* will say... *I* will seek your good"). The grammar tracks a movement from individual recall, through corporate experience, to individual responsibility — the pilgrim leaves the city carrying the city's peace as a personal vocation.

The strophic shape mirrors the arc of any pilgrimage: anticipation, presence, departure-with-blessing. This is one reason the psalm has functioned across so many traditions as the **arrival liturgy** for any sacred journey.

## Lens 3: The Larger Story (Jewish and Christian Trajectories)

Within Book V's editorial logic, Ps 122 is the third step on the ascent staircase. Ps 120's exile, Ps 121's confidence on the road, and now Ps 122's arrival at the gates — three psalms enacting the structure of the pilgrim journey. The tribal language of v. 4 and the Davidic-throne language of v. 5 frame the city as *covenantal capital*, the visible meeting of God's people with God's reign.

In **Jewish reception**, Ps 122 holds a place of extraordinary importance. *Mishnah Bikkurim* describes the procession of the first-fruits offering up to Jerusalem with Levites singing psalms; the Songs of Ascents are central. The phrase *sha'alu shelom Yerushalayim* (122:6) has become a fixed formula in Jewish liturgy and in personal correspondence — Jews around the world close letters and prayers with this phrase. The *Birkat ha-Mazon* (grace after meals) in its full form includes the prayer for the rebuilding of Jerusalem, drawing on the Songs of Ascents tradition. The psalm is recited as part of the *Tikkun Hatzot* (midnight service of mourning over the destruction of the Temple) and in numerous festival contexts. **Rashi** (11th c.) reads v. 3's "bound firmly together" as the city's eschatological perfection — when the heavenly Jerusalem joins the earthly. **Ibn Ezra** (12th c.) emphasizes the pilgrimage realism. **Radak** (12th–13th c.) develops the Davidic-throne theology. **Midrash Tehillim** on Ps 122 multiplies the wordplay, finding numerous resonances of *shalom* throughout the psalm. Modern Jewish commentators — **Buber**, **Heschel**, **Levenson**, **Sarna** — situate the psalm at the heart of the *aliyah* tradition. **Yochanan Muffs** and others have explored its political-theological dimensions.

In **Christian reception**, Ps 122 is the **definitive arrival psalm** of the church's spirituality. **Augustine**'s *Enarrationes* on Ps 121 (Latin numbering) reads the earthly Jerusalem as a sign of the heavenly *Jerusalem-supernam*, the city of God toward which the Christian *peregrinus* (pilgrim) is en route. The "feet standing in your gates" become the eschatological standing of the saints in the New Jerusalem. **Cassiodorus**, **Aquinas**, **Luther**, and **Calvin** all extend this reading. The psalm is a fixture of every Christian liturgical tradition: in the **Catholic Liturgy of the Hours** it is sung at Vespers; the Eastern Orthodox use it in the Hours; the Anglican tradition has set it innumerable times. **Hubert Parry's** anthem **"I was glad"** (1902), composed for the coronation of King Edward VII, has been sung at every British coronation since; the piece — with its acclamations *Vivat!* — translates the psalm into an English ceremonial idiom that has shaped Anglophone Christian imagination of royal-religious procession. Modern critical scholarship — **Gunkel**, **Mowinckel**, **Westermann**, **Kraus**, **Hossfeld–Zenger**, **Goldingay**, **Wenham** (on the Psalter's shape), **Brueggemann** (on the polyphony of city and covenant), **Miller**, **Brown**, and **Beth Tanner** — converges on the Song-of-Zion / pilgrimage genre while debating the dating and the political implications. **Eugene Peterson**'s *A Long Obedience in the Same Direction* devotes a chapter to Ps 122 under the title "Worship," naming the psalm as the recovery of corporate worship at the heart of long-haul discipleship.

The **New Testament** uses the Jerusalem-pilgrimage tradition centrally. Jesus' statement "I must be in my Father's house" (Luke 2:49, where the Greek *en tois tou patros mou* corresponds to Hebrew *beit avi* / *beit Yahweh*) draws on the very phrase that opens Ps 122. Jesus weeps over Jerusalem (Luke 19:41–44; 13:34–35), naming what the city has not known of *the things that make for peace* — a direct echo of Ps 122's *shalom* prayer. Paul collects the relief offering for the Jerusalem church (Rom 15; 2 Cor 8–9) as a post-Temple version of the tribal *aliyah*. The book of Revelation closes with the descent of the New Jerusalem (Rev 21–22), the eschatological fulfillment of the city Ps 122 names. Hebrews 12:22–24 names the Christian community as having "come to Mount Zion and to the city of the living God, the heavenly Jerusalem."

## Lens 4: The Theological Intent

**Tension 1 — How shall the contemporary reader pray for the peace of Jerusalem?** Ps 122:6 has become the most politically contested verse in the Psalter. The verse is read at high political volume by **Christian Zionism** (e.g., John Hagee, Christians United for Israel) as a divine mandate for unconditional support of the modern State of Israel and its claim to Jerusalem; by **Jewish religious Zionism** (Mercaz HaRav school, Gush Emunim heritage) as a call to settle and secure the Land; by **Palestinian Christian theology** (Naim Ateek, Mitri Raheb, Sabeel Liberation Theology Center) as a prayer for *real* peace that cannot be fulfilled while the Palestinian population — including Palestinian Christians whose communities pre-date 1948 — lives under occupation; by **Mark Braverman** and other Jewish critics of the occupation as a call to apply prophetic justice to current Israeli policy; and by **liberal Christian** traditions as an irenic prayer for the city as a religious-shared symbol. *Steelman for each:* The Christian Zionist reading honors the literal Jerusalem of the text and the unbroken biblical narrative of God's covenant with Israel; it refuses replacement theology. The Palestinian Christian reading honors the *shalom* word — peace as Hebrew *shalom* is wholeness, restored relationship, justice (the *mishpat* of v. 5) for all the city's inhabitants — and refuses to read the verse as supporting the dispossession of any group from the very city the psalm celebrates. The Jewish religious-Zionist reading honors the ancient *aliyah* tradition and the people's longing across exile. The liberal-irenic reading honors the city's status as sacred to three Abrahamic faiths. *Where this leaves us:* Ps 122 prays for *shalom* — and *shalom* in biblical Hebrew is not the absence of conflict but the presence of justice, wholeness, and right relationship. The verse's reading is unfalsifiable as an immediate political program (it does not name modern policy questions), and falsifiable only by what counts as actual peace. Pray for the peace of Jerusalem in a way that includes everyone the city actually contains — that is the test the verse leaves unresolved and that every reader has to make under their own ethical conscience.

**Tension 2 — Is the psalm's joy at the pilgrimage available to readers without a Temple?** The psalm presupposes a literal house of the LORD where tribes go up. After 70 CE, neither Jews nor Christians have access to that building. **Three Christian positions** stand in the literature. **(1) The replacement reading** (much patristic and medieval tradition): the church is the new Israel, and Christ is the new Temple (John 2:19–21); Ps 122 is fulfilled when believers gather wherever they are. **(2) The eschatological reading** (Augustine more carefully, modern Reformed): the literal Jerusalem points forward to the heavenly Jerusalem of Rev 21; pilgrimage is to a future city, and present worship is its anticipation. **(3) The continuing-narrative reading** (Wenham, contemporary Reformed-evangelical pilgrimage scholarship, Catholic and Orthodox): the literal-historical Jerusalem remains theologically meaningful even after 70 CE, and Christian pilgrimage to Jerusalem continues as a real practice (millions still make this trip annually). On the **Jewish side**, the psalm has been kept alive across nearly two millennia of diaspora: the tradition of facing Jerusalem during prayer, of saying "next year in Jerusalem" at Passover, of mourning at the Western Wall — these practices kept the verse functional even when no pilgrimage was possible. *Steelman for each:* Replacement honors the NT's strong claims about Christ and the Temple. Eschatological honors the unfulfilled hope. Continuing-narrative honors the ongoing reality of Jerusalem as a place. *Where this leaves us:* The psalm refuses to be reduced to either pure literalism or pure spiritualization. It names a real city in a real history, and it names a longing that survives every destruction of buildings. Ps 122 has been sung continuously in synagogue and church for two and a half millennia, much of that time without any Temple to walk to — and yet the gladness of v. 1 has remained.

**Where this leaves us:** Psalm 122 is the arrival psalm of the pilgrim collection. The pilgrim has crossed Meshech and Kedar, lifted eyes to the hills, and now stands within the gates. The psalm names what is real about the city — its bound-together masonry, its tribal-pilgrimage tradition, its Davidic judicial tradition — and prays the city's wholeness. The wordplay on *shalom* and *Yerushalayim* is itself a small theological argument: the city's name and its destiny share a root, and prayer is what keeps that connection live. The psalm refuses to choose between the joy of arrival and the work of intercession; *I was glad* (v. 1) and *I will seek your good* (v. 9) are the same person speaking, four strophes apart.

The psalm leaves us with a question that every Jerusalem-related question keeps reopening: *what would it look like to pray for the peace of an actual city, in such a way that everyone in the city is included in the prayer?*

What place — physical or communal — would you pray *shalom* over this week, and whose presence in that place would your prayer have to include?
