# Ezekiel 36: The New Heart, the Sprinkled Water, and the Name's Vindication

## Lens 1 — Historical World

Chapter 36 is the theological climax of the *restoration block*'s opening movement (chs. 33–37) and one of the most theologically loaded chapters in the OT. It contains the *new heart* and *new spirit* promise (vv. 25–27), the *for my name's sake* logic (vv. 22–23), the announcement of the land's re-tilling and re-populating (vv. 8–15, 33–38), and the most concentrated set of recognition-formula occurrences in any single chapter of the prophet. The chapter has no date formula, but its placement after chapter 35 and the news of Jerusalem's fall in 33:21 sets it in the post-587 period — late 587 or 586 BC. The standard scholarly architecture — Greenberg (Anchor 22B, 1997), Block (NICOT, 1998), Zimmerli (Hermeneia, 1983), Daniel Block's later monograph work, and Margaret Odell (Smyth & Helwys, 2005) — treats ch. 36 as the chapter in which the prophet's restoration theology becomes theologically definitive: the conditions of return are articulated in their fullest covenantal-anthropological depth.

The chapter addresses the *mountains of Israel* (36:1, 4, 8) directly — the same geographical figure that ch. 6 had earlier addressed for judgment, now reversed for promise. The *waste places* and *desolate cities* (36:4, 10, 33–35) are the depopulated Judahite settlements destroyed in the Babylonian campaign; the archaeological record at Lachish (Tell ed-Duweir, excavations of Tufnell, Aharoni, Ussishkin), Tell Beit Mirsim, Tell Halif, and the Negev forts confirms a pattern of widespread sixth-century destruction. The promise *the desolate land shall be tilled* (v. 34) addresses this archaeological reality: cities once-burnt, fields once-untended, will be restored. The historical horizon of the promise is the Persian-period Judah of the late sixth and fifth centuries BC; whether the prophet anticipated *that* specific historical realization or articulated a more open-ended hope is a contested question in the scholarship, with Greenberg and Block reading vv. 8–15 and 33–38 as referring to the immediate post-exilic return and Walther Zimmerli reading them more eschatologically.

The Hebrew vocabulary of the new-heart promise is the chapter's theological signature. *Lev hadash* (לֵב חָדָשׁ, "new heart") at 36:26 — the operative noun phrase. *Ruach hadasha* (רוּחַ חֲדָשָׁה, "new spirit") at 36:26. *Lev basar* (לֵב בָּשָׂר, "heart of flesh") at 36:26, contrasted with *lev haʾeven* (לֵב הָאֶבֶן, "heart of stone") in the same verse. *Ruchî* (רוּחִי, "my Spirit") at 36:27 — YHWH's own Spirit, distinct from and ontologically higher than the *new spirit* of v. 26, although the two are juxtaposed without explicit theological clarification. The verb *zāraq* ("sprinkle") at 36:25 — the priestly cultic verb used in Lev 14 (the cleansing of the leper), Num 19 (the red heifer ritual), and Exod 24:6–8 (the covenant blood). The verb *ṭāhēr* ("cleanse") at 36:25, 33 — the priestly purity vocabulary. *Lĕmaʿan šĕmî* (לְמַעַן שְׁמִי, "for my name's sake") at 36:22 — the chapter's controlling theological logic.

The *for my name's sake* formula is theologically arresting. *It is not for your sake, O house of Israel, that I am about to act, but for the sake of my holy name, which you have profaned among the nations* (36:22). *And the nations will know that I am the LORD, declares the Lord GOD, when through you I vindicate my holiness before their eyes* (36:23). The logic is unflinching: the exile's *theological* cost was not Israel's suffering but YHWH's *reputation* among the nations, and the restoration's *theological* purpose is the vindication of YHWH's name, not the alleviation of Israel's distress. Block notes that this is one of the OT's most direct articulations of what the systematic-theological tradition will call *theocentric soteriology* — salvation grounded in God's own glory rather than in human merit or human need.

The Al-Yahudu evidence (Pearce and Wunsch, CUSAS 28, 2014) places the chapter's audience in the established Judahite communities of southern Babylonia — the deportees of 597 and 587 BC who had settled into stable agricultural and commercial life along the Chebar canal. These were not destitute refugees but settled Judahite households participating in Neo-Babylonian economic life. The *new heart* promise of 36:26 addressed a settled, increasingly comfortable diaspora whose theological challenge was not despair but accommodation — the temptation to assimilate and abandon the covenantal hope.

The *sprinkled water* of 36:25 has its proximate antecedent in the Levitical purity rituals. The *mei niddah* of Numbers 19 (red-heifer ashes mixed with running water and sprinkled on those defiled by corpse-contact) is the closest priestly parallel; the *sprinkled blood* of Exodus 24:8 (the covenant ratification at Sinai) the closest covenantal parallel. Lawrence Hoffman's *Beyond the Text* (1987) develops the case that 36:25 stands at the intersection of purity-cleansing and covenant-ratification, and that its later reception in Christian baptism preserves the structural conjunction. The Jewish *mikveh* emerges in the Second Temple period; the Christian *baptisma* of John the Baptist and the early church inherits both the Ezekielian vocabulary and the *mikveh* practice.

## Lens 2 — Literature

The chapter is structured in three movements. Verses 1–15 are the oracle to the mountains of Israel, addressed to the desolated land itself in second-person personification. Verses 16–32 are the central oracle on the new-heart-and-new-spirit promise, with the *for my name's sake* logic at 22–23, the cleansing-and-renewal at 25–27, the recovenanting at 28, and the shame-as-fruit-of-restoration at 31–32. Verses 33–38 are the closing oracle on the rebuilt cities and the multiplying flock. The form is *Heilsorakel* (salvation oracle) layered with *Mahnrede* (admonition speech). The transitions between movements are marked by the messenger formula and the *word of the LORD* opening (vv. 1, 16). Block notes that the chapter's structural sophistication — three movements with theological climax in the central panel — is one of the prophet's most carefully composed compositions.

The *leitwort* of the chapter is *šēm* — "name." Five occurrences of *šĕmî* ("my name") in the central panel (36:20, 21, 22, 23 [twice]) anchor the chapter's controlling theological logic. The *for my name's sake* formula reorders the entire restoration narrative: the salvation-historical purpose is YHWH's self-vindication, not Israel's restoration *per se*. The repetition is the argument; every move in the central panel either grounds in or extends from the *šēm*-logic.

Form-critically, the chapter combines several distinct salvation-oracle subforms. The *land-restoration oracle* of vv. 1–15 deploys the second-person address to geography (cf. Ezek 6's reverse pattern). The *covenant-renewal oracle* of vv. 24–28 deploys the covenant formula *you shall be my people, and I will be your God* (v. 28; cf. Lev 26:12, Jer 31:33, Ezek 11:20, 14:11, 37:23, 27). The *eschatological-fertility oracle* of vv. 29–30, 33–35 deploys the Edenic *garden of Eden* comparison (v. 35, *like the garden of Eden*) — the most explicit Eden-comparison in Ezekiel and a structural echo of the Eden imagery in 28:13 (the king of Tyre as *in Eden, the garden of God*) and the larger Edenic-restoration pattern of Isaiah 51:3.

The most distinctive literary feature of the chapter is the *I-will* sequence in vv. 24–28: *I will take you… I will gather you… I will bring you… I will sprinkle… I will cleanse… I will give you a new heart… I will put my Spirit… I will deliver you*. Block counts ten *I-will* statements in five verses; the divine first-person agency is sustained without interruption. The literary effect parallels the *I-myself* sequence of 34:11–16 — the prophet's voice yields to divine first-person action, and the structural agent of the restoration is YHWH alone. This is not incidental; the *monergistic* literary structure is itself an argument for the *monergistic* theology that the Reformed tradition will later articulate.

**The Papyrus 967 textual problem** is the chapter's most consequential textual question. P967 (early third century AD), the oldest substantially complete Greek manuscript of Ezekiel, is *missing* 36:23c–38 — the section containing the new-heart-and-new-spirit promise itself. The textual evidence has been studied by Floyd Filson (1943), Johan Lust (*Ezekiel and His Book*, BETL 74, 1986), Leslie McGregor (*The Greek Text of Ezekiel*, 1985), and Ashley Crane (*Israel's Restoration*, VTSup 122, 2008). *Lust, McGregor, Crane* argue that 36:23c–38 is a *later Hebrew expansion* absent from P967's Vorlage — the new-heart promise entered the textual tradition relatively late as a redactional clarification. *Block, Greenberg, Allen* argue the absence reflects scribal homoeoteleuton or deliberate harmonization, and the section is original. The debate is not settled; the theological stakes — whether the new-heart promise is original Ezekielian theology or a later interpretive layer — keep the question loaded.

The chapter's cleansing-vocabulary at 36:25 — *I will sprinkle clean water on you, and you shall be clean from all your uncleannesses* — is one of the OT's most influential single verses in Christian sacramental theology. The verb *zāraq* and the noun *mayim ṭĕhôrîm* (clean waters) bridge the Levitical purity tradition (Num 19) with the eschatological cleansing announced at the chapter's center. Hoffman (*Beyond the Text*, 1987) argues that the verse is the *halfway house* between the priestly purity tradition and the later Jewish *mikveh* and Christian baptism — both later practices presuppose the Ezekielian articulation of cleansing-with-water as a covenantal-purificatory event.

## Lens 3 — Larger Story

**Jewish reception** treats 36:26–27 as one of the OT's most significant covenant-renewal texts, the Ezekielian parallel to Jeremiah 31's new covenant. The rabbinic tradition reads the *new heart* and *new spirit* eschatologically: the messianic age will see the renewal of Israel's heart and the giving of the Spirit, with the law's *internalization* completed (cf. Jer 31:33). The Bavli (b. *Shabbat* 105a, b. *Sukkah* 52a) treats the *evil inclination* (yetzer ha-ra) as the *heart of stone* of Ezek 36:26, with the messianic age's promise being its replacement by the *heart of flesh*. Maimonides (*Mishneh Torah*, *Hilkhot Teshuvah* 9, *Hilkhot Melakhim* 12) integrates Ezek 36 into his eschatological theology: the messianic age will see the cleansing of Israel's iniquities and the giving of the Spirit. The medieval kabbalistic tradition (the Zohar on parashat Va'era) treats the *new spirit* as the descent of the *Shekhinah* into the renewed Israel.

The Qumran *Hodayot* (1QH^a) and *Community Rule* (1QS) deploy Ezek 36 vocabulary directly. 1QH^a 8 speaks of the Spirit's giving and the heart's cleansing in language that echoes 36:25–27; 1QS 4 speaks of eschatological purification and the spirit of holiness. James VanderKam's *The Dead Sea Scrolls Today* (rev. 2010) documents the reception's depth.

**Christian reception** is concentrated in the Pauline corpus and the patristic baptismal theology. **2 Corinthians 3:3–6** — *you are a letter from Christ, written not with ink but with the Spirit of the living God, not on tablets of stone but on tablets of human hearts* — deploys Ezek 36:26 directly, with the *tablets of stone / tablets of human hearts* opposition explicitly recalling the *heart of stone / heart of flesh* of Ezek 36. Paul's argument that the *new covenant* is *of the Spirit, not of the letter* (2 Cor 3:6) presupposes the Ezekielian articulation. **Romans 8:9–11** — *if anyone does not have the Spirit of Christ, he does not belong to him* — extends the same logic into Christian pneumatology. Richard Hays's *Echoes of Scripture in the Letters of Paul* (1989) and *The Conversion of the Imagination* (2005) document Paul's sustained engagement with Ezek 36 in the context of his pneumatological theology.

The patristic baptismal tradition reads Ezek 36:25 as the OT type of Christian baptism. Tertullian's *De Baptismo* (ca. 200) cites the verse as proof-text. Cyril of Jerusalem's *Mystagogical Catecheses* develops the connection: the catechumens' baptismal water is the Ezekielian sprinkled water, the cleansing from idolatry the Ezekielian cleansing from idols, the gift of the Spirit at confirmation the Ezekielian gift of new spirit. Calvin's *Institutes* 4.15 cites the verse, as does the Westminster Confession 28. Everett Ferguson's *Baptism in the Early Church* (2009) documents the *mikveh*-baptism continuity.

The patristic and medieval tradition develops the *new heart / heart of stone* contrast pastorally. Augustine's *Confessions* 9 deploys the verse for his pre-conversion diagnostic and post-conversion experience. Aquinas (*ST* I-II.106–108) reads 36:26–27 as the OT prefiguration of what the new law accomplishes through the indwelling Spirit. The Reformed tradition makes it central to regeneration: the Westminster Confession 10 cites Ezek 36:26 as proof-text for *monergistic* renewal. The Wesleyan and Catholic traditions read the verbs (*I will sprinkle, I will cleanse, I will give, I will put, I will remove, I will cause*) with greater emphasis on grace-enabled human response.

## Lens 4 — Theological Intent

**Tension 1: The new-heart / new-spirit promise — monergism or synergism?** The verbs in 36:25–27 are uniformly divine first-person and active: *I will sprinkle, I will cleanse, I will give, I will put, I will remove, I will cause*. The Reformed tradition reads this as the OT's most direct articulation of monergistic regeneration: YHWH alone is the agent of the new heart's giving; the human will is the recipient, not the cooperator. The Wesleyan-Arminian and Catholic traditions read the same verbs as articulating prevenient or infused grace that enables but does not coerce the human response.

The five positions:
1. *Reformed monergistic*: the verbs are uniformly divine; the human will is passive-receptive in regeneration; the new heart is given, not cooperatively produced. Calvin *Institutes* 3.3; Westminster Confession 10; Charles Hodge *Systematic Theology*. Steelman: the chapter's literary form is monergistic — ten consecutive *I-will* statements — and any reading that softens divine agency into cooperation flattens the chapter's literary force.
2. *Wesleyan-Arminian prevenient grace*: the divine verbs articulate prevenient grace, the Spirit's prior work that restores the human will to genuine response capacity; the regenerated heart is genuinely the human person's heart, renewed but not coerced; cooperation is real even if grace-enabled. John Wesley *Sermons* on prevenient grace; Thomas Oden *Classic Christianity*. The position preserves human moral agency within a robust account of grace.
3. *Catholic infused grace*: the divine verbs articulate the *infusion* of grace into the soul — sanctifying grace that genuinely transforms but does not displace the human will; the renewed heart cooperates with grace in the works of charity that follow. Aquinas *ST* I-II.109–114; *Catechism* §§1996–2005. The position preserves the Catholic tradition's *grace-and-cooperation* dialectic.
4. *Eastern Orthodox synergic*: the divine verbs articulate the divine *energies* operating in synergy with the human person; *theosis* is genuinely cooperative; the new heart is the human heart deified through participation in the divine energies. Vladimir Lossky *The Mystical Theology of the Eastern Church*; John Zizioulas *Being as Communion*. The position preserves the Orthodox synergic theology of *theosis*.
5. *Pentecostal-charismatic*: the verses describe the Spirit's immediate work; the new birth and Spirit-baptism are the present-tense realization of the promise. Gordon Fee *God's Empowering Presence*.

Steelman the position you find least congenial. If you tend toward Reformed monergism, steelman Wesleyan prevenient grace: the chapter is preceded and followed by chapters that presuppose human moral agency (the *šûb* of ch. 33; the *qaḥ et libbam* of 18:31, *make for yourselves a new heart*); a strict monergistic reading flattens the larger book's repeated address to human responsibility. If you tend toward synergic readings, steelman the Reformed position: the chapter's literary form is unmistakable — ten *I-will* divine first-person statements without any *if you* or *and you* conditional — and any reading that recovers human cooperation must explain why the chapter's syntax is so unflinching.

**Tension 2: The *for my name's sake* logic — divine glory or divine self-vindication?** *It is not for your sake, O house of Israel, that I am about to act, but for the sake of my holy name, which you have profaned among the nations* (36:22). The classical theological tradition (Augustine, Aquinas, Calvin, Edwards) reads this as the OT's most direct articulation of the doctrine that *God's ultimate end in his works is his own glory*. Modern critical theology (open theism, process theology, some liberation theologians) finds the doctrine theologically problematic — a self-vindicating God risks looking like a vain God, a deity whose moral status is undermined by the priority placed on his own reputation.

The five positions:
1. *Classical theistic (Edwardsian)*: God's ultimate end in all his works is his own glory; *for my name's sake* is the OT's clearest articulation of the principle; the doctrine is biblical, ancient, and theologically necessary because any lesser end would subordinate God's perfections to created realities. Jonathan Edwards *The End for Which God Created the World* (1765); John Piper *Desiring God* (1986); Edwards *Religious Affections*.
2. *Augustinian-Catholic*: God's glory and creaturely good are united; *for my name's sake* articulates the priority of glory without opposing creaturely good, since rightly ordered good consists in recognizing divine glory. Augustine *De Civitate Dei* 14.28; Aquinas *ST* I.44.
3. *Reformed covenantal*: *for my name's sake* operates within the covenantal structure in which God has bound his name to Israel's destiny; the name's vindication is inseparable from the people's restoration. Geerhardus Vos *Biblical Theology*.
4. *Open theist / process critique*: the *for my name's sake* logic, taken at face value, presents a theologically problematic image of God — a deity primarily concerned with reputation, who acts to vindicate himself rather than for the genuine good of creatures; modern theology must read the verses with hermeneutical caution. Clark Pinnock *The Openness of God* (1994); John Sanders *The God Who Risks*. Steelman of the position: the *theatrical* dimension of the *šēm*-logic — God acting to be *seen* vindicating himself — is morally uncomfortable, and a theology that affirms it without qualification risks endorsing precisely the kind of pride it would condemn in creatures.
5. *Heschelian / liberation*: God's *šēm* is not private reputation but the public memory of justice; the name's vindication in 36:22–23 is the vindication of *justice* in history. Heschel *The Prophets*; Gutiérrez *Theology of Liberation*. The position reframes the *šēm*-logic as ethically thick rather than narcissistically thin.

Steelman the position you find least congenial. If you tend toward classical theism, steelman the open-theist critique: the *for my name's sake* language, taken at face value without theological qualification, risks producing precisely the deity-as-vain-monarch caricature that secular critics reasonably mock; a theology that simply repeats the formula without critical theological development becomes liturgically untenable. If you tend toward open-theist or critical readings, steelman the Edwardsian classical position: the alternative — a God whose ultimate end is creaturely good rather than divine glory — collapses theological ontology, since any God who exists *for the sake of* something other than himself is not the God of classical theism.

ONE unresolved question: When YHWH announces *I will give you a new heart… and cause you to walk in my statutes* (36:26–27), and the believer experiences the new birth, who is the moral agent of the subsequent obedience? If the believer obeys, was it the believer who obeyed or the divine *cause* that walked? The chapter assumes both — that YHWH causes and that Israel walks — without adjudicating. The theological tradition has not adjudicated either. The unresolved question is whether the divine causation and the human walking are two descriptions of the same event or two events in cooperative sequence, and the answer determines large stretches of subsequent Christian theology.

## The Thread

Three non-obvious cross-references. First, **Jeremiah 31:31–34** — the new covenant promise — is the OT parallel to Ezek 36:26–27 and the canonical pair-text. Jeremiah promises *the law on the heart*; Ezekiel promises *the Spirit within*. The two promises are theologically complementary: the law's internalization (Jer 31) and the Spirit's indwelling (Ezek 36) together constitute the OT's articulation of what the NT will call the new birth. Hebrews 8:8–12 cites Jeremiah 31 directly as fulfilled in Christ; Hebrews assumes the Ezekielian Spirit-promise as background, and the connection is structural to Hebrews' new-covenant theology.

Second, **2 Corinthians 3:3–6** — *you are a letter from Christ, written not with ink but with the Spirit of the living God, not on tablets of stone but on tablets of human hearts* — is Paul's most direct deployment of Ezek 36:26 into Christian theology. The *tablets of stone / tablets of human hearts* opposition is verbatim Ezekiel; the *Spirit of the living God* deploys the *Ruchî* of 36:27. Paul's argument that the *new covenant is of the Spirit, not of the letter* (3:6) presupposes Ezek 36 as the OT articulation of the Spirit's promise. The Pauline reception is the bridge between the Ezekielian promise and the Christian doctrine of regeneration.

Third, **Hebrews 8:8–13** — the citation of Jeremiah 31 in the context of the new covenant's superiority — closes the canonical loop. Hebrews cites Jeremiah explicitly while presupposing Ezekiel implicitly: the *new covenant* of Jer 31 has its Spirit-pneumatological correlate in Ezek 36, and Hebrews' Christology (Christ as the high priest of the new covenant; Christ as the mediator of the better promises) integrates both prophetic strands. The chapter's *for my name's sake* logic also surfaces in Hebrews 13:15 (*the fruit of lips that acknowledge his name*) and in the Hebrews letter's broader emphasis on the *name above every name* (cf. Phil 2:9–11). The Ezekielian *šēm*-theology, the new-heart promise, and the new-covenant Spirit-theology converge in Hebrews into a single theological vision of the post-Pentecost church as the realization of Ezekiel's announced restoration.
