# Ezekiel 37: Dry Bones, Joined Sticks, and the Resurrection of a Nation

## Lens 1 — Historical World

Ezekiel 37 sits inside the great restoration block of the book — chapters 33–48 — that follows the news of Jerusalem's fall in 33:21. The exile community in Babylonia in the 580s BC is no longer hearing oracles of judgment but oracles of return. Chapter 36 has just promised a new heart and a new spirit (36:26–27) and a regathering to the land (36:24); chapter 37 makes that promise visible. Chapters 38–39 will then narrate the apocalyptic Gog oracle, and chapters 40–48 will close the book with the temple-vision dated to April 573 BC (40:1). Block (NICOT, 1998) and Greenberg (Anchor, 1997) both treat 37 as the rhetorical climax of the restoration material proper, before the book turns to apocalyptic and architectural genres.

The historical setting is the morale collapse of the Judean exile community after 587. Lamentations preserves the language; Ezekiel 37:11 quotes it directly: "Our bones are dried up, and our hope is lost; we are indeed cut off." Zimmerli (Hermeneia, 1983) reads this verse as the genuine voice of the *gôlâ* in Tel-abib (Ezek 3:15), the discouragement that the prophet's vision is constructed to answer. Daniel Block notes that the imagery of an unburied battlefield draws on the actual memory of the campaigns of 588–586: corpses left where they fell, no proper burial, the deepest disgrace possible in a culture where burial in the family tomb was a fundamental marker of continuity (cf. Jer 8:1–2). The vision of the valley full of bones is not a generic image of death; it is the specific image of an Israelite army defeated and abandoned in the open.

The valley (*biq‛āh*) of 37:1 is most likely the same valley plain Ezekiel saw in 3:22, often identified by interpreters with the wide alluvial plain near the Chebar canal. Greenberg leaves the geography deliberately vague, treating it as a vision-space rather than a fixed location. The "very high mountain" of 40:2 will sharpen this distinction — visions in Ezekiel often have *spatial* logic rather than *geographic* coordinates.

The sign-act of the two sticks (37:15–28) belongs to a recognizable Ezekielian genre: prophetic actions performed in front of the exile community as embodied oracles (cf. the brick of 4:1–3, the shaving of 5:1–4, the baggage of 12:3–7). Walther Eichrodt and Block both observe that the two-sticks sign-act addresses a question that mattered intensely to the Babylonian exiles: would the restoration include the long-vanished northern tribes? The kingdom of Israel had been deported by Assyria in 722 BC — a century and a half earlier — and was, for all practical purposes, lost. Ezekiel insists, against this historical fact, that YHWH will reunify Joseph and Judah into one nation under one Davidic king (37:22, 24). This is, in Block's phrase, "a programmatically counter-empirical claim."

The reference to "my servant David" (37:24–25) does not require the prophet to expect the historical David to return. As in Jeremiah 30:9 and Hosea 3:5, the formula functions dynastically: the future king will come from the house of David, will embody the covenant ideal, and will end the divided-kingdom era that began in 1 Kings 12. Greenberg argues that this is the closest Ezekiel comes to explicitly *messianic* language, and that later Second Temple Jewish messianism (Psalms of Solomon 17, 4QFlorilegium) draws on this very passage.

## Lens 2 — Literature

Three distinct literary forms are stitched together in chapter 37: a *vision-report* (37:1–14), a *sign-act with interpretation* (37:15–23), and an *oracle of salvation* (37:24–28). Greenberg argues the seams are deliberate — each form makes the same claim in a different idiom, reinforcing the rhetorical pressure on the despairing audience.

The vision-report itself follows the standard form Zimmerli identified across the prophetic corpus: (1) transport ("the hand of the LORD was upon me"), (2) seeing, (3) a divine question ("can these bones live?"), (4) a commanded action ("prophesy"), (5) the action's effect, (6) interpretation. The threefold rhythm of *bones → sinews/flesh → breath* (37:7–10) deliberately echoes Genesis 2:7, where YHWH forms the man from dust and breathes into him the breath of life. Block reads this as the most extended creation-allusion in the prophets: the restoration is a *new creation*, a recapitulation of Genesis 2.

The Hebrew word *rûaḥ* dominates the chapter — Block counts ten occurrences in 37:1–14, more than in any other comparable passage in the Hebrew Bible. *Rûaḥ* carries three semantic layers in this chapter: spirit (the divine *rûaḥ* that transports the prophet, 37:1), wind (the four winds the prophet summons, 37:9), and breath (what enters the slain, 37:10). The translator must choose, but the Hebrew lets the reader feel all three simultaneously. The Septuagint preserves much of this through *pneuma*, which does the same triple work in Greek and is one of the lexical bridges to John 3:8 and Acts 2:2.

The two-sticks sign-act has been subject to text-critical scrutiny. The Hebrew of 37:16 reads "stick" (*‛ēṣ*), which can also mean "tree" or "wood." The Septuagint translates *rhabdos* (rod, staff). Some scholars have suggested wooden tablets used for inscriptions, which would match the inscribed-text feature of the sign. Greenberg treats this debate as inconclusive but stresses that the *image* — two pieces of wood becoming one in the prophet's hand — is meant to be visually striking before any verbal explanation is given.

The oracle of salvation in 37:24–28 culminates in the formula that will recur seven times in chapters 40–48: "they shall be my people, and I will be their God" (37:23, 27). Block reads this *Bundesformel* (covenant formula) as the structural anchor of the entire restoration block. The chapter ends with the clause that will dominate the temple vision: "I will set my sanctuary in their midst forevermore" (37:26, 28), preparing the architectural reading to come.

The *apocalyptic* dimension of chapters 37–39 deserves a literary note. Although Daniel and Revelation are the genre-defining apocalypses, John J. Collins and others have argued that Ezekiel 37–39 contains the Old Testament's most extended apocalyptic-eschatological material outside Daniel — the resurrection vision, the cosmic-symbolic enemy Gog, the universal and eschatological scope. Some commentators treat 37 as proto-apocalyptic and 38–39 as fully apocalyptic; others (Block) resist the genre label but acknowledge the eschatological vector.

## Lens 3 — Larger Story

The reception history of Ezekiel 37 is one of the richest in the Hebrew Bible. The chapter's two natural readings — *national restoration* and *bodily resurrection* — split the Jewish and Christian traditions in instructive ways.

In Jewish reception, the chapter has long been read as primarily about *national* restoration, with bodily resurrection as a secondary or homiletic application. The Mishnah (Sanhedrin 10:1) lists "one who says there is no resurrection of the dead" among those who have no share in the world to come, and the Talmud (b. Sanhedrin 92b) cites Ezekiel 37 as a proof-text for resurrection — yet the same Talmudic passage records the famous dispute: was the vision *literal* (a resurrection that actually happened, of Ephraimites who left Egypt early), *parabolic*, or *future*? Rabbi Judah holds it was literal; Rabbi Nehemiah, parabolic. The Targum to Ezekiel renders the vision in resurrection-eschatological terms. The vision is also the haftarah reading for the Sabbath of Passover in the synagogue lectionary, tying it liturgically to the Exodus and the deliverance of the people. The frescoes from the third-century synagogue at Dura-Europos (in modern Syria) include a remarkable visual cycle of the dry-bones vision, evidence that the chapter was central to the imagination of diaspora Jewry in late antiquity.

Jon Levenson, in *Resurrection and the Restoration of Israel* (Yale, 2006) — the major recent monograph on the topic — argues that the dichotomy between national restoration and bodily resurrection is a modern imposition. In ancient Israelite thought, Levenson argues, the people of Israel are not a metaphor *for* something more real; they are themselves the embodied reality of God's covenant. The restoration of the people *is* the resurrection of the people. Bodily resurrection emerges within Second Temple Judaism (Daniel 12, 2 Maccabees 7) as the necessary corollary of this national hope: if the covenant is faithful to *individuals*, then individuals who died in faithfulness must be raised. Ezekiel 37, on this reading, does not have to choose; it contains both readings in nuce.

In Christian reception, the patristic tradition aggressively claimed 37 as a resurrection prooftext. Tertullian's *De Resurrectione Carnis* 30 spends extensive space on Ezekiel 37, arguing that the vivid physicality of the vision — bones rattling, sinews binding, flesh covering, breath entering — refutes any spiritualizing reading. Cyril of Jerusalem, *Catechetical Lecture* 18, makes Ezekiel 37 a centerpiece of his catechesis on the resurrection of the body. Justin Martyr's *On the Resurrection* (fragment) appeals to the chapter. The chapter shaped the development of the resurrection clause in the Apostles' Creed and the more elaborate Nicene phrase "I look for the resurrection of the dead, and the life of the world to come."

Calvin would have reached this chapter only had he completed his *Lectures on Ezekiel*, which break off at chapter 20. We therefore lack a Reformation-era systematic treatment from him on 37. The Westminster divines and Puritan tradition, however, regularly cited 37 in their treatments of the resurrection (cf. John Owen's *The Glory of Christ*).

The *resurrection-Christological* line runs from Ezekiel 37 through 1 Corinthians 15. Paul's claim that Christ's resurrection is the firstfruits of those who have died (1 Cor 15:20) is constructed against the same horizon Ezekiel sketches: a corporate resurrection in which the prototype individual (David / the Messiah / Christ) raises the people. N. T. Wright's *The Resurrection of the Son of God* (2003) traces this chain in detail, arguing that Second Temple Jewish resurrection-belief is the indispensable context for understanding what Paul thought happened on Easter morning.

The *two-sticks Christological* reading runs through Ephesians 2:14, where Christ "has made us both one and has broken down in his flesh the dividing wall of hostility" — Paul's argument that Jew and Gentile are made one in Christ echoes the structural logic of Ezekiel 37:15–23, even if the application differs. Patristic readers (Jerome, Theodoret) make the connection explicit. The dispensational tradition resists this typological move, arguing that the two sticks must refer to a *future* national reunification of literal Jewish tribes (Walvoord, Pentecost). The amillennial-spiritualizing reading (Calvin's heirs, Hoekema) treats the church as the fulfillment of the *one nation, one shepherd* promise.

Ezekiel 37 is also a key passage in modern Zionist political theology. The chapter has been claimed by both religious Zionists, who read the establishment of the modern state of Israel in 1948 as a partial fulfillment of 37:21, and by anti-Zionist Orthodox groups (Neturei Karta), who argue that the *Davidic king* clause has not been fulfilled and so the modern state lacks the covenantal markers Ezekiel specifies. The chapter has thus become one of the most politically contested texts in the Hebrew Bible.

## Lens 4 — Theological Intent

**Tension 1: dry bones — bodily resurrection or metaphor of national restoration?** The Christian patristic-medieval tradition (Tertullian, Cyril, Jerome, Aquinas) reads Ezekiel 37 as a primary prooftext for the resurrection of the body. The plain sense of 37:11, however, identifies the bones as "the whole house of Israel" — a *national* image of exile and return, not a doctrinal proof of individual resurrection. Modern critical scholarship (Zimmerli, Block) has tended to insist on the national-restoration reading and to treat the resurrection use as later theological appropriation. The most powerful synthesis is Levenson's: the categories are not opposed in ancient Israelite thought. The restoration of the people, in a culture that did not separate individual from corporate existence, includes *as a necessary implication* the resurrection of those who died faithful but did not see the return. The chapter does not have to choose between metaphor and literal resurrection because the dichotomy itself is a post-Enlightenment imposition. Both Tertullian and Block, on this reading, are partially right and partially wrong. The deeper question is whether contemporary readers can recover the worldview in which the two readings are *one* reading.

**Tension 2: "one nation, one shepherd" — Christological fulfillment or unfulfilled Jewish hope?** The promise of 37:24 — "my servant David shall be king over them, and they shall all have one shepherd" — has been read by the Christian tradition since the New Testament as fulfilled in Christ, the good shepherd (John 10:11) who unites Jew and Gentile (Eph 2:14–16). Jewish readers, both ancient and modern, have read it as an unfulfilled future hope: the Messiah, son of David, will yet come, will yet reunify the twelve tribes, will yet establish the everlasting covenant of peace (37:26). The supersessionist Christian reading risks erasing the *literal* national content of the promise; the strict Jewish reading risks treating Christ's first advent as theologically irrelevant. N. T. Wright's mediating proposal — that Christ fulfills *the vocation of Israel* without abolishing the literal national hope — preserves both horizons but at the cost of a typological framework many Jewish readers cannot accept and many dispensationalist Christian readers find too spiritualizing. The tension is irreducible and structurally definitive of Jewish-Christian theological difference.

**One unresolved question:** Does the everlasting "covenant of peace" (37:26) and the "sanctuary in their midst forevermore" (37:26, 28) require a *physical, future temple* on the soil of the land of Israel — as the dispensational tradition reads chapters 40–48 — or is the sanctuary's permanence theologically realized in the indwelling Spirit (37:14) and the body of Christ (John 2:19–21)? Ezekiel 37 raises this question and refuses to answer it, leaving the temple vision of chapters 40–48 to carry the weight.

## The Thread

The *rûaḥ* of 37:9 ("Come from the four winds, O breath") is the same word that hovers over the waters in Genesis 1:2, that animates the dust in Genesis 2:7, that fills Acts 2:2 ("a sound like a mighty rushing wind"), and that Jesus invokes in John 3:8 ("the wind blows where it wishes"). Ezekiel 37 is the textual hinge that connects these four scenes: the resurrection vision is a Pentecost prefigured.

The "exceedingly great army" of 37:10 (*ḥayil gādôl mə‛ōd mə‛ōd*) appears again — with deliberate verbal contrast — in Ezekiel 38:15, where Gog comes against the restored people with "a great army." The army resurrected in 37 is the army that must withstand Gog in 38–39. The literary architecture of chapters 37–39 is thus a single sweep: dry bones → resurrected army → eschatological battle.

The two-sticks sign-act of 37:15–23 inverts the prophetic sign-act of Jeremiah 19:1–13, in which Jeremiah breaks a clay flask to symbolize the irreparable destruction of Judah. Ezekiel takes two broken pieces (Joseph and Judah) and makes them one. The reversal is deliberate: where Jeremiah broke, Ezekiel mends. The reversal also points forward to Zechariah 11:7–14, where the prophet again takes two staffs and breaks them — a sign of *covenantal failure*. The biblical arc thus moves: broken (Jeremiah) → joined (Ezekiel) → broken again (Zechariah) → finally joined in Christ (Ephesians 2).

The "valley" (*biq‛āh*) of 37:1 connects to two other valleys in the prophetic tradition: the valley of decision (Joel 3:14) and the valley of the shadow of death (Psalm 23:4). The Septuagint of 37:1 uses *pedion*, the same word used in the LXX of Joel. The reader who knows the Septuagint hears Ezekiel and Joel as one prophetic chord: the valley where the dead rise and the valley where the nations are judged are, eschatologically, the same valley.
