# Esther 5: The Two Banquets and the Fifty-Cubit Gallows

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

Chapter 5 lands "on the third day" (5:1) of Esther's fast — historically the third day after the Nisan-13 decree, in the Passover-overlapping early days of Nisan, Xerxes' twelfth year (474 BC). The narrative time is concentrated: a single day's events from the throne room appearance in the morning to the gallows construction in the evening. After chapter 4's deliberate slow dialogue, chapter 5 accelerates into action.

The chapter's set pieces are densely Persian. The **inner court** (*ha-chatzer ha-penimit*, הֶחָצֵר הַפְּנִימִית, 5:1) of the palace at Susa, the **throne room** opposite the entrance, and the **golden scepter** held out from the throne are all attested in Achaemenid material culture and in the Greek historians. The Persepolis treasury reliefs (now housed primarily in the Iran Bastan Museum) depict the king seated on his throne with the long scepter (*bittu*) in his right hand; surviving Apadana reliefs from the eastern stairway show petitioners approaching with bowed posture. **Aeschylus's *Persae*** (472 BC, performed only two years after the events the chapter describes) opens with the queen mother Atossa entering the royal presence with elaborate attendant ceremony and being received by the chorus of Persian elders — a near-contemporary Greek dramatization of the protocol the chapter assumes. **Herodotus 3.118, 3.140** documents the death-without-summons rule under Darius and Xerxes; **Herodotus 7.187** records Xerxes' own throne practice during the Greek campaign.

The **scepter-touching gesture** (5:2: "Esther approached and touched the tip of the scepter") is the chapter's most iconographically resonant image. Bush (WBC) and Berlin (JPS) connect it to the Persian gesture of *proskynesis* — the abasement gesture of approach to the king, which Greek writers found intolerable (Plutarch *Themistocles* 27; Arrian *Anabasis* 4.10–12 on Alexander's troubled adoption of it). The Hebrew *naga* "to touch" suggests a formal acknowledgment rather than a kiss; the iconographic parallel is the Persepolis stair relief where a tribute-bearer reaches toward the king's hand. The scepter is itself attested as a royal instrument in the Persepolis Treasury Tablets and in surviving Achaemenid metalwork.

**Haman's gallows** (5:14, *etz*, עֵץ, "tree/gallows") at fifty cubits — roughly 75 feet — is comic hyperbole. Berlin and Bush both note that Persian capital punishment was typically **impalement**, not "hanging" in the modern sense; the Hebrew *talah al ha-etz* is the standard biblical idiom for impalement (Deut 21:22–23; Josh 8:29). Herodotus 3.125 and 6.30 record Persian impalements; the Behistun inscription catalogues Darius's impalements of rebels. The fifty-cubit figure is physically impossible and deliberately so. Levenson: "The gallows is the chapter's set-piece joke. Haman builds an impossible scaffold for the man who will hang from it."

The **half of my kingdom** offer (5:3, 5:6 — *ad chatzi ha-malkhut*, עַד חֲצִי הַמַּלְכוּת) is a recurring comic refrain. The same offer is made to Salome in Mark 6:23 and to Esther a third time in 7:2. The phrase is **stock court hyperbole** — Berlin treats it as the Persian-court equivalent of "anything you want" — and not a serious offer. The Persian king did not divide his kingdom; he had no co-regent. Plutarch *Artaxerxes* 27 records similar grandiose promises that were never meant to be taken literally. The book uses the formula three times; each iteration reinforces the king's emptily-grandiose register.

**Zeresh** (5:10, 14), Haman's wife, bears a Persian name (possibly from *zairī* "golden, yellow" or *zar* "gold"). She appears twice in the book — here as the gallows-counselor, and again in 6:13 as the prophetic-seer who predicts Haman's fall. The doubling is literarily deliberate. Persian queens and wives of high officials are well-attested as political actors: Atossa in Herodotus 7.3 securing Xerxes' succession; Amestris in Herodotus 7.61 and 9.108–113 in the harem politics; Parysatis in Ctesias as an active poisoner. The chapter's portrait of Zeresh as Haman's domestic counselor is institutionally plausible.

**Authorship and dating** continue from prior chapters — anonymous, late Persian to early Hellenistic. The textual situation in chapter 5 is more straightforward than in chapter 4: there are no major Greek Additions inserted within chapter 5 (Addition C precedes 5:1; Addition D, the elaborated throne-room scene, replaces the much shorter MT 5:1–2). Addition D in the LXX expands Esther's approach into a near-fainting scene with explicit divine intervention ("God changed the spirit of the king to gentleness, and he sprang from his throne in alarm"), a striking theological *fix* for the MT's silence. Carey Moore (Anchor) and Bush (WBC) treat Addition D as a Greek composition responding to the MT's terseness. The AT preserves yet another form of the throne scene, somewhat between MT and LXX. Josephus *Antiquities* 11.234–245 follows the LXX with further apologetic elaboration.

**Key terms.** *Ha-chatzer ha-penimit* (הֶחָצֵר הַפְּנִימִית, "the inner court," 5:1) — the architectural threshold beyond which entry is fatal without summons. *Sharbit ha-zahav* (שַׁרְבִיט הַזָּהָב, "the golden scepter," 5:2) — the instrument of life. *Mishteh ha-yayin* (מִשְׁתֵּה הַיַּיִן, "the wine banquet," 5:6) — the formal name of the second course of the feast. *Ha-etz* (הָעֵץ, "the tree/gallows," 5:14) — the same word used for impalement throughout the book; a literary leitwort for execution that returns six times across chapters 5–9. *Chamishim amah* (חֲמִשִּׁים אַמָּה, "fifty cubits," 5:14) — the comic-hyperbolic dimension.

## Lens 2 — The Literature

Chapter 5 is the **comic peak before peripety** — the moment when Haman's pride reaches its full extension and the structural pivot of chapter 6 (the king's sleepless night) is laid as a loaded charge. The chapter is built as two paired scenes: (i) Esther's throne-room approach and the first banquet (5:1–8), and (ii) Haman's homecoming and the gallows-construction (5:9–14). Levenson and Berg both note the structural symmetry — both scenes are about *honor*, with Esther honored by the king and Haman honoring himself.

In the **chiastic structure** of the book, chapter 5 is paired with chapter 7: the first banquet here is structurally answered by the climactic banquet in chapter 7 where Esther reveals Haman's plot. The doubling of the banquet is the chapter's load-bearing literary device. **Frederic Bush** (WBC) and **Adele Berlin** (JPS) both press the question: *why* does Esther delay? She has the king's full attention at 5:3 and again at 5:6, and twice declines to make her petition. Three readings have been proposed:

(i) **Strategic delay** (Berg, Niditch): Esther is studying the king's mood, building social capital, ensuring Haman will be present at the moment of revelation. The two banquets are calculated to compromise Haman thoroughly before exposing him. This is the "shrewd diaspora stratagem" reading.

(ii) **Hesitation under pressure** (Bush, Berlin's earlier essays): Esther's nerve falters at 5:6, she postpones a day; the second banquet is the moment of returned courage. The reading honors Esther's psychological realism but underestimates the structural symmetry that the doubling produces.

(iii) **Literary necessity** (Levenson, Beal): the doubling is not a psychological choice but a structural requirement; chapter 6 — the chiastic pivot, the night the king cannot sleep — must fall *between* the two banquets, and the doubling exists to make that placement possible. The reading honors the book's literary architecture.

The three readings are not exclusive; Levenson allows that Esther is also strategic and may also be hesitant. But the structural reading is the strongest, because chapter 6 is not just *between* the banquets — it *requires* the gap. Without the postponement, no sleepless night, no chronicles-reading, no Mordecai-honoring procession, no peripety.

**Haman's hubris speech** in 5:11–12 is a literary set piece in the wisdom-tradition mode. He recounts (i) the splendor of his riches, (ii) the number of his sons, (iii) all the promotions with which the king had honored him, (iv) his elevation above the officials, and (v) Esther's exclusive invitation. The list of self-praise is deliberately reminiscent of **Proverbs 16:18** ("Pride goes before destruction, and a haughty spirit before a fall"); **Proverbs 27:1** ("Do not boast about tomorrow"); and the wisdom-tradition catalogue of the boastful. Yair Zakovitch reads the speech as an inverted echo of Job 29 (Job's reminiscence of past honor, in righteous lament rather than pride). The speech ends in the chapter's most-quoted self-undermining sentence: *kol zeh einennu shoveh li* — "yet all this is worth nothing to me" (5:13) — the comic-tragic admission that all the empire's honor cannot outweigh one Jew's refusal to bow.

**The doubling of the banquet** establishes the *mishteh* leitwort at its densest: 5:4, 5, 6, 8, 12, 14 — six occurrences in fourteen verses. The book's signature word is at maximum saturation in this chapter, and the saturation marks the comic peak. The legal leitwort *kedat* is suspended in chapter 5 — Esther's approach is *not* "according to the law" (4:16), and the chapter operates outside legal protocol.

**Literary devices.** The chapter is structured around **ironic juxtaposition**. Esther wins the king's favor with no demand made; Haman wins the king's favor and demands a 75-foot gallows. Esther speaks twice and says almost nothing of substance; Haman speaks twice and says nothing but his own glory. Esther conceals her petition; Haman conceals nothing. Berlin: "The chapter is built as a study in opposite registers."

**Zeresh's counsel** (5:14) is one of the chapter's signature ironies. She advises the gallows; Haman approves; the gallows is built; chapter 7 will see Haman impaled on it. The reversal is the *peripeteia* the book is famous for — Aristotelian peripety played for genuine theological-literary weight rather than Greek tragic pity. **Zeresh herself** will reverse her counsel in 6:13 ("If Mordecai… is of the Jewish people, you will not overcome him") — a prophetic admission that her own husband has misread the cosmos.

**The absent God.** Chapter 5 is the second consecutive chapter (with chapter 4) in which the divine name does not appear in the MT. The throne-room scene is described in purely human terms: Esther "won favor" (*nas'ah chen*, 5:2), the king "saw" her and held out the scepter. Compare Genesis 39:21 (Joseph: "the Lord was with him"); Daniel 1:9 (Daniel: "God gave him favor"); the explicit divine attribution is everywhere absent in Esther. The LXX Addition D supplies it: "God changed the spirit of the king to gentleness." The MT does not. Beal: "The chapter operates entirely on human means, and the king's mercy is never named as God's."

**Textual variants.** The LXX **Addition D** replaces the brief MT 5:1–2 with a 16-verse expanded throne-room scene including Esther's near-fainting and explicit divine intervention. Addition D is preserved in the major LXX manuscripts (Sinaiticus, Vaticanus, Alexandrinus) and is canonical for Catholics and Orthodox. The AT preserves a third form, somewhat between MT and LXX. The Vulgate, following Jerome, places Addition D at the end of the book in the Latin tradition. The MT's terseness is the textual outlier — the Greek tradition supplies the theological elaboration the Hebrew refuses.

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

Chapter 5 places the book at its high point of *peripety* setup, and the canonical resonances are structural rather than thematic. The hubris-before-fall pattern is wisdom-tradition (Proverbs 16:18, 18:12), but it also belongs to the Joseph cycle (Genesis 41:14–45 — Joseph's elevation tests the brothers' pride) and to the Daniel cycle (Daniel 4 — Nebuchadnezzar's hubris and humiliation). The diaspora court tale is the genre Levenson, Niditch, and Wills all locate Esther within, and chapter 5 is the genre's signature scene: the petitioner approaches the throne, the antagonist boasts, the reversal is set.

**Jewish Interpretive Trajectory.** Second Temple sources expand the scene: the LXX Addition D supplies Esther's near-fainting and divine intervention; Josephus *Antiquities* 11.234–245 elaborates further, with Esther swooning and God turning the king's anger. The rabbinic tradition (b. Megillah 15b–16a, Esther Rabbah) reads the chapter through *hashgachah* (providence): the king's holding-out of the scepter is divinely arranged; Esther's two-banquet strategy is Mordecai's instruction transmitted through divine guidance. **Megillah 15b** asks why Esther delayed, and offers seven separate answers — the multiplicity itself is a sign of the rabbis' interpretive engagement. **Rashi** follows the strategic-delay reading. **Ibn Ezra** is more philological. **Ralbag** introduces ethical reflection on the wisdom of Esther's timing.

The most distinctive Jewish-interpretive feature of chapter 5 is its **liturgical performance**. The Megillah is read on Purim with elaborate vocal practice; Haman's name is drowned out by groggers (*ra'ashanim*); the comic-hyperbolic register of the gallows speech is performed with broad expression. Aaron Koller (*Esther in Ancient Jewish Thought*) emphasizes that the chapter was *meant* to be performed, and the rabbinic and medieval reception preserved the performance tradition — chapter 5 is one of the chapters where the comic register comes through most fully in performance.

Modern Jewish scholarship (Berg, Fox, Berlin, Levenson, Hazony, Niditch) reads the chapter literarily, with attention to the doubling-banquet structure, the wisdom-tradition hubris frame, and the diaspora-stratagem reading of Esther's delay. **Sandra Berg's** monograph traces the chapter's parallel-banquet structure as the book's signature literary device. **Levenson's OTL commentary** treats the chapter as the comic peak before peripety and connects it explicitly to Aristotelian *peripeteia* and to the wisdom-tradition's pride-before-fall pattern.

**Christian Interpretive Trajectory.** The patristic record on chapter 5 is dominated by the Greek Additions; **Origen** read Addition D as canonical; **Jerome** distinguished but preserved it. The throne-room scene with explicit divine intervention (Addition D) was the version most early Christian readers knew. **Clement of Rome** (*1 Clem* 55) implicitly references it.

**Medieval Christian** reading allegorizes heavily. **Rabanus Maurus** (*Expositio in librum Esther*) reads Haman's hubris as the devil's pride before fall — Lucifer's *non serviam* — and the gallows as the cross of Christ that the devil prepared for himself. The reading is theologically rich but historically inattentive: it loses the wisdom-tradition shape and the diaspora-political weight. **Bede** and the **Glossa Ordinaria** carry the line forward.

The **Reformation** brings the usual divisions. **Luther's** dismissal of Esther broadly (*Table Talk* §475) means no Reformed commentary from him on chapter 5. **Calvin's** silence on Esther leaves no commentary. The **English Puritans** (William Perkins, Joseph Hall, Matthew Henry) developed a providential-typological reading: Haman as the type of the proud earthly tyrant whose fall illustrates Proverbs 16:18; Esther as the type of the church's intercession.

**Critical scholarship** (Wellhausen, Lewis B. Paton ICC 1908) read the chapter as nationalist comedy with deficient theology. **Bickerman** recovered it as Hellenistic literary art. **Contemporary Christian commentary** — Moore (Anchor), Bush (WBC), Karen Jobes (NIVAC), Iain Duguid, Greidanus — reads the chapter literarily and providentially, with Jobes emphasizing the providential structure and Duguid emphasizing the chapter's silence about God.

**Feminist readers** have given chapter 5 sustained attention. **Athalya Brenner** and **Esther Fuchs** read Esther's two-banquet delay as compromised femme-fatale stereotype — the seductive queen working by indirection rather than direct petition. **Sandra Berg** and **Susan Niditch** read it as shrewd diaspora strategy in the trickster-tradition (parallels: Abigail in 1 Sam 25; Tamar in Gen 38; Judith in the apocryphal book). The contrast in feminist readings is sharp; **Sidnie White Crawford** offers a mediating position that Esther is *both* compromised and shrewd, and the book leaves the moral evaluation open.

**Where the traditions converge and diverge.** Jewish reading honors the chapter's comic-performative register; Christian reading allegorizes it; feminist reading is divided between critical-stereotype and trickster-strategy readings. Levenson reads the chapter's *peripeteia* setup as the canonical instance of wisdom-tradition reversal staged in diaspora-court mode — a literary form that Christian commentary has often allegorized away rather than reading on its own terms.

## Lens 4 — The Theological Intent

**Tension 1: Indirection vs. direct petition — is Esther's two-banquet delay shrewd or compromised?** Three positions:

The **trickster-strategy** reading (Berg, Niditch, Crawford, much of contemporary Jewish scholarship): Esther's delay is calculated diaspora maneuver, paralleling Abigail (1 Sam 25), Tamar (Gen 38), and Judith — women in the canon and apocrypha who use indirection as a survival technology against unequal power. The two banquets compromise Haman thoroughly (he is twice exposed as Esther's only invited guest, twice present when she addresses the king) and ensure his structural defeat. Steelmanned: the reading honors Esther's agency and locates her in a robust canonical tradition of female trickster-figures whose maneuvers are explicitly endorsed by the texts (David honors Abigail; Tamar is vindicated; Judith is celebrated).

The **femme-fatale-critical** reading (Brenner, Fuchs, some early feminist readings): Esther's delay reproduces the patriarchal stereotype of the seductive queen working through dinner and wine rather than direct speech; the reading encodes a misogynist framework that punishes Vashti's directness and rewards Esther's indirection. Steelmanned: the reading correctly identifies the chapter's deep ambivalence about female speech; Vashti spoke directly and was deposed, Esther speaks obliquely and is rewarded, and the contrast is structurally suspicious.

The **wisdom-tradition** reading (Levenson, Bush, Berlin's later commentary): Esther's delay is the literary mechanism of *peripeteia* — the chapter is built to allow chapter 6 to fall between the banquets, and the doubling is the book's signature device, not a moral statement about Esther's strategy. Steelmanned: this is the reading most faithful to the book's literary architecture; the doubling is structural before it is psychological or strategic.

The **rabbinic** reading (Megillah 15b, Esther Rabbah): seven separate answers are given in the Talmud for Esther's delay — divine arrangement, fear of Haman, fear of God, study of the king's mood, etc. The multiplicity is its own answer: the rabbis treat the question as legitimately open. Steelmanned: the rabbinic refusal to choose a single answer honors the chapter's deliberate ambiguity.

*Where this leaves us:* the four readings are not equally weighted. The wisdom-tradition reading is structurally strongest; the trickster-strategy reading is most empowering of Esther's agency; the femme-fatale reading correctly identifies the chapter's ambivalence; the rabbinic reading honors the unresolvable question. The chapter does not adjudicate, and the unwillingness to adjudicate is itself the book's mode.

**Tension 2: Hubris and divine retribution — does the chapter teach a wisdom-doctrine of pride-before-fall, an allegorical doctrine of the devil's self-destruction, or a literary doctrine of comic peripety?** Three positions:

The **wisdom-traditional** reading (Proverbs 16:18, the Christian moralists from Augustine through Matthew Henry): Haman's hubris is the canonical illustration of the wisdom-tradition's pride-before-fall doctrine; the chapter teaches that self-exaltation precedes destruction, and the gallows-built-for-another is the moral instrument of the boaster's own ruin. Steelmanned: the reading is canonically resonant — Proverbs and the wisdom literature articulate exactly this pattern, and the chapter is a literary instantiation of it.

The **patristic-medieval allegorical** reading (Rabanus Maurus, Glossa Ordinaria, the Christian devotional tradition): Haman is the devil whose pride seeks the destruction of God's people; the gallows is the cross — the instrument the devil prepared for Christ that became the instrument of his own defeat (cf. 1 Cor 2:8: "the rulers of this age… would not have crucified the Lord of glory"). Steelmanned: the typological reading reads the chapter cosmically and connects it to the central Christian narrative; the pattern of "the weapon turned against its maker" is genuinely scriptural (Psalm 7:15–16; Psalm 9:15).

The **literary-comic** reading (Levenson, Beal, Bush, Berlin, Berg): the chapter is comic *peripeteia* in the Aristotelian sense, and its theological content is the literary structure itself; the empire's largest tyrant builds the largest gallows for the smallest petty grievance, and the structure of the inversion is the book's witness. The chapter teaches by form, not by doctrine. Steelmanned: this reading honors the book's carnivalesque register and refuses to flatten the comic excess into a moralizing lesson; the gallows is a joke before it is a moral, and the book's theology is lived in the joke.

*Where this leaves us:* the wisdom-tradition and literary-comic readings can coexist; the allegorical reading is now mostly archaeological. The chapter's theology is in its structure: hubris reaches its peak in 5:11–13, the gallows is built in 5:14, and chapter 6 will see the king cannot sleep. The structure *is* the argument.

**One unresolved question.** If the chapter's theology lives in its structure — the doubling banquets that make peripety possible, the gallows built for the man who will hang from it — what does it mean that the canon's most explicit demonstration of providential reversal is also its most carefully literary, with no divine name to anchor it? Can structure carry providence on its own?

## The Thread

**Proverbs 16:18; 18:12.** "Pride goes before destruction." Haman's 5:11–13 boast is the canonical illustration; the gallows of 5:14 is the destruction the proverb predicts.

**Psalm 7:15–16; Psalm 9:15.** "He makes a pit, digging it out, and falls into the hole that he has made… The nations have sunk in the pit that they made; in the net that they hid, their own foot has been caught." The "weapon turned against its maker" pattern is the chapter's structural backbone, and the Psalmist's language is the canonical voice for it.

**1 Samuel 25:14–35 (Abigail).** Abigail's strategic intervention before David, with food and wine and indirection, is the canonical female-tactical-petition parallel. Berg argues Esther 5 deliberately echoes 1 Samuel 25.

**Esther 7:9–10 (forward link).** Harbona will name the gallows "fifty cubits high" at Haman's house. The 5:14 gallows is the chapter's loaded charge that fires in 7:10 — Haman impaled on his own scaffold, the *peripeteia* the book is famous for.
