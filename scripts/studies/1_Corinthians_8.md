# 1 Corinthians 8 — Going Deeper

## Historical World

The chapter begins peri de tōn eidōlothytōn — "now concerning food offered to idols" — the third in a sequence of peri de markers (7:1, 7:25, 8:1) that almost certainly answer questions the Corinthians sent Paul, perhaps via the delegation of 16:17. To grasp what is at stake, one has to grasp what eidōlothyton was in Roman Corinth around AD 53-54.

Roman Corinth, refounded by Julius Caesar as Colonia Laus Iulia Corinthiensis in 44 BC after Mummius's destruction in 146 BC, was the capital of the senatorial province of Achaia and the seat of the proconsul whose tenure is fixed by the Gallio inscription at Delphi (AD 51-52). It was a freedman colony perched on the diolkos between the Ionian and Aegean seas, dominated by the Acrocorinth with its sanctuary of Aphrodite, and ringed by temples — to Apollo (the surviving archaic temple), to the imperial cult, to Demeter and Kore on the slopes, and most importantly for this chapter, the Asclepieion just north of the city walls. Excavations at the Asclepieion uncovered three small dining rooms (triclinia), each with stone benches for about eleven diners, used for cultic meals; the anatomical terracotta votives recovered there (legs, ears, breasts, genitalia) attest the kind of healing piety that drew ordinary Corinthians into temple precincts. Murphy-O'Connor (St. Paul's Corinth, 1983) and Dale Martin (The Corinthian Body, 1995) reconstruct these dining rooms as the social setting of "eating in an idol's temple" in 8:10.

Eidōlothyton — "idol-food" — referred to the meat of animals slaughtered in temple sacrifice, of which a portion was burned to the deity, a portion taken by the priests, and a portion either consumed in the temple's dining room or sold off in the macellum (the meat market). Justin Meggitt (Paul, Poverty and Survival, 1998) and Wendell Willis (Idol Meat in Corinth, 1985) have shown how thoroughly civic life — birthdays, weddings, guild dinners, civic festivals — was woven into sacrificial meat distribution. Meat was a luxury for non-elite Corinthians; outside festival days, ordinary people rarely ate it. The poor would have encountered it almost exclusively as eidōlothyton. Bruce Winter (After Paul Left Corinth, 2001) emphasizes the imperial cult's saturation of public space: a Christian who refused all eidōlothyton refused, in effect, civic participation.

The Corinthian church was socially mixed. The Erastus pavement near the theater — "Erastus, in return for his aedileship, laid the pavement at his own expense" — is plausibly the Erastus of Romans 16:23 (oikonomos tēs poleōs). Theissen and Meeks established that the congregation included a few high-status patrons and a majority of low-status freedmen, slaves, and the working poor. Paul's "we know that all of us possess knowledge" (8:1) is almost certainly a quotation back to the Corinthians of their own slogan — likely the position of the educated, philosophically literate "strong" who claimed enlightened indifference to idols and freely accepted dinner invitations in temple precincts.

## Literature

The chapter is a tightly braided diatribe. Paul opens with a slogan-and-correction structure that runs through the letter: "all of us possess knowledge" (their slogan) — "this knowledge puffs up, but love builds up" (his correction). The same move recurs in 8:4, 6:12, 10:23. Margaret Mitchell (Paul and the Rhetoric of Reconciliation, 1991) reads 1 Corinthians as a single deliberative argument for concord (homonoia), and chapter 8 is a textbook unit of that argument: thesis (knowledge versus love), exposition (the theological content of the supposed knowledge), application (the case of the weak brother), and personal example (8:13 — "I will never eat meat").

The architecture of 8:1-3 is chiastic and characteristically Pauline. The Corinthians prize knowing; Paul reframes the prize as being known. "If anyone loves God, he is known by him" (egnōstai hyp' autou) inverts the human-active to divine-active voice. Anthony Thiselton (NIGTC 2000) notes the perfect tense — to be known by God is a settled, prior reality — and pairs it with Galatians 4:9 ("you have come to know God, or rather to be known by God"). The verse is the chapter's hinge: knowledge that does not flow from being-loved-and-known by God is the kind that puffs up.

The doxological core, 8:6, breaks the prose into an almost creedal cadence:

- one God, the Father (heis Theos ho Patēr), from whom (ex hou) are all things and for whom (eis auton) we exist
- one Lord, Jesus Christ (heis Kyrios Iēsous Christos), through whom (di' hou) are all things and through whom we exist

The two prepositions — ek and dia — distribute creation between Father and Son without a third. Gordon Fee (NICNT 1987 rev. 2014) calls this the most decisive Christological text in the Pauline corpus. Hans Conzelmann (Hermeneia 1969) and Joseph Fitzmyer (Anchor Yale 2008) note its hymnic feel — possibly liturgical, possibly Paul's own composition.

The closing movement (8:7-13) drops from doxology to pastoral case. The argument runs: knowledge is not universally distributed; some have a "weak conscience" (asthenēs syneidēsis); their consciences are wounded (mōlypetai) by the example of the strong; the strong "destroy" (apollytai) the weak brother for whom Christ died; therefore the strong sin against Christ. The crescendo at 8:13 is rhetorical hyperbole in the form of a personal vow — "if food (brōma) makes my brother stumble, I will never eat meat (krea) into the age (eis ton aiōna)." The shift from brōma to krea is precise: Paul is not vowing vegetarianism; he is vowing total avoidance of the disputed substance.

## The Larger Story

### The Jewish trajectory

The single most important Jewish background is the Shema (Deut 6:4): "Hear, O Israel: the LORD our God, the LORD is one" (YHWH eloheinu YHWH ehad). Twice-daily recitation was already standard practice in Second Temple Judaism (m. Berakhot 1; the Nash Papyrus from c. 150 BC preserves it as a liturgical text). Jewish monotheism — the refusal to acknowledge any God beside YHWH — was the boundary marker of the diaspora synagogue, and idol food was its concrete frontier.

Mishnah Avodah Zarah (compiled c. AD 200 but reflecting earlier Tannaitic rulings) prohibits eating, drinking, or commercially benefiting from anything offered to idols. The Apostolic Decree of Acts 15:29, instructing Gentile believers to abstain from "what has been sacrificed to idols," reflects the Jewish-Christian compromise position. Paul's stance in 1 Corinthians 8 stands in a different register from both. Daniel Boyarin (A Radical Jew, 1994) and Mark Nanos read Paul as still working within Jewish categories — affirming monotheism uncompromisingly while pushing on the boundaries of how Jewish norms apply to Gentiles in Christ.

The most striking Jewish move is in 8:6. Larry Hurtado (Lord Jesus Christ, 2003) and Richard Bauckham (Jesus and the God of Israel, 2008) have argued that this verse is among the earliest unmistakable instances of Christological monotheism: Paul takes the Shema's "Lord our God, the Lord is one" (Kyrios ho Theos hēmōn Kyrios heis in the LXX), splits it into "one God, the Father" and "one Lord, Jesus Christ," and yet — and this is the point — does not break Jewish monotheism. Bauckham's category of "divine identity" captures this: Jesus is included within the unique identity of YHWH, sharing in creation (the function the Shema reserves to God). Paul has rewritten the central Jewish prayer with Jesus inside it. Whether this is a "high Christology" already at AD 54 (Hurtado/Bauckham) or a developing trajectory (James Dunn, Did the First Christians Worship Jesus?, 2010, more cautious; Maurice Casey skeptical) is the live debate.

### The Christian trajectory

Patristic interpretation of 1 Corinthians 8 ran along two tracks. On idol food itself, Justin (1 Apology 1.66), Tertullian (De Idolatria), and the canons of early synods (Elvira c. 305) hardened into total prohibition; eidōlothyton became one of the marks distinguishing Christian from pagan civic life. By the time of Augustine, the underlying logic was less about cultic contagion and more about scandal — the weak-brother frame.

On the doxological core, 8:6 was a major Nicene-era proof text. Athanasius and the Cappadocians cited it for the consubstantial divinity of the Son. The Arian counter-reading — the Son is not the Father; the Father alone is "one God" — forced the church to specify what Paul's prepositions meant. By Augustine's De Trinitate (V-VII), the orthodox reading was settled: the Son is "one Lord" within the "one God," not a second deity nor a subordinate creature.

The Reformation reopened the strong/weak question under the rubric of adiaphora — things indifferent. Luther's Romans-12 freedom-for-the-neighbor became programmatic. Calvin (Institutes 3.19) treated 1 Corinthians 8 as the exegetical foundation of Christian liberty under the discipline of love. Westminster Confession 20.2 follows: "God alone is Lord of the conscience, and hath left it free from the doctrines and commandments of men, which are, in any thing, contrary to His Word; or beside it, in matters of faith, or worship." The Catholic moral-theological tradition systematized the same problem under cooperation in evil — formal versus material, proximate versus remote — articulated by Alphonsus Liguori and codified in modern manuals.

The modern missiological tradition has read chapter 8 alongside chapter 9 as the charter of contextualization. Andrew Walls and Lamin Sanneh on translatability; the Lausanne movement on cultural sensitivity; Catholic missio ad gentes in Vatican II's Ad Gentes — all draw on the strong/weak dialectic to ask which cultural elements a new church must reject and which it may baptize.

## Theological Intent

### Tension 1: Christian liberty and the weak brother

What does Paul actually require of the strong?

**The Reformed adiaphora reading.** The strong are theologically right; idols have no real existence; food in itself is morally neutral. But Christian liberty is conditioned by love, not abstract right. Westminster 20.2 and Calvin together: liberty exists, but its exercise is bounded by the conscience of the neighbor and the building-up of the body. Practically: yes to the macellum, no to the temple dining room, yes or no to the dinner invitation depending on the company. Strong reading. The text supports the structure.

**The Lutheran two-uses-of-the-law reading.** The law's first use (civil restraint) and second use (revealing sin) both bear on this case; the strong's freedom is real but the law of love restrains its public exercise. Steelman: this reading captures the dialectic between freedom and constraint without collapsing into legalism. It also has the virtue of taking seriously Paul's hyperbolic vow at 8:13 — Paul models the constraint, not just commends it.

**The Catholic moral-theology of cooperation reading.** The strong's eating in the temple constitutes proximate scandal — placing an occasion of sin before the weak. Even formally indifferent acts become sinful when their material effect is the destruction of the brother (8:11). This frame has been applied across centuries to questions from civic religion to investment in immoral industries. Strength: it gives precise tools for graded judgment. Weakness: the categories can become so fine that they substitute for the love Paul foregrounds.

**The Anabaptist separationist reading.** The pacifist-separatist tradition (Schleitheim 1527, Hutterite communalism) reads chapters 8-10 as warrant for ascetic withdrawal from civic religion altogether. The "strong" who eat in temple precincts are not enlightened but compromised; the church is to mark itself off from idolatrous civic life by its visible practices. Steelman this position: it takes seriously how thoroughly Roman civic religion saturated every meal, and refuses the comfortable middle that allows the church to baptize whatever the dominant culture serves.

The text's own pressure runs against any reading that turns Christian liberty into a mere right to be exercised. Knowledge "puffs up" (physioi); love "builds up" (oikodomei). The verb oikodomeō runs through the letter (3:9, 14:3-5, 14:17, 14:26) as Paul's master-image for what the church is supposed to be doing — building, not asserting.

### Tension 2: Christological monotheism in 8:6

How much weight can 8:6 bear?

**Early high Christology (Hurtado, Bauckham).** 8:6 is a binitarian rewriting of the Shema, datable to within twenty-five years of the crucifixion. Jesus is included within the unique divine identity by being assigned the function of agency in creation — a function reserved by Second Temple Judaism for God alone. This is not the product of a long evolution from low to high Christology; it is the Christological starting point of the earliest church.

**Evolutionary Christology (Dunn).** 8:6 reflects high-functional honor accorded to the risen Christ but stops short of full ontological identification with God. Dunn's distinction between functional and ontic categories: Christ acts in the place of God, mediates God's purposes, but the move to consubstantial language ("of one substance") is a fourth-century clarification, not a first-century datum. Steelman this view: it takes seriously that Paul has not said heis Theos heis Kyrios as a single phrase about a single subject; he has split the formula. Reading the split as a settled binitarian statement may be a fourth-century projection.

**The Jewish-rewriting reading (Boyarin).** Paul is doing what other Second Temple Jews did with Wisdom, Logos, and the Memra — locating a second figure within or alongside the divine identity without yet sharpening the question of how. 8:6 is a Jewish move, not a Christian-against-Jewish move. The trajectory toward Nicea is real but not inevitable from this text alone.

**The unitarian/Socinian reading.** A minority — Socinians, modern Unitarian Universalists, Christadelphians, Jehovah's Witnesses — read 8:6 as decisive evidence that Paul distinguished the one God (the Father) from the one Lord (Jesus, who is not God), reading Kyrios in 8:6 in a lower-than-divine sense. The reading struggles with how Paul preserves Jewish monotheism while introducing a second figure to whom creation is also ascribed (a function the Shema reserves to YHWH alone). Steelman attempt: Paul is honoring Jesus with the title Lord that Israel gave to its Messiah and its emperors, not consciously redrawing the doctrine of God.

The strongest reading remains Bauckham's. The grammatical move — splitting the Shema's two terms while preserving its monotheism — is too deliberate to be accidental, and it functions in 1 Corinthians 8 not as decoration but as the polemical core: against the "many gods and many lords" of Roman polytheism, the Christian community confesses one God and one Lord, in whom and through whom all things exist. To say less is to read against the grammatical pressure of Paul's own formulation.

### Where this leaves us

Paul refuses to settle the case at the level of theological propositions. The Corinthian "strong" had a defensible monotheism — "an idol has no real existence" — and a defensible ethics — "food will not commend us to God." Paul concedes both. He then writes the entire rest of the chapter to demonstrate that the question is wrongly posed. The question is not what is true of food and idols. The question is what the strong are doing to the weak brother for whom Christ died.

The most uncomfortable line in the chapter is 8:11: "by your knowledge this weak person is destroyed (apollytai), the brother for whom Christ died." The verb is the same Paul uses in 1:18 for those who are perishing. To wound a weak brother's conscience is to undo, by one's knowledge, what the cross did. This is no longer a question of liberty; it is a question of cruciformity. Knowledge that "puffs up" — the philosophically literate sneer at the convert who still feels a shudder at the smell of sacrifice — operates against the cross.

### One unresolved question

Where exactly does the strong/weak dynamic stop applying? The Pauline framework requires the strong to bear the conscience-burden of the weak. But contemporary disputes — over alcohol and food rules, over participation in cultural rites, over what Christians may and may not do alongside their non-Christian neighbors — quickly run into the question of whether the "weak" position is being given a permanent veto. Paul nowhere says the weak conscience is correct; he says it must not be wounded. How long does the bearing-with last? At what point does pastoral care for the weak require not deference but formation? The chapter does not say.

### The Thread

Knowledge that knows the truth about idols but does not know the brother for whom Christ died is the wrong kind of knowledge. Paul sets in front of the Corinthians a doxological miniature — one God, one Lord, all things from him and through him — and uses it not to settle a metaphysical dispute but to relocate the frame. To be in this one God and this one Lord is to be in a body in which the destruction of one brother for whom Christ died is the destruction of what the cross was for. The chapter closes not with a rule but with a vow, and the vow is the apostolic shape of the answer: I will give up what is mine before I will harm what is his.
