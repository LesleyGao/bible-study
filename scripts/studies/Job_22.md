# Job 22: The Fabricated Indictment

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

Eliphaz the Temanite rises for the third and final time, and something has visibly broken in the friends' argument. Teman, as elsewhere in the book, points to northwest Arabia — a district of Edom renowned in biblical tradition for its sages (Jeremiah 49:7, Obadiah 8-9). The world Eliphaz inhabits and imputes to Job is the clan-based patriarchal economy of the Transjordanian-Arabian cultural zone during the second millennium BCE: pastoral wealth counted in flocks and servants, land controlled by the powerful (22:8), justice administered at the village gate, the destitute surviving on the gleaning-rights of widows and orphans. The accusations Eliphaz invents — withholding pledge-garments overnight (22:6), refusing water and bread to the weary (22:7), crushing the arms of the fatherless (22:9) — are precisely the covenant-violations Israelite legal codes will later catalogue (Exodus 22:21-27, Deuteronomy 24:17-22). Their currency in Eliphaz's mouth tells us less about Job's actual behavior than about the ancient Near Eastern moral vocabulary shared across Edom, Canaan, and Mesopotamia.

The authorship and dating of Job remain among the most contested questions in Hebrew Bible scholarship. Marvin Pope (Anchor Bible), David Clines (Word Biblical Commentary), and Carol Newsom (New Interpreter's Bible) argue for a post-exilic composition, pointing to Aramaisms, the developed figure of הַשָּׂטָן (*ha-satan*, "the accuser") in the prologue, and theological affinities with Second Isaiah and post-exilic wisdom. Robert Gordis and Francis Andersen push earlier, reading Job as pre-exilic, possibly Solomonic in its oldest strata, built on a much older patriarchal folk tradition. The decisive middle witness is Ezekiel 14:14, which by the sixth century BCE treats Job alongside Noah and Daniel (or Danel) as a figure of proverbial righteousness — meaning the Job tradition is already ancient by the exile even if the poetic dialogues were shaped later. Most scholars now accept a composite theory: an old folk narrative wrapping a later, sophisticated poetic dialogue, with final editing in the Persian period.

The archaeological and textual comparanda for chapter 22 are rich. *Ludlul bēl nēmeqi* ("I Will Praise the Lord of Wisdom"), the so-called Babylonian Job, stages a righteous sufferer whose friends likewise probe for hidden sin. The *Babylonian Theodicy* is an acrostic dialogue between a sufferer and a pious friend whose arguments parallel Eliphaz's almost line for line — divine justice is unsearchable, the wicked appear to prosper, humility is counseled. Ugaritic texts from Ras Shamra illuminate the poetic idiom: the phrase "gold of Ophir" (22:24, אוֹפִיר, *ophir*) has cognates in trade-route inventories from Ugarit and later South Arabian inscriptions. The Dead Sea Scrolls preserve 11QTargum Job, an Aramaic translation whose wording at points diverges from the Masoretic Text and clarifies obscure Hebrew forms. The Septuagint of Job is famously about four hundred lines shorter than the MT, suggesting either Greek abridgment or a shorter Hebrew *Vorlage*; the cuts cluster heaviest in the third cycle, which matters enormously for chapters 22-27.

Five Hebrew terms carry the chapter. שַׁדַּי (*Shaddai*, "the Almighty," שַׁדַּי) appears six times in this chapter alone — an archaic divine title favored in Job and in the patriarchal narratives, linked etymologically either to "mountain" (Akkadian *šadû*) or to "field/breast" (Hebrew *śādeh*/*šad*). חֲבֹל (*ḥăbōl*, "pledge," חֲבֹל) in 22:6 is the technical legal term for the garment-pledge regulated in Exodus 22:26-27. צֶדֶק (*ṣedeq*, "righteousness," צֶדֶק) threads through Eliphaz's lecture as what Job has allegedly forfeited. הוֹכָחָה (*tōkaḥat*, "reproof," תּוֹכַחַת) and מוּסָר (*mûsār*, "instruction," מוּסָר) in 22:4 and 22:22 establish the wisdom-school vocabulary of pedagogical suffering. Finally, בֶּצֶר (*beṣer*, "gold-ore," בֶּצֶר) in 22:24, a rare metallurgical term, anchors the chapter's climactic image of riches relinquished to the dust.

## Lens 2 — The Literature

The book of Job opens and closes with prose (chapters 1-2, 42:7-17) framing a vast poetic dialogue (3:1-42:6). Chapter 22 sits deep inside the poetic core, at the opening of the third and final cycle of speeches between Job and his friends. The first two cycles follow a predictable pattern: Eliphaz, Job, Bildad, Job, Zophar, Job. But beginning here, in chapter 22, the architecture begins to collapse. Eliphaz delivers his third speech with force. Job replies. Bildad's third speech in chapter 25 is only six verses. Zophar never speaks a third time at all. Fragments of what look like friends' speeches appear in Job's mouth across chapters 26-27. This textual disturbance is one of the most discussed phenomena in Job scholarship; Marvin Pope, David Clines, and Norman Habel all argue the third cycle has suffered textual corruption, reordering, or scribal excision. Some conservative readers (Andersen, Hartley) defend the received order as deliberate: the friends are running out of arguments, and the disintegration of their speeches is itself the poet's signal that orthodoxy has exhausted itself. Either way, chapter 22 is the last fully-formed foray of the friends, and it carries the weight of their collapsing case.

The literary strategy of Eliphaz's third speech is striking. Having run out of general arguments about the fate of the wicked, he turns specific and falsifies. The rhetorical movement is classic prosecutorial: opening with theological distance-questions (22:2-4) that frame God as morally unaffected by human behavior, then the sudden pivot to accusation (22:5, "Is not your evil abundant?"), then the concrete bill of particulars (22:6-9), then the consequence clause (22:10-11), then a doxological detour on divine transcendence (22:12-14) that defensively inoculates his reasoning against Job's experience, then the old-road topos of the antediluvian wicked (22:15-20), and finally an altar call (22:21-30). The Hebrew parallelism is tight and legal: verse 6 uses synonymous parallelism (exact pledges / strip clothing), verse 9 chiastic parallelism (widows sent away / fatherless arms crushed). The invented specificity is itself the literary giveaway. Robert Alter notes that when biblical speakers abandon generality for concrete charges, the text demands we ask whether the charges are true — and chapters 1-2 have already told us they are not.

Legal vocabulary saturates the chapter. The *rib* pattern — covenant lawsuit — runs through the verbs "reprove" (*yōkiaḥ*), "enter into judgment" (*bô' ba-mišpāṭ*), and the pledge-garment terminology. Habel reads Eliphaz as assuming the role of a prosecuting attorney who has exceeded his evidence. The curse-form embedded in 22:10-11 ("snares are all around you, sudden terror overwhelms you, darkness, a flood of water") follows the classic ancient Near Eastern treaty-curse structure attested in Hittite and neo-Assyrian vassal treaties — a form the Hebrew Bible reuses in Deuteronomy 28 and Leviticus 26. Eliphaz is performing a micro-covenant-curse liturgy over Job.

The closing section (22:21-30) is formally a *Heilsorakel*, a salvation oracle with conditional protasis ("If you return...") and apodosis ("then the Almighty will be your gold"). Wordplay saturates it: gold-as-dust (22:24) inverts to God-as-gold (22:25), a chiastic reversal that becomes explicit in the Hebrew with *beṣer* ("gold-ore") and *beṣārayik* ("your precious things") echoing against each other. The final verse (22:30), with its enigmatic "he delivers even the one who is not innocent," has puzzled translators — the Hebrew אִי־נָקִי (*'î-nāqî*, literally "not-innocent" or possibly "island of the innocent") admits multiple readings. The LXX differs from the MT here, and 11QTargum Job offers a distinct Aramaic phrasing, suggesting early textual instability in this very verse.

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

Within the Hebrew canon Job sits among the wisdom writings, in the Ketuvim, alongside Proverbs and Ecclesiastes. The three form a wisdom dialectic: Proverbs teaches the order of retribution, Ecclesiastes the breakdown of that order under the observation of time and chance, and Job the protest against its misapplication to innocent suffering. Eliphaz's third speech is effectively a hostile recital of Proverbs' retribution logic — and the book of Job exists precisely to refuse that recital.

The Jewish trajectory of reading Job is long and self-critical. The apocryphal *Testament of Job* (1st century BCE–1st century CE) retells the story with Job as patient saint, elides the dialogues, and extends the friends' theology into hagiography. The Qumran 11QTargum Job, paleographically dated to the late Herodian period, is significant because it preserves large portions of an Aramaic Job and occasionally resolves MT obscurities. The Talmud's treatment in *Bava Batra* 15a-16b contains the famous debate over whether Job ever lived: Rabbi Shimon ben Laqish says "Job never existed and was never created; he was a parable," while others insist on his historicity. Rashi reads Eliphaz pastorally — his errors are those of a well-meaning teacher who has over-systematized divine providence. Abraham Ibn Ezra notices the grammatical oddities of chapter 22 and flags several verses as textually unstable. Maimonides, in *Guide of the Perplexed* III.22-23, treats the friends as representing the four major positions on providence in his intellectual world: Eliphaz voices the Torah-retribution view, Bildad the Mu'tazilite view, Zophar the Asharite view, and Elihu the correct position. For Maimonides, Eliphaz's error is treating providence as mechanical. Nachmanides offers a more mystical reading, connecting Job's suffering to transmigration. Martin Buber in *The Prophetic Faith* reads Job 22 as the point at which the friend becomes accuser, rupturing the *I-Thou* of friendship. Jon Levenson's work on creation and the persistence of evil reads the chapter as evidence that the book refuses to let theodicy stabilize. James Kugel emphasizes the changing interpretive frames — how each generation of Jewish readers used Job to address the suffering actually in front of them. Avivah Zornberg's literary-psychoanalytic readings locate in Eliphaz's fabricated charges the defensive projection of a pious mind refusing the possibility of innocent suffering.

The Christian trajectory is equally layered. Gregory the Great's *Moralia in Job*, thirty-five books of moral and allegorical commentary composed between 579 and 595 CE, treats Eliphaz as a figure of heretical orthodoxy — a teacher whose doctrines, correct in the abstract, become poisonous when misapplied. Origen, Chrysostom, and Augustine use Job as a proto-typological figure; Augustine in particular reads Job's sufferings Christologically. Thomas Aquinas's *Expositio super Iob ad litteram* is one of his most sustained biblical commentaries and reads chapter 22 as Eliphaz's failure to distinguish between God's ordinary providence and extraordinary permission. John Calvin preached 159 sermons on Job between 1554 and 1555 — some of the most concentrated attention Job has ever received — and on chapter 22 he argues that Eliphaz utters true propositions inside a false indictment, so that Job's task is to reject the application while honoring the theology. Luther read Job as a parable of *Anfechtung*, spiritual trial, and was particularly struck by how pious speech can wound. Julius Wellhausen, Hermann Gunkel, and Gerhard von Rad in the critical tradition read the book as a crisis inside the Israelite wisdom tradition, with the friends voicing the settled view and Job the protest. Clines's three-volume Word Biblical Commentary is now the standard critical reference; Newsom's Moral Imaginations study reads Job 22-25 as a rhetorical breakdown of the discourse of retribution. Gustavo Gutiérrez's *On Job: God-Talk and the Suffering of the Innocent* reads Eliphaz from within Latin American liberation theology as the voice of the established religious order refusing to see the innocent poor. Ellen Davis in *Getting Involved With God* treats Eliphaz as the cautionary figure for pastors — the person whose theology has become weaponry. Susannah Ticciati's *Job and the Disruption of Identity* argues the disintegration of the third cycle is precisely the point: the friends' discourse is dying on stage. Norman Habel and John Hartley represent more conservative literary-theological readings, defending textual integrity. Andersen's Tyndale commentary reads with pastoral restraint, urging that Eliphaz not be demonized even as he errs.

The convergences across Jewish and Christian readings are striking. Both traditions identify Eliphaz as the seductive figure of pious wrongness. Both recognize that his theology is largely orthodox and largely misapplied. Both wrestle with the question of whether to emend or preserve the disturbed third cycle. The divergences cluster around theodicy: Jewish readings more often leave the question open and live inside it, while Christian readings press toward Christological or eschatological resolution.

## Lens 4 — The Theological Intent

### Tension 1: Can pious speech be slander?

Eliphaz's third speech invents sins Job has not committed (1:1 pronounces Job *tām wĕ-yāšār*, "blameless and upright"). If this is slander wrapped in theological vocabulary, the chapter forces us to ask whether the right framework can generate false charges when applied by a defensive interpreter.

- **Catholic (Aquinas, Gregory the Great)**: Yes. Eliphaz commits the sin of rash judgment (*iudicium temerarium*). The Catechism (§2477-2478) treats this as a sin against the Eighth Commandment. Correct doctrine does not license imputation of specific unknown sins. Aquinas's commentary calls this a failure of the intellectual virtue of prudence, not merely a moral lapse.
- **Reformed Protestant (Calvin, Ticciati)**: Yes, and the chapter is evidence of how depravity corrupts pious minds. Calvin is unusually severe: "Eliphaz, being unable to overcome Job by truth, manufactures falsehood, and this is how self-deception works in the godly." Total depravity extends even to the consolation of sufferers.
- **Lutheran (Luther, Bonhoeffer)**: The friend here functions as the false law, crushing without grace. *Anfechtung* is intensified when the wounding comes from the religious community. The pastoral implication is that the speech of the church to the suffering can itself be a work of the devil.
- **Orthodox (Chrysostom, patristic tradition)**: Less psychologically framed; more concerned with Job's endurance under false accusation as ascetic imitation of Christ. Eliphaz is a figure of the tempter, whose errors Job absorbs without succumbing.
- **Jewish (Maimonides, Buber, Zornberg)**: Maimonides sees Eliphaz's speech as technically correct theology misapplied; it is not slander but mistaken diagnosis. Buber intensifies: Eliphaz has converted friendship into courtroom, rupturing covenantal relationship. Zornberg reads the fabrication as projective defense — Eliphaz cannot bear the reality of innocent suffering, so he conjures guilt.
- **Critical (Clines, Newsom, Gutiérrez)**: The speech exposes the violence latent in retribution theology. When a framework cannot accommodate counter-evidence, it generates spurious evidence to preserve itself. Gutiérrez names this the ideology of the comfortable against the afflicted.

The least-intuitive position to steelman is the Orthodox/patristic: that the false accusation is providentially useful. In this view, Eliphaz's slander is the occasion of Job's deeper refinement, a component of the trial rather than an interruption of it. The discomfort of this position is that it risks sanctifying abusive speech. Its strength is that it refuses the modern therapeutic assumption that suffering must be shielded from misinterpretation to yield meaning; Job's greatness, on this reading, grows precisely because he must articulate truth against a sanctified lie.

### Tension 2: Does divine transcendence imply divine indifference?

Eliphaz's doxological detour (22:12-14) makes a subtle move. He describes God as "high in the heavens," walking on "the vault of heaven," veiled by "thick clouds." He attributes to Job the inference that God therefore cannot see. But Eliphaz himself, in asserting transcendence to rebuke Job, has come dangerously close to his own caricature. Does the theological affirmation of God's transcendence have built-in traction against claims of divine injustice, or does it in fact create the space for such claims?

- **Classical Theist (Thomas, Westminster Divines)**: Transcendence and providence are jointly affirmed; God's height is precisely the ground of his perfect knowledge, not its limit. No problem.
- **Open Theist (Pinnock, Boyd)**: Transcendence as classically framed has created the problem; God's relational presence, not metaphysical altitude, is the relevant attribute. Job 22 inadvertently exposes the weakness of a purely metaphysical doctrine of God.
- **Orthodox (Lossky, Zizioulas)**: The essence-energies distinction resolves the tension. God in his essence is transcendent, but in his energies is radically immanent. Eliphaz confuses the two and so gets both wrong.
- **Jewish (Heschel, Fackenheim)**: Heschel's divine pathos counters Eliphaz: God is not detached but affected, not distant but summoned. Job knows this intuitively; Eliphaz has lost it.
- **Liberation (Gutiérrez, Sobrino)**: Abstract transcendence serves the powerful by removing God from history; the concrete immanence of God in the suffering innocent refutes Eliphaz's altitude-theology.

Where this leaves us: Eliphaz's third speech is the moment when orthodox theology, deprived of counter-evidence that can re-enter its frame, begins to manufacture evidence. He is not demonic. He is the sincere theologian whose system has run out of answers and whose love of the system now outweighs his love of his friend. The disturbing question the chapter presses is whether the framework we hold most dear will, under sufficient cognitive pressure, produce in us the same invented charges against the afflicted — whether our theologies, too, become slanders when we can no longer bear their failure.

One unresolved question: if the book of Job is partly the exposure of pious wrongness, why does God in 42:7 tell Eliphaz "my wrath is kindled against you... for you have not spoken of me what is right, as my servant Job has" — and then require Job to pray for him? What kind of theology has to be pastorally mediated by the person it wounded in order to be forgiven?

## The Thread

- **Matthew 7:1-5** — "Judge not, that you be not judged... first take the log out of your own eye." Jesus's warning against the rhetorical posture Eliphaz assumes in 22:5-9.
- **James 3:1-12** — "Not many of you should become teachers... the tongue is a restless evil, full of deadly poison." James writes inside the Job-tradition of noting how the religious teacher's speech can kill.
- **Zechariah 3:1-5** — The *satan* standing to accuse Joshua the high priest. The accuser-figure Eliphaz has become here prefigures the courtroom accusations the book's prologue told us Job's real adversary was staging.
- **Romans 14:4** — "Who are you to pass judgment on the servant of another? It is before his own master that he stands or falls." Paul's refusal of the Eliphaz-posture inside the Christian community.
