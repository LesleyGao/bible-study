# 2 Corinthians 6 — Scholarly Analysis

## Lens 1: The Historical World Behind the Text

The Corinth from which Paul's correspondents read this letter was a Roman colony refounded by Caesar a century earlier, the seat of the proconsul of Achaia, and a city where status performance was, in Bruce Winter's phrase, the dominant social grammar. Theissen, Meeks, Murphy-O'Connor, and Dale Martin have together established that the Corinthian congregation was socially heterogeneous: an Erastus with a Latin-inscribed pavement (Romans 16:23), Gaius the householder, Stephanas's family, slaves, freedmen, women of independent means like Phoebe across the gulf at Cenchreae, Diaspora Jews, and former pagans who had grown up amid the temples of Apollo, Aphrodite, Asclepius, and the imperial cult. By the mid-50s, when Paul was writing 2 Corinthians from Macedonia after Titus's reassuring report, he had been in correspondence and conflict with this community for roughly five years. The painful visit, the tearful letter, and the rivals he calls "super-apostles" had pushed the relationship to the edge.

Chapter 6 reflects three hard local realities. The first is the social pressure on Christ-believers, especially Gentile converts, to keep participating in the civic and domestic religious life that defined Corinthian belonging. Bruce Winter's *After Paul Left Corinth* and *Seek the Welfare of the City* documents how thoroughly civic identity was bound up with sacrificial meals, guild banquets in temple precincts, dinner-party hospitality at which idol-meat circulated, and the liturgies of the imperial cult. Refusing to be "yoked" with unbelievers had concrete consequences for trade-guild membership, marriage prospects, family obligations, and political standing.

The second reality is the catalogue of Paul's own sufferings in 6:4–10, which is not rhetorical hyperbole. Acts 16 (Philippi), Acts 17 (Thessalonica, Berea), Acts 19 (Ephesus), and 2 Corinthians 11:23–28 corroborate beatings with rods (a magisterial Roman penalty), imprisonments, riots, hunger, and sleeplessness. The peristasis-catalogue genre — lists of hardships used by philosophers and statesmen to demonstrate virtue — was familiar from Stoic-Cynic diatribe (Epictetus, Seneca) and from earlier Greek funeral and triumphal rhetoric. John Fitzgerald's *Cracks in an Earthen Vessel* gathered the comparative material; Paul knows the genre and inverts it. Where the Stoic sage proves virtue by surviving hardship with detachment, Paul proves authentic apostleship by enduring hardship while remaining vulnerable, dependent, and visibly weak.

The third reality is the contested character of 6:14–7:1 itself. Whether Paul wrote this passage or whether a later editor inserted it remains, since Jerome Murphy-O'Connor's *Paul: A Critical Life* and his earlier 1988 *RB* article, an open scholarly question. The dualistic vocabulary — Beliar, lawlessness, light/darkness, temple/idols — has impressive parallels in Qumran sectarian literature, especially 4Q174 (the *Florilegium*), 1QM (the *War Scroll*), 1QS (the *Community Rule*), and 4Q177 (*Catena A*). The Beliar-figure, the binary lot of light vs. darkness, the temple-purity logic, and even the specific catena method of stitching scriptural fragments are at home in Qumran's Essene-adjacent worldview. Joseph Fitzmyer's classic 1961 essay argued that the passage was a Qumran-style Jewish-Christian composition Paul did not write. Carol Stockhausen, G. K. Beale, Linda Belleville, Witherington, and most defenders of Pauline authenticity respond that the catena method is consistent with Paul's other quotation practices (compare Romans 9–11) and that the rhetorical move fits the larger pastoral pleading of chapter 6.

Whichever way that question goes — and it is genuinely contested — the historical setting of the original Corinthian recipients makes the message intelligible. They lived in a city in which idolatry was not a private religious option but a social-political infrastructure. The call to "come out from their midst" (6:17) was not a withdrawal into a sect; it was a refusal of the patron-client dinner party at the Asclepion, the trade-guild meal in the temple of Apollo, and the imperial cult procession that defined what it meant to be Corinthian.

## Lens 2: The Literature of the Text

The chapter has three movements: the apostolic appeal of 6:1–10, the relational appeal of 6:11–13, and the disputed exhortation to separation in 6:14–7:1.

In 6:1–2 Paul cites Isaiah 49:8 LXX precisely. The double *idou nyn* ("behold, now") is Paul's interpretive expansion: the "favorable time" (*kairos dektos*) and the "day of salvation" (*hēmera sōtērias*) of Isaiah's Servant Song are now — eschatological time has folded into Corinthian time. Isaiah 49 is the Servant's commissioning speech to be "a light to the nations" (49:6); Paul's use of it positions his own apostolate as continuous with the Servant's, a move he had already made implicitly at 4:6 ("the light of the gospel of the glory of Christ").

The peristasis-catalogue of 6:4–10 is one of the most rhetorically elaborate passages in Paul. It has four sub-sections: nine hardships introduced by *en* (in afflictions, hardships, calamities, beatings, imprisonments, riots, labors, sleepless nights, hunger — 4–5); eight virtues introduced by *en* (purity, knowledge, patience, kindness, Holy Spirit, genuine love, truthful speech, power of God — 6–7a); a transition through the "weapons of righteousness for the right hand and for the left" (7b — *dia* construction); a triple antithetical refrain through *dia* (honor/dishonor, slander/praise — 8a); and the climactic *hōs ... kai* paradoxes of 8b–10. The seven-fold paradox structure of 8b–10 — *hōs planoi kai alētheis*, *hōs agnooumenoi kai epiginōskomenoi*, *hōs apothnēskontes kai idou zōmen*, *hōs paideuomenoi kai mē thanatoumenoi*, *hōs lypoumenoi aei de chairontes*, *hōs ptōchoi pollous de ploutizontes*, *hōs mēden echontes kai panta katechontes* — is the rhetorical climax of the apologetic arc that began at 2:14.

In 6:11–13 Paul shifts register entirely. The vocative "Corinthians" (*Korinthioi*) is intimate; he uses such direct address only here and in Galatians 3:1 ("foolish Galatians"). The metaphor *peplatyntai* ("our heart is wide") and the imperative *platynthēte kai hymeis* ("widen yourselves also") trade on a physical image of the heart as an opened, expansive interior space — Furnish notes the Septuagintal background (Ps 119:32 LXX). The aside "I speak as to children" reactivates the parental metaphor of 1 Corinthians 4:14–15.

Then comes the abrupt shift at 6:14. The participle *heterozygountes* — being unequally yoked, drawing on the agricultural prohibition of Deuteronomy 22:10 ("you shall not plow with an ox and a donkey together") and the broader Levitical kilayim (forbidden mixtures, Lev 19:19) — has no direct parallel in the rest of Paul. The five rhetorical questions of 6:14b–16a build a tight chiasm:
- partnership (*metochē*): righteousness vs. lawlessness
- fellowship (*koinōnia*): light vs. darkness
- accord (*symphōnēsis*): Christ vs. Beliar
- portion (*meris*): believer vs. unbeliever
- agreement (*synkatathesis*): temple vs. idols

Then the catena of scriptural quotations in 6:16b–18 stitches Leviticus 26:11–12, Ezekiel 37:27, Isaiah 52:11, Ezekiel 20:34, and 2 Samuel 7:14 into a single composite citation. The composite-quotation technique is well attested in Qumran's *pesher* and *florilegium* literature; whether it points away from Pauline authorship or simply confirms Paul's rabbinic training is the crux of the interpolation debate.

The hapax legomena pile up here. *Heterozygountes* (6:14), *symphōnēsis* (6:15), *Beliar* (6:15 — the only NT occurrence), *synkatathesis* (6:16), *molysmos* (7:1), and *epiteleō* used in this construction are all unusual. The seven hapax legomena in this short passage are the strongest single argument for the interpolation thesis on lexical grounds, though defenders point out that hapax legomena cluster wherever Paul reaches for vocabulary suited to a particular argument.

## Lens 3: The Larger Story

The Jewish background to 6:14–7:1 is dense and largely Qumran-shaped. The *yahad* community at Qumran organized itself around an absolute binary: the sons of light versus the sons of darkness, the lot of God versus the lot of Beliar. *1QS* III–IV (the "Two Spirits" treatise) describes the world as governed by the Prince of Light and the Angel of Darkness; *1QM* organizes the eschatological war between the two camps; *4Q174* (Florilegium) and *4Q177* (Catena A) stitch scriptural fragments — including 2 Samuel 7:14 — into a vision of the eschatological community as God's true temple. Daniel Boyarin and Eyal Regev have separately argued that Paul stands in an apocalyptic-Jewish stream that shares vocabulary and conceptual structure with Qumran without being identical to it. The temple-purity logic — that the believing community is itself the dwelling place of God and must therefore be kept holy — is also Qumranic (1QS VIII–IX explicitly identifies the *yahad* as the temple).

Older Jewish material reinforces the picture. Ezra 9–10 and Nehemiah 13:23–29 narrate the post-exilic crisis over intermarriage with foreign women, with Ezra forcing dissolutions of mixed marriages and Nehemiah physically tearing out beards. Maimonides codified the rabbinic prohibition in *Hilkhot Issurei Bi'ah*. Tobit 4:12 instructs the son to "marry a woman from the seed of your fathers." Jubilees 22:20 and 30:7–17 amplify the prohibition with apocalyptic intensity. The Holiness Code's *kilayim* prohibitions (Leviticus 19:19, Deuteronomy 22:9–11), of which "you shall not plow with an ox and a donkey together" is one, supply the metaphor of being yoked across kinds. Whether Paul's *heterozygountes* trades primarily on the marriage prohibition or on the broader purity logic is debated.

The Christian trajectory has applied 6:14 in remarkably different directions. Tertullian (*Ad Uxorem* 2.2–3) and Cyprian read the verse as a prohibition against Christian marriage to pagans. Augustine in *De Bono Coniugali* and elsewhere developed a more nuanced theology of mixed marriage that acknowledged the apostolic counsel of 1 Corinthians 7:12–14 (the unbelieving spouse may be sanctified by the believing one) while urging believers to seek Christian partners. The medieval canonical tradition codified the impediment of *disparitas cultus* — disparity of cult — as a diriment impediment to valid marriage, requiring dispensation. The 1983 Code of Canon Law (canon 1086, 1124–1129) and CCC 1633–1637 preserve the substance: a Catholic may marry a non-Christian only with explicit permission and with promises regarding the Catholic upbringing of children.

The Reformed tradition continued the marriage application but extended it. Calvin in his *Commentary on 2 Corinthians* applied the verse to "all unlawful associations" — friendships, business partnerships, alliances — that compromise the believer's primary loyalty. The Puritan Thomas Manton expanded the application to civic and political alliances. The Anabaptist tradition radicalized the principle: the *Schleitheim Confession* (1527), article 4, on separation, applied the unequal-yoke logic to participation in civic government, oath-taking, military service, and economic associations with the unredeemed world. Mennonite and Hutterite communities have lived this out as a form of social separation that can look from outside like withdrawal but from inside like alternative community.

The modern application questions are sharper. Carl F. H. Henry, R. C. Sproul, and the Reformed mainstream have generally extended the yoking metaphor to include marriage and business partnership while qualifying it for normal civic engagement. John MacArthur and conservative-evangelical pastoral counseling treat marriage to a non-Christian as the central application. Catholic teaching has gradually opened: the post-Vatican II treatment of mixed marriage in *Matrimonia Mixta* (Paul VI, 1970) softened the older near-prohibition. Lefebvrist and traditionalist Catholic communities resist that softening. On ecumenical relations, the question of whether 6:14 prohibits formal alliance among Christian traditions or refers only to alliance with unbelievers turns on what counts as "unbelief." The most cautious modern critical reading — Furnish, Thrall, Matera — restricts the verse's primary referent to participation in pagan idol-meals and temple practice, which was the live pastoral problem at Corinth (1 Cor 8–10). On that reading, broader applications are legitimate extensions but not the verse's first meaning.

The patristic engagement with 6:1–10 went elsewhere. Chrysostom's homilies on 2 Corinthians 12 and 13 dwell at length on the peristasis-catalogue, treating it as the model of pastoral suffering. Augustine cited 6:10 ("as having nothing yet possessing everything") in his correspondence on Christian poverty. The Reformation reformers, Calvin especially, used 6:1–10 as the definition of true ministry against what Calvin took to be the false ministry of the late-medieval prelate.

## Lens 4: Theological Intent and Tensions

Two tensions deserve direct treatment.

The first is whether 6:14–7:1 is Paul's. The interpolation thesis (Murphy-O'Connor, Fitzmyer, Joseph Gnilka, Hans Dieter Betz in part) rests on four claims: the seven hapax legomena and the cluster of rare vocabulary; the dualistic Beliar/light-darkness/temple-idol binary echoing Qumran 4Q174; the abrupt context disruption between 6:11–13 and 7:2–4 which both speak of opening hearts and pleading for affection; and what some interpolation-defenders read as a "let us cleanse ourselves" works-soteriology in 7:1 that sits uneasily with Paul's grace-driven theology elsewhere. The steelman version of the interpolation thesis is that an early Jewish-Christian editor with Qumran sympathies inserted a discrete homiletic unit between 6:13 and 7:2 because the sentiment seemed to fit Paul's temple-of-the-living-God language at 6:16 and his pastoral concern about idolatry. The defenders of authenticity (Stockhausen, Beale, Belleville, Witherington, Harris, Thrall in the end) respond that the catena method is consistent with Paul's other rabbinic-style proof-texting (Romans 9–11, Galatians 3), that the temple-of-God identification is a Pauline commonplace (1 Cor 3:16–17, 6:19), that the imperative "let us cleanse" matches Paul's parainetic style elsewhere (2 Cor 7:1 has a clear Pauline structural parallel in 1 Thess 4:1–8), and that the hapax legomena are unsurprising when Paul reaches for a particular pastoral-prophetic register. There is no manuscript evidence of any kind for an interpolation; *all* surviving witnesses including P46 contain the passage. On balance, the case for Pauline authorship remains stronger, but the question is genuinely live.

The second tension is what "do not be unequally yoked" means. Catholic teaching on disparity of cult (CCC 1633–1637) prohibits without dispensation a Catholic's marriage to a non-Christian; mixed-confession Christian marriage is permitted but discouraged. Reformed tradition has historically restricted the yoking metaphor to marriage and "intimate alliances" of a comparable kind, including business partnerships when one party's primary loyalty is incompatible with the other's. The Anabaptist Schleitheim tradition extended it to all alliances with the world-system, including military and civic ones, and lived this in disciplined community separation. The modern critical reading restricts the original referent to pagan idol-temple participation in mid-first-century Corinth and treats further applications as analogical extensions. The most defensible position recognizes that all four readings have legitimate footholds in the text but differ in scope. The verse's primary historical referent is the idol-temple problem at Corinth. The verse's natural canonical extension covers the marriage question (which is why 1 Cor 7:39 specifies marrying "in the Lord"). The verse's broader extension to business partnership and civic alliance is a defensible application requiring case-by-case wisdom rather than a blanket prohibition. The Anabaptist extreme — total separation from civic life — over-reads the verse but recovers something the Reformed and Catholic mainstreams sometimes lose, namely that the gospel is a public counter-formation, not merely a private interior reorientation.

The pastoral payoff is the bridge between 6:1–10 and 6:14–7:1. The apostolic ministry that has just been described as enduring beatings, imprisonments, hunger, and slander while remaining truthful, kind, and loving is the same ministry that now calls the Corinthians to refuse contaminating alliance. The connection is not legalism but eschatological coherence: a community that has been reconciled to God in Christ (5:18–21), that lives in the now of the day of salvation (6:2), and that participates in the ambassadorial commission (5:20) cannot consistently keep one foot in the temple of the living God and another at the table of demons (1 Cor 10:21). The "open heart" appeal of 6:11–13 and the "do not be unequally yoked" appeal of 6:14 are, on the strongest reading, the same appeal: be wholly Paul's people, because Paul's people are wholly God's.
