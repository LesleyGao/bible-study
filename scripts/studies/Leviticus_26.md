# Leviticus 26: Seven Times More

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

Leviticus 26 is the programmatic conclusion of the Holiness Code (Lev 17–26). In Knohl's reconstruction it is H's rhetorical crescendo — the oration that binds together the code's preceding legislation with the classic ancient Near Eastern treaty-ending pattern of blessings for fidelity and curses for violation. The chapter presupposes a covenant community about to enter the land, but its curses (vv. 14–39) are so specifically shaped to the experience of military defeat, siege, deportation, and exile that generations of critical scholars have detected *vaticinia ex eventu* — prophecy-after-the-event — shaped by the seventh- or sixth-century Assyrian and Babylonian catastrophes. The question of direction of dependence between Leviticus 26 and Deuteronomy 28 has defined the Hebrew Bible's source-critical conversation for a century.

The political-cultural backdrop is the ANE vassal-treaty tradition. The standard Hittite treaty of the fourteenth–thirteenth centuries BCE concludes with divine-witness lists, blessings for fidelity, and increasingly graphic curses for infidelity. George Mendenhall's 1954 article and Klaus Baltzer's *Covenant Formulary* opened this comparative field. The Neo-Assyrian vassal treaties — especially Esarhaddon's succession treaty of 672 BCE, recovered at Nimrud and published in full by Simo Parpola and Kazuko Watanabe — furnish extraordinarily close parallels to the curse rhetoric of Leviticus 26 and Deuteronomy 28. The Sefire Aramaic treaty inscriptions (8th c. BCE, probably between Mati'el of Arpad and Bar-ga'yah) include curse sections threatening agricultural sterility, cannibalism, and demographic collapse in language that reads as a near-direct parallel to Lev 26:26 and 26:29. Moshe Weinfeld's *Deuteronomy and the Deuteronomic School* (1972) argued that Deut 28's curses show direct literary dependence on Esarhaddon's succession treaty; Bernard Levinson and Jeffrey Stackert have extended this to Lev 26 with more qualification.

Material culture: Leviticus 26 imagines a settled agrarian society that knows iron-heaven drought ("your heavens like iron and your earth like bronze," v. 19), baking ovens in which ten women share one hearth (v. 26), high places (*bāmôt*, v. 30) and incense altars (*ḥammānîm*, v. 30), walled cities (v. 25), and the practice of deportation (v. 33). The image of "ten women in one oven" is not a metaphor; it reflects the concrete material conditions of siege scarcity attested at Lachish and other Judahite sites during the Assyrian and Babylonian campaigns, where bread-rationing by weight is archaeologically recoverable through burnt-grain stratigraphy. The reference to the land "enjoying its Sabbaths" (vv. 34–35, 43) is picked up explicitly by the Chronicler (2 Chr 36:21) to interpret the 586 BCE exile and Persian-period restoration as YHWH's making up the accumulated unkept sabbatical years.

Hebrew terms to track:

- **šebaʿ ʿal-ḥaṭṭōʾtêḵem** (שֶׁבַע עַל־חַטֹּאתֵיכֶם) — "sevenfold for your sins"; the refrain that structures the five escalating curse cycles (vv. 18, 21, 24, 28)
- **qerî** (קֶרִי) — "contrariness, hostility," the key term of vv. 21, 23, 24, 27, 28, 40, 41; a hapax-like usage outside this chapter, it designates Israel's and God's reciprocal walking-at-cross-purposes
- **ʾāšām** (אָשָׁם) — "make amends, guilt-offering," v. 41: the hoped-for moral reparation
- **šabbĕtôt** (שַׁבְּתוֹת) — "sabbaths," the plural used pointedly of the land's unkept rests (v. 35)
- **hitwaddû** (הִתְוַדּוּ) — "confess," v. 40: the hithpael of *yādâ*, the precise technical term for penitential confession that undergirds the Day of Atonement liturgy of Lev 16

Archaeological and textual anchors: 4QLevb preserves portions of the chapter; LXX shows some expansion and smoothing. The great parallel text for the curse rhetoric is Esarhaddon's succession treaty (VTE), where, for example, the curse "May Sin, brightness of heaven and earth, clothe you in leprosy" (§§ 419–421) sits alongside Lev 26's "wasting disease and fever that consume the eyes." The agricultural curses of VTE §§ 528–533 and the cannibalism curse of § 448–450 echo Lev 26:19–20 and 26:29 closely enough that direct or mediated literary contact is the best hypothesis.

Authorship and dating: Leviticus 26 is H. Knohl argues in *The Sanctuary of Silence* (1995) that H is late eighth-century, produced in response to the Assyrian crisis; Milgrom accepts a pre-exilic H with exilic additions (vv. 34–45 are commonly assigned to a somewhat later hand or to H's final redactor responding to 586 BCE). Wellhausen's post-exilic placement is now largely abandoned. Childs, in *Introduction to the Old Testament as Scripture*, sets the question aside in favor of canonical function.

## Lens 2 — The Literature

Leviticus 26 opens with a short abstract of the covenant's core (vv. 1–2 — no idols, keep sabbaths, reverence sanctuary) and then unfolds in three large movements: blessings for obedience (vv. 3–13), curses for disobedience in five escalating cycles (vv. 14–39), and an epilogue of hope conditioned on confession (vv. 40–45). A closing subscription (v. 46) seals the code.

The curse section's architecture is its defining literary feature. Five times the text raises the stakes with the refrain *šebaʿ ʿal-ḥaṭṭōʾtêḵem* — "seven times more for your sins" — at vv. 18, 21, 24, 28 (with v. 14 as the initial condition). Each cycle ratchets the punishment: disease and military defeat (vv. 16–17), drought (vv. 18–20), wild beasts (vv. 21–22), sword and plague (vv. 23–26), cannibalism and exile (vv. 27–39). The number seven pervades: seven-times-over for sin, in a code where the seventh day, seventh year, and seven-times-seventh year organize calendrical holiness (Lev 25). Baruch Levine reads the structure as a literary inversion of jubilee's ascending seven-cycles — where Lev 25 climbs toward liberty, Lev 26 ratchets toward desolation, and only the land's accumulated unkept sabbaths (the link to Lev 25:1–7) form the bridge between the two.

The chapter's rhetoric borrows the ANE treaty blessing-and-curse finale but inflects it theologically. Hittite and Neo-Assyrian vassal treaties invoke a pantheon of divine witnesses; Leviticus 26 knows only one, and that one is the suzerain himself. The "walking contrary" formula (*hāloḵ ʿimmāḵem bĕ-qerî*, vv. 21, 23, 24, 27, 28) casts the breakdown in reciprocal terms: Israel walks *qerî* against YHWH; YHWH walks *qerî* against Israel. Milgrom and Levine emphasize that *qerî* is not divine arbitrary violence but covenantal mirroring.

The blessings section (vv. 3–13) climaxes in the extraordinary v. 12: *wĕhithallaḵtî bĕtôḵăḵem wĕhāyîtî lāḵem lēʾlōhîm wĕʾattem tihyû-lî lĕʿām* — "I will walk in the midst of you, and I will be your God, and you shall be my people." The verb *hithallek* ("walk about, take strolls") is the same verb used of God in Eden (Gen 3:8) — H is proposing that covenant life restores the pre-fall intimacy of God's walking among his people. Paul cites this verse explicitly at 2 Corinthians 6:16 as the definitive description of the church as temple.

The hope-epilogue (vv. 40–45) is syntactically extraordinary. It does not say "if they confess, then I will restore," as if forgiveness were contingent on repentance as efficient cause; the Hebrew holds together the two movements — Israel's confession and God's remembering — as the single shape of covenant renewal. Ramban reads this as the Torah's deepest theological claim about repentance. Childs treats vv. 44–45 as the Old Testament's clearest affirmation that exile is not the end of the covenant.

Textual variants are modest. 4QLevb and related Qumran fragments substantially confirm the MT; LXX expands the curse section in a few places with repetitive intensifiers but preserves the structure.

On the Lev 26 / Deut 28 question: the older consensus (Wellhausen, Driver) had Deuteronomy 28 as the prior text and Leviticus 26 as its priestly imitation. Knohl and Milgrom reverse the dependency: H is the earlier text, and Deuteronomy 28 is the Deuteronomic expansion of H's curse rhetoric. Bernard Levinson argues for a more complex intertextuality where both texts draw independently on Neo-Assyrian treaty curse formulae. There is no settled answer, but the archaeological discovery of VTE's parallels has shifted weight toward direct ANE dependence for both biblical texts rather than either one deriving primarily from the other.

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

**Jewish interpretive trajectory.** The Second Temple period reads Leviticus 26 as the theology of the exile. 2 Chronicles 36:21 explicitly interprets the Babylonian exile through vv. 34–35: the land enjoys its sabbaths while the people are in Babylon. Daniel 9 builds on this reading with the seventy-year calculation. Philo's *De Praemiis et Poenis* ("On Rewards and Punishments") treats Lev 26 and Deut 28 together as the covenantal logic of history. Josephus in *Antiquities* 4.113–120 paraphrases the blessings and curses. The Qumran *Damascus Document* (CD 1.3–11) begins its reconstruction of Israel's history with the imagery of Lev 26 — God's face hidden because of unfaithfulness — and reads the Qumran community as the remnant that has confessed and humbled its uncircumcised heart (cf. v. 41).

Rabbinic interpretation foregrounds the chapter's theological gravity. The *tokhaḥah* ("rebuke") — the name by which tradition knows the curse section — is read in synagogue in an undertone, the reader rushing through the verses. Sifra Bechukotai provides a meticulous verse-by-verse tannaitic midrash. Bavli Megillah 31b discusses the reading practice; it is widely attested that the *tokhaḥah* is not split between readers and that some congregants avoid being called up for its aliyah. Rashi reads *qerî* as "by chance" — Israel's sin is to treat covenant faithfulness as random coincidence rather than divine providence — and thus YHWH's *qerî* mirrors back the same apparent randomness. Ibn Ezra reads *qerî* more neutrally as "contrariness." Ramban's commentary on Lev 26 is the most extensive in his entire Torah commentary; he reads the chapter as a prophetic outline of Israel's entire exilic history through the Second Temple destruction and beyond, finding in v. 44 the seed of messianic restoration. Maimonides treats the chapter's curses as pedagogical within his theology of repentance (*Hilchot Teshuvah*).

Modern and contemporary Jewish thought on Lev 26 turns inevitably toward theodicy after the Shoah. Abraham Joshua Heschel's *The Prophets* reads the chapter within prophetic pathos: divine suffering mirrors covenantal partnership. Jon Levenson's *Sinai and Zion* and *The Death and Resurrection of the Beloved Son* take vv. 40–45 as paradigmatic covenant-remembrance. The more radical responses: Richard Rubenstein's *After Auschwitz* (1966) argues that Leviticus 26's covenantal theology — sin leading to suffering, suffering disciplining the people — cannot be held after the murder of a million children, and Rubenstein renounces the covenantal frame. Emil Fackenheim's *To Mend the World* formulates the "614th commandment" — not to hand Hitler a posthumous victory by abandoning Judaism — and wrestles with how Leviticus 26's theology can be received while refusing any reading that rationalizes the death camps. Irving Greenberg's "Cloud of Smoke, Pillar of Fire" formulates his famous principle: "no statement about God may be made that would not be credible in the presence of burning children." Eliezer Berkovits in *Faith After the Holocaust* works through divine self-limitation (*hester panim*). None of these writers can take Leviticus 26 in a straightforward retributive reading; all are forced to re-read it as something other than causal theodicy.

**Christian interpretive trajectory.** Origen's *Homilies on Leviticus* 16 allegorizes the blessings spiritually while preserving their literal bite for historical Israel. Irenaeus (*Against Heresies* 4) reads the chapter as demonstrating that the same God rewards and disciplines — a polemic against Marcion's two-God separation. Augustine in *City of God* 17–18 incorporates Lev 26 into his theology of the two cities, reading the exilic curses as Israel's historical experience and the restoration as fulfilled in Christ. Chrysostom's homilies on Hebrews gesture frequently to Lev 26 to explain the OT covenant's curses; Gregory the Great treats the sevenfold discipline morally in his pastoral writings. Aquinas (*ST* I-II q. 105) discusses Lev 26 under the judicial-precepts rubric.

Luther's commentaries (particularly his lectures on Deuteronomy, which travel through Lev 26 as parallel material) stress the Augustinian reading: the law's curses expose sin and drive the hearer to Christ. Calvin's *Commentary on the Four Last Books* treats Lev 26 at length; his Reformed reading sharpens the federal-covenantal dimension: the chapter displays the covenant of works in its pedagogical function.

Critical scholarship since the nineteenth century has been consumed by the source-critical and comparative-ANE dimensions covered above. Key monographs: Milgrom's *Leviticus* 23–27 (AB, 2001); Knohl, *The Sanctuary of Silence* (1995); Hartley's WBC *Leviticus* (1992); Sklar's *Leviticus* (TOTC, 2014); Mark Rooker's NAC; R. W. L. Moberly's broader work on covenant; Jeffrey Stackert's *Rewriting the Torah*; Jacob Milgrom and Shalom Paul on the curse imagery.

Contemporary theological use is substantial. Walter Brueggemann's *The Prophetic Imagination* draws on Lev 26 for the prophetic logic of exile and restoration. N.T. Wright's *Paul and the Faithfulness of God* treats Lev 26 and Deut 28 as structuring the meta-narrative of Jewish exile-and-return theology that frames the New Testament. Richard Hays's *Echoes of Scripture in the Letters of Paul* finds multiple Pauline allusions to Lev 26. John Howard Yoder and Ched Myers engage the chapter for Christian political theology. Christopher J. H. Wright's *Old Testament Ethics for the People of God* works through the covenantal-ethical logic.

**Where they converge and diverge.** Both traditions recognize that Leviticus 26 supplies the grammar for understanding exile theologically — sin, judgment, memory, restoration. Both affirm the climactic note of v. 44–45: God will not finally break covenant. Where they diverge: Christian reception often collapses the chapter's specific historical trajectory into a generalized theology of sin and grace, with Christ's cross as the ultimate curse-bearing (Galatians 3:13 using Deut 21:23 in a structure that parallels Lev 26). Jewish reception, especially post-Shoah, resists such collapse; the curses have specific historical referents and cannot be harmonized into a universal theological scheme without betraying the bodies in whose name the text must be read. The dispute over *qerî* is emblematic: Christian readers tend to follow the Vulgate and LXX toward "opposition" or "anger"; Rashi's "by chance" preserves the moral-philosophical sharpness that treating providence as randomness is the specific sin.

## Lens 4 — The Theological Intent

**First tension: the theodicy of covenant curses.** Does Leviticus 26 prescribe, predict, or explain suffering? And if the latter, what do we do with suffering that exceeds anything the chapter's moral framework seems to accommodate?

- **Catholic:** The Catechism treats covenantal blessings and curses within the broader framework of divine pedagogy (*CCC* 708, 1963) and the ordinary providence by which God brings good out of evil. The reception of Lev 26 in Catholic teaching has been cautious; John Paul II's *Tertio Millennio Adveniente* and *We Remember: A Reflection on the Shoah* (1998) explicitly refused to read the Shoah as divine punishment, reflecting a theological recalibration after Vatican II and *Nostra Aetate*.
- **Reformed:** Classical federal theology (Calvin, the Westminster Standards) read Lev 26 as the covenant of works displayed pedagogically — blessing and curse structure covenantal history, though the covenant of grace in Christ absorbs the curse. Modern Reformed theologians (Michael Horton, *Introducing Covenant Theology*) continue this reading with more attention to the literary and canonical shape.
- **Lutheran:** Luther's law/gospel distinction keeps Lev 26's curses firmly on the law side — they convict and drive to Christ, but their pedagogical function is universal, not tied to specific historical events.
- **Anabaptist:** Yoder reads the chapter as part of the Old Testament's political theology of covenant — blessings and curses are the community's own shape, not an external divine interference in foreign politics. The community's faithfulness produces the fruit of faithfulness; its unfaithfulness produces the consequences internal to unfaithfulness.
- **Orthodox:** Schmemann and Zizioulas read the chapter liturgically — sabbath, sanctuary, and dwelling (v. 11, v. 12) are the horizon of communion, and the curses mark the phenomenology of communion refused.
- **Jewish (post-Shoah):** Rubenstein renounces covenant theology as morally impossible after the Shoah. Fackenheim wrestles harder: covenant must continue, but Leviticus 26 cannot be read as the causal frame. Greenberg refuses any theology not credible in the presence of burning children. Levenson and Sacks preserve covenant but insist that suffering on the Shoah's scale cannot be slotted into Lev 26's sevenfold-discipline schema; Heschel's divine pathos supplies an alternative grammar.
- **Critical:** Milgrom, Knohl, and Bergsma treat the chapter as ANE covenant-treaty rhetoric, historically conditioned, whose enduring theological value lies not in the causal mechanism (sin produces exile) but in the moral seriousness — the covenantal community is responsible for the world it inhabits.

The position least intuitive to modern sensibilities is the post-Shoah Jewish refusal of straightforward retributive theodicy (Rubenstein, Greenberg). The steelman: Leviticus 26 presupposes proportional divine response to moral failure. The Shoah is either (a) proportional — in which case the entire moral framework of covenant is unintelligible, because no proportionality can justify burning children — or (b) disproportional — in which case Leviticus 26 cannot be the operative interpretive frame. Greenberg's methodological rule is not a rejection of the text; it is a refusal to cheapen the text by using it to rationalize what it was never built to rationalize. This is a disciplined reader-response position that takes the text more seriously, not less.

**Where this leaves us:** Every major tradition holds that Lev 26 teaches the gravity of covenantal infidelity and the faithfulness of God who will not finally break covenant. Where they diverge is whether the chapter's causal schema — sin produces specific suffering — can bear the weight of history's actual catastrophes, or whether the text must be re-read so that its moral seriousness is preserved without instrumentalizing the dead.

**Second tension: nationalism and the universality of covenant.** Leviticus 26 is addressed specifically to Israel; its blessings and curses are territorial (the land), political (enemies and exile), and ethnic (the covenant with Jacob, Isaac, Abraham). Can the chapter be read in a way that is not ethnonational?

- **Catholic:** *Lumen Gentium* 16 and *Nostra Aetate* 4 affirm the continuing election of Israel while extending covenant structures analogically to the Church as the new people of God. Leviticus 26's land-theology is typological: the Church inherits the promises in Christ, but the literal Jewish people remain beloved.
- **Reformed:** Covenant theology reads Lev 26's promises as fulfilled in the new covenant, with the visible church inheriting the covenantal obligations. Christian Zionism (a specific dispensationalist reading) reverses this, reading the territorial promises as active and unfulfilled.
- **Jewish:** The chapter's ethnoterritorial specificity is not a problem to be solved; it is the covenantal form. Sacks's "dignity of difference" frames this as particularism that serves universal flourishing — Israel's particular covenant is the ground of its witness to the nations.
- **Anabaptist:** Yoder and Myers read the chapter within a political theology that refuses both Christian supersessionism and Christian nationalism. The curses apply to any community that breaks covenant; the blessings are the fruit of any community that keeps it.
- **Critical:** Historical-critical scholarship (Milgrom, Levine) emphasizes that the chapter participates in ANE ethnonational covenant rhetoric; what is distinctive is the monotheistic commitment, not the ethnic particularism.

**Where this leaves us:** The chapter's particularism is neither neutralized by universalizing moves nor defensible as simple ethnonationalism. Responsible reading holds the particularity (this is Israel's covenant with YHWH) together with the text's own universal horizon (the exodus as act "in the sight of the nations," v. 45).

**One unresolved question:** If Leviticus 26 is pre-exilic H rhetoric that uses Neo-Assyrian treaty curse imagery to warn Israel before the catastrophe, and if 2 Chronicles 36:21 reads the exile back through Lev 26:34–35, does the chapter function as prophecy fulfilled, prophecy self-fulfilling, or prophecy retroactively read onto an event whose actual cause was Babylonian imperial ambition?

## The Thread

Three non-obvious cross-references.

First: 2 Corinthians 6:16. Paul cites Lev 26:11–12 directly — "I will make my dwelling among them and walk among them, and I will be their God, and they shall be my people" — as the definitive description of the Corinthian church. The verb *hithallek* connects to Eden (Gen 3:8) and the tabernacle (2 Sam 7:6); Paul's move is to read Lev 26's blessing as realized in the new-covenant church as temple. This is one of the few places the New Testament quotes Leviticus verbatim.

Second: Daniel 9:11–14. Daniel's great confessional prayer during the Babylonian exile cites "the curse and oath that are written in the Law of Moses the servant of God" as fulfilled upon Jerusalem; the phrasing signals both Lev 26 and Deut 28. Daniel then calculates the seventy years of exile (9:2, from Jer 25:11) and proposes a further schema of seventy weeks (9:24–27). The jubilee-multiplied arithmetic of Daniel 9 is intelligible only through Lev 25–26's sevens; Qumran's 11QMelchizedek and the Book of Jubilees are direct inheritors.

Third: Ezra 9 and Nehemiah 9. Both prayers read the post-exilic restoration explicitly through Lev 26:40–45's logic of confession: the uncircumcised heart humbled, iniquity acknowledged, the covenant remembered. Nehemiah 9:32–37 is the clearest prose equivalent — a liturgical confession that enacts vv. 40–41 in narrative form. The post-exilic community self-consciously understood itself as the embodiment of Lev 26's hope-epilogue; it is the chapter's first historical attempt to live as the people who have confessed and returned.
