# Daniel 9: The Seventy Weeks and the Most Contested Calculation in the Bible

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

The chapter opens with a dating formula that has bedeviled commentators for centuries: "In the first year of Darius the son of Ahasuerus, by descent a Mede, who was made king over the realm of the Chaldeans." This is the famous *Darius the Mede* problem. No king by that name appears in any Persian, Babylonian, or Greek source covering the transition from Babylonian to Persian rule (539 BC). Cyrus the Great took Babylon directly; there is no intervening Median monarch. The proposals are well known. **D.J. Wiseman** suggested *Darius the Mede* is a throne name for Cyrus himself, who is described as "king of the Medes" in some Babylonian texts. Others identify him with **Gubaru** (or Ugbaru), the general who entered Babylon ahead of Cyrus and is recorded in the Nabonidus Chronicle. **John J. Collins**, in his Hermeneia commentary — the standard critical reference — concludes that Darius the Mede is best understood as a literary construct, a conflation of Median tradition with the schematic four-empire scheme of Daniel 2 and 7, where a Median empire must precede the Persian for the typology to work. **Carol Newsom** (OTL, 2014) reads it as the author's deliberate bending of historical sequence to fit theological architecture.

The chapter places Daniel reading **Jeremiah's letters** (Jer 25:11–12; 29:10) — the prophecy that the desolations of Jerusalem would be accomplished in seventy years. The reading is happening in roughly 539/538 BC, the moment when Cyrus was issuing the decree of return preserved in the **Cyrus Cylinder** (538 BC, British Museum, BM 90920) — the single most important archaeological witness to the Persian policy of repatriation that frames the whole post-exilic literature. Material culture: late Neo-Babylonian / early Persian Babylon, the diaspora communities along the Chebar canal, the Jewish scribal class with access to the Jeremiah scroll. The penitential prayer (9:3–19) reflects the Deuteronomistic confession-and-restoration formula visible in Nehemiah 9, Ezra 9, Baruch 1, and 1 Kings 8 — what **Mark Boda** has analyzed as the "penitential prayer tradition" of the Second Temple period.

The chapter's chronological horizon, however, points elsewhere. Verses 24–27 — the *seventy weeks* — describe in compressed form a sequence of events terminating in an *anointed one cut off* and an *abomination that makes desolate*. For the critical consensus (Collins, Newsom, **Maurice Casey** in *Son of Man*, **Lester Grabbe**, **Philip Davies**, **James Montgomery** in the ICC), this points unmistakably at the Antiochene crisis of 167–164 BC: Antiochus IV Epiphanes, the murdered high priest Onias III, the desecration of the Jerusalem temple by the altar to Zeus Olympios (1 Maccabees 1:54), and the rededication under Judas Maccabeus three years later. The whole chapter, on this reading, is a Maccabean-era composition reusing a 6th-century setting to address a 2nd-century crisis. **Porphyry of Tyre** made this argument first in the 3rd century AD; we have it preserved by **Jerome's *Commentary on Daniel* (407)**, written explicitly *contra Porphyry*. Conservative scholars (**Stephen Miller** in NAC, **Joyce Baldwin** in TOTC, 1978, **Gleason Archer**) defend the chapter as 6th-century predictive prophecy.

Key Hebrew terms: *shavu'im shiv'im* (שָׁבֻעִים שִׁבְעִים, "seventy sevens" or "seventy weeks," 9:24); *mashiach nagid* (מָשִׁיחַ נָגִיד, "an anointed one, a prince," 9:25); *yikkaret* (יִכָּרֵת, "shall be cut off," 9:26); *shiqquts meshomem* (שִׁקּוּץ מְשׁוֹמֵם, "abomination that makes desolate," 9:27, picked up at 11:31 and 12:11). The Hebrew of chapter 9 — like all of chapters 8–12 — contrasts with the Aramaic block of 2:4b–7:28; the bilingual seam is one of the strongest internal-structural facts of the book and a key piece of evidence in the dating debate. The Old Greek (LXX) and Theodotion versions of Daniel diverge sharply, and at 9:24–27 the Greek translators clearly struggled with the Hebrew arithmetic — itself evidence that the calculation was already opaque to ancient readers. Eight Daniel manuscripts survive at Qumran (1QDan-a, 1QDan-b, 4QDan-a through 4QDan-e, 6QDan), the earliest dated to roughly 125 BC — strikingly close to the proposed Maccabean composition date if the critical consensus is right.

## Lens 2 — The Literature

The chapter splits cleanly into two literary forms. Verses 3–19 are a **penitential prayer** — one of the most beautiful pieces of OT prose, structured as a Deuteronomistic confession. Its closest parallels are Nehemiah 9, Ezra 9:6–15, Baruch 1:15–3:8, and Solomon's prayer in 1 Kings 8. The form is fixed: invocation of God's covenant character, confession in the first-person plural, recitation of the curses written in the Law of Moses, plea for mercy on the basis of God's name and reputation rather than the people's righteousness. Some critics (Montgomery, Collins) argue that the prayer is a pre-existing liturgical composition the author of Daniel inserted into the chapter; others (Newsom, Baldwin) argue for compositional unity. Either way, the prayer's theology is profoundly traditional — its reference to "the curse and oath that are written in the Law of Moses" (9:11) is a direct allusion to Deuteronomy 28–30.

Verses 20–27 then shift into **apocalyptic chronological revelation** — Gabriel's interpretation given to Daniel "at the time of the evening sacrifice" (a striking detail, since by 9:21 the temple had been destroyed for nearly fifty years and there was no evening sacrifice; the phrase is a temporal marker in the diaspora consciousness). The genre here is what Collins identifies in his *Apocalyptic Imagination* as **chronological apocalyptic** — the use of schematized periodization to structure history toward a divinely decreed end. The seventy-weeks scheme reuses Jeremiah's seventy years through what scholars call "**actualizing exegesis**" or *pesher*-style reinterpretation — the technique perfected at Qumran and visible throughout 1 Enoch's *Apocalypse of Weeks* and the *Book of Jubilees*. **Klaus Koch**'s influential work on Daniel's apocalyptic genre traces this *Geschichtsentwurf* — schematized history-design — to broader Near Eastern wisdom traditions but argues that Daniel 9's tight numerical compression is distinctively Jewish.

The Hebrew of 9:24–27 is famously difficult. The phrase *shavu'im shiv'im* is grammatically masculine plural (literally "seventy sevens"), not feminine *shavu'ot* (the standard word for seven-day weeks). This is the textual ground for reading the *sevens* as units of years rather than days — a *shemittah*-cycle reading that connects the seventy weeks to the Jubilee theology of Leviticus 25 and the Sabbatical-year reckoning of 2 Chronicles 36:21. **Andrew Steinmann** and **Roger Beckwith** have argued the years should be reckoned in 360-day prophetic units; others insist on plain solar years. The breakdown — 7 + 62 + 1 — itself suggests three distinct historical phases. The masoretic accentuation places the major break after the seventh week (so that the sixty-two weeks begin a new period), while the Greek versions and most modern Christian translations push the break to after week sixty-nine — a single seemingly minor punctuation choice that radically changes how the *anointed one* is identified. Newsom flags this as one of the most consequential textual variants in the Hebrew Bible.

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

The chapter sits at the deepest stratum of post-exilic theological reflection: how to read prophecy that has not been straightforwardly fulfilled. **Jeremiah's seventy years** (Jer 25:11–12; 29:10) had pointed to the duration of the Babylonian exile. The numbers don't quite work historically — Jerusalem fell in 587 BC and the first returns happened in 538 BC, a span of about 49 years; or one can count from the first deportation in 605 to the rebuilding of the temple in 516 BC, which gets closer to 70. But by Daniel 9's vantage, the *desolations of Jerusalem* are still ongoing in some sense, and Gabriel's interpretation re-reads Jeremiah's seventy *years* as seventy *weeks of years* — 490 years — pushing the horizon of fulfillment far beyond the Persian return. This is the moment when Israelite prophecy becomes self-consciously interpretive: a prophecy is read by a later generation as still requiring fulfillment, and the angel's word *expands* rather than contracts the timeline. **Michael Fishbane**'s *Biblical Interpretation in Ancient Israel* identifies this as the seedbed of all subsequent Jewish exegesis.

**Jewish Trajectory**: The Talmudic tractate **Sanhedrin 97b** computes various messianic timetables from Daniel 9 and concludes that the calculations have failed but the messianic age remains a hope. **Saadia Gaon** (10th c.) developed an explicit chronological reading anchored on the destruction of the Second Temple. **Rashi** read the *anointed one cut off* as Agrippa II, the last of the Herodian rulers; **Ibn Ezra** as a high priest; **Abrabanel** in *Mayanei ha-Yeshua* (15th c., Italy) wrote at length specifically *to refute* the Christological reading and propose a Jewish messianic reading anchored at the destruction of AD 70 with a still-future consummation. Modern Jewish scholarship: **Robert Gordis**, **Daniel Boyarin** (*The Jewish Gospels*) treats Daniel 9's *anointed one* as one of the proto-messianic categories that 1st-century Christianity inherited from Second Temple Judaism rather than invented; **Aaron Koller** has worked on the chapter's place in the post-exilic chronological imagination.

**Christian Trajectory**: **Hippolytus of Rome's *Commentary on Daniel*** (c. 200) — the earliest extant Christian biblical commentary of any kind — applied the seventy weeks to Christ's first advent and to a still-future Antichrist, distributing the 70th week between the two. **Jerome's *Commentary on Daniel*** (407) was written explicitly to refute Porphyry; Jerome works through the chronology systematically and acknowledges the difficulty of fitting the numbers, ultimately landing on a Christological reading anchored on the death of Christ. **Theodoret of Cyrus** read it Christologically. **Aquinas** treated Daniel 9 in scattered references. **Joachim of Fiore** (12th c.) folded the chapter into his three-ages eschatological scheme, an intervention that shaped Reformation chiliasm. **Luther's *Preface to Daniel*** (1530) emphasizes the Christological reading. **Calvin's *Lectures on Daniel*** (1561, two volumes) — perhaps the most rigorous pre-critical Christian engagement — defends a Christological-historical reading and rejects the "gap" reading entirely. Critical: **S.R. Driver**, **James Montgomery** (ICC), **H.H. Rowley**, and the contemporary canon — Collins, Newsom, Casey, Grabbe — are unanimous on the Maccabean reading. Conservative: Miller, Baldwin, Archer, **Edward Young**, **Gerhard Pfandl**, **Robert Gurney**.

The dispensational reading that dominates Anglo-American evangelicalism — **J.N. Darby**'s 19th-century innovation, popularized through **C.I. Scofield**'s *Reference Bible* (1909) — inserts a *gap* of indefinite length (now over 2,000 years) between the 69th and 70th weeks. The 69 weeks ran to Christ's first advent; the 70th week is a still-future seven-year Tribulation initiated by a peace covenant with Israel and culminating in a future *abomination of desolation* by an end-time Antichrist. **Hal Lindsey's *Late Great Planet Earth*** (1970) and the **Tim LaHaye / Jerry Jenkins *Left Behind* series** (1995–2007) translate this reading into popular eschatology that has shaped how millions of evangelical Christians read current events. Whatever one thinks of the textual basis for the gap, its cultural footprint is enormous.

**Where they diverge**: The seventy-weeks calculation is the single most contested chronological prophecy in the Bible. The Christological reading (Hippolytus, Jerome, Calvin) reads the 69 weeks as running from a Persian decree to Christ's death and the 70th week as Christ's ministry, with the temple's AD 70 destruction as the *desolation*. The Maccabean reading (Collins, Newsom, Casey) reads 7 + 62 + 1 = 490 years from Jeremiah's vantage to the desecration in 167 BC and rededication in 164 BC, with the *anointed one cut off* identified as Onias III (murdered 171 BC) and the *prince to come* as Antiochus IV. The dispensational reading (Darby, Scofield) inserts a multi-millennial gap. The modern Jewish messianic reading (Saadia, Abrabanel) maintains a still-future fulfillment.

## Lens 4 — The Theological Intent

**Tension 1: The seventy weeks calculation.** What is at stake in this question is not a small numerical quibble but the whole architecture of how scripture relates to history. The Christological reading turns on identifying *the anointed one cut off* (9:26) with Christ — and the early Christian tradition unanimously did so. The numbers, on standard Christological calculations starting from Artaxerxes' decree of 458 or 444 BC, can be made to land remarkably close to AD 27–30, the traditional dating of Christ's ministry and crucifixion. Calvin defended this calculation against medieval Jewish counter-readings; modern conservative scholarship (Miller, Pentecost, Walvoord) defends versions of it. The Maccabean reading (Collins, Newsom, Casey, almost universal in critical scholarship) reads the *anointed one* as Onias III, the murdered high priest of 171 BC whose death Josephus narrates (*Ant.* 12.5.1), and identifies the *prince to come* as Antiochus IV. The numbers fit the Maccabean horizon imperfectly but more naturally than they fit the Christ horizon. Steelman the Maccabean position: the entirety of chapter 11 is built around the Antiochene crisis with extraordinary precision; chapter 8's vision of the "little horn" is universally recognized as Antiochus; the *abomination that makes desolate* of 9:27 is the same phrase used in 11:31 of Antiochus's altar to Zeus, where the historical referent is undeniable. Steelman the Christological position: the *anointed one* is the natural reading of *mashiach* in any messianic context; the 69-week calculation does land near the period of Christ; 1st-century Jews (including Josephus, *Ant.* 10.11.7) read Daniel as messianic. Steelman the dispensational position: the temple's destruction by "the people of the prince who is to come" (9:26) most naturally points to the Roman destruction of AD 70, and the Roman emperor / future Antichrist typology offers a coherent reading of the prince as both Titus and a future eschatological figure. Steelman the Jewish messianic position: the chapter's hope-formula in 9:24 ("to finish transgression, to put an end to sin, to bring in everlasting righteousness") has not yet been visibly accomplished in the world; faithful Jewish reading must hold the prophecy open. *Where this leaves us:* the textual evidence underdetermines the calculation. Every reading requires interpretive moves that go beyond the words. What you bring to the text shapes what you find.

**Tension 2: The penitential prayer and the question of *who is righteous*.** Daniel's prayer in 9:3–19 confesses *we have sinned* — the first-person plural is unflinching. Daniel includes himself, the most pious figure in the entire OT narrative, among those whose sins have brought the calamity. The Catholic tradition reads this as exemplary corporate solidarity in sin, the basis for confession and the Sacrament of Reconciliation. The Reformed tradition (Calvin) reads it as exemplary covenant solidarity that nevertheless points to the inadequacy of human righteousness — Daniel pleads not on the basis of *our* righteousness but on the basis of God's *great mercy* (9:18). The Orthodox tradition reads the prayer as the model of *metanoia*, repentance as turning rather than as juridical confession. The Jewish reading (Abrabanel, modern liturgy) embraces the prayer's corporate-covenant logic: Israel's exile is not arbitrary; it is the consequence of covenant breach, and restoration awaits both divine mercy and Israel's *teshuvah*. The critical reading (Collins, Newsom) places the prayer in the Deuteronomistic theological tradition that underpins the post-exilic worldview. *Where this leaves us:* the prayer is one of the most theologically substantive passages in the OT, and its underlying logic — that mercy is not deserved but freely given — is the seedbed of both Pauline grace theology and rabbinic *teshuvah*.

**The unresolved question:** If the seventy-weeks calculation has resisted every honest attempt at consensus over two thousand years of careful interpretation — Christological, Maccabean, dispensational, Jewish-messianic, all defended by serious readers — what does the persistence of the riddle tell us about scripture's relation to history? The text seems to invite calculation while resisting closure. Is this a feature or a failure?

## The Thread

Cross-references that move beyond the obvious:

**Jer 25:11–12 / 29:10 → Dan 9:2** — the seventy years explicitly cited; the foundational instance of biblical prophecy being re-read as still requiring fulfillment. **2 Chronicles 36:21** — the same seventy years interpreted via the Sabbatical-year theology of Leviticus 25–26.

**Mark 13:14 / Matthew 24:15** — Jesus citing "the abomination that makes desolate, spoken of by the prophet Daniel." This single citation makes Daniel's chapter 9 (and 11–12) one of the most consequential OT references in the Synoptic apocalyptic discourse. The reference is doubly contested: did Jesus point forward to AD 70, to a still-future tribulation, or both?

**Nehemiah 9 / Ezra 9 / Baruch 1:15–3:8** — the parallel penitential prayers of the Second Temple period, against which Daniel 9's prayer is best read. The form is so consistent that some scholars (Mark Boda) speak of a single penitential-prayer tradition.

**Hebrews 9:26** — "He has appeared once for all at the end of the ages to put away sin by the sacrifice of himself." The verbal echoes of Daniel 9:24 ("to put an end to sin, and to atone for iniquity") are not accidental; the author of Hebrews is reading Daniel 9 as Christologically fulfilled.
