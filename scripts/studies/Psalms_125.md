# Psalm 125 — Going Deeper

## Lens 1: The Historical World Behind the Text

Psalm 125 is the sixth in the **Songs of Ascents** (*Shir HaMa'alot*, שִׁיר הַמַּעֲלוֹת) — the fifteen-psalm pilgrim collection (Pss 120–134) within Book V (Pss 107–150) of the Psalter. The superscription is the bare *shir ha-ma'alot*, with no Davidic, Solomonic, or other attribution. The Songs of Ascents were most plausibly used by pilgrims ascending to Jerusalem for the three Deuteronomic festivals (Passover, Pentecost/Shavuot, Tabernacles/Sukkot, per Deut 16:16); *Mishnah Sukkah* 5:4 preserves the tradition of Levites singing them on the **fifteen semicircular steps** that led from the women's court to the men's court (Court of Israel) in the Second Temple — one psalm per step. Sigmund Mowinckel's cult-functional reading places the Songs of Ascents in the actual liturgy of Israel's festival calendar.

The psalm's content — a meditation on the security of those who trust in the LORD, framed by Zion's geography — fits the broader **post-exilic** Sitz-im-Leben of the collection. The reference in v. 3 to "the scepter of wickedness" (*shevet ha-resha*) resting on "the land allotted to the righteous" (*goral ha-tzaddiqim*) suggests a context of foreign domination over the land — most plausibly the Persian or early Hellenistic period. The Hebrew vocabulary and grammatical features (the late particle *she-* in v. 5; the wisdom-saturated diction) are consistent with Persian-period composition. **Hossfeld–Zenger** (Hermeneia commentary), **Kraus** (BKAT), and **Goldingay** all read the psalm as post-exilic.

Gunkel's form-critical genre is **psalm of trust** (*Vertrauenspsalm*), with elements of **wisdom psalm** (*Weisheitspsalm*) and a closing **liturgical blessing**. The psalm has the typical psalm-of-trust structure: an opening confession of confidence (vv. 1–2), a theological assertion about the moral order (v. 3), a petition (v. 4), a contrast with the wicked (v. 5a), and a final blessing (v. 5b). Some scholars (notably Westermann) classify it as a hybrid; the wisdom features (the contrast between the righteous and the crooked, the proverb-like assertions) place it near the psalms of moral order (Pss 1, 37, 73).

The opening simile is the psalm's signature image: *ha-botchim ba-Yahweh ke-har-Tziyon lo-yimmot le-olam yeshev* (הַבֹּטְחִים בַּיהוָה כְּהַר־צִיּוֹן לֹא־יִמּוֹט לְעוֹלָם יֵשֵׁב, "those who trust in the LORD are like Mount Zion, which cannot be moved, but abides forever," v. 1). The verb *batach* (בָּטַח, to trust, rely upon) is the standard biblical verb for confident reliance — its noun form *bitachon* has become the technical Hebrew term for trust in God. The participle *ha-botchim* (those who trust) names the category that the simile describes. The verb *yimmot* (to be shaken, totter, slip) is the negation of stability — Zion does not totter (cf. Pss 46:6; 93:1; 96:10 — the king on the throne is not shaken).

The geographical claim is theologically rich. Zion (Hebrew *Tziyon*, צִיּוֹן) is the southeastern hill of Jerusalem — originally the Jebusite stronghold David captured (2 Sam 5:7), later the site of the Temple (the name extended to the Temple Mount and ultimately to the entire city). Mount Zion is not, geographically speaking, the highest mountain in Judea or even in Jerusalem — Mount of Olives is taller, and the Mount of Hermon to the north dwarfs them all. But theologically, Zion is *the cosmic center*, the navel of the world (Ezek 38:12), the place where heaven and earth meet (Ps 132:13–14 — the LORD has chosen Zion as his dwelling). The psalm's claim that those who trust in the LORD are *like Mount Zion* is therefore not a claim about physical immovability but about cosmic-theological stability — the trusting are part of the same divine architecture as the Temple Mount.

Verse 2 develops the geographical theology: *Yerushalayim harim saviv lah ve-Yahweh saviv le-ammo me-attah ve-ad-olam* (יְרוּשָׁלִַם הָרִים סָבִיב לָהּ וַיהוָה סָבִיב לְעַמּוֹ מֵעַתָּה וְעַד־עוֹלָם, "as the mountains surround Jerusalem, so the LORD surrounds his people, from this time forth and forevermore"). This is **literally true geography**: Jerusalem sits in a topographical bowl, surrounded on north (Mount Scopus), east (Mount of Olives), south (Hill of Evil Counsel), and west (the western hills) by higher elevations. A pilgrim approaching the city sees the encircling ridges before seeing the city itself. The psalm takes that physical fact and theologizes it: the encircling mountains are a visible parable of the encompassing presence of God around his people.

Verse 3 introduces the pressing problem: *ki lo yanuach shevet ha-resha al goral ha-tzaddiqim lema'an lo-yishlechu ha-tzaddiqim be-avlatah yedeihem* (כִּי לֹא יָנוּחַ שֵׁבֶט הָרֶשַׁע עַל גּוֹרַל הַצַּדִּיקִים לְמַעַן לֹא־יִשְׁלְחוּ הַצַּדִּיקִים בְּעַוְלָתָה יְדֵיהֶם, "for the scepter of wickedness shall not rest on the land allotted to the righteous, lest the righteous stretch out their hands to do wrong"). The Hebrew *shevet* (שֵׁבֶט) is a tribal staff or royal scepter (cf. Gen 49:10's "the scepter shall not depart from Judah"); the *shevet ha-resha* names the rule of wickedness, most likely foreign domination over the post-exilic land. The verb *yanuach* (rest, settle) is the same root as *menuchah* (rest, dwelling place); the wickedness is being denied permanent settlement. *Goral* (גּוֹרָל) means lot, allotted portion — the land of Israel was *given by lot* in the Joshua tradition (Josh 14–19). The land assigned to the righteous shall not be permanently held by the wicked.

The closing v. 5 contains the famous benedictory phrase *shalom al-Yisrael* (שָׁלוֹם עַל־יִשְׂרָאֵל, "peace be upon Israel"). The same blessing closes Ps 128:6 — the only two occurrences in the Psalter. The phrase has become a fixed liturgical formula in Jewish worship.

The Septuagint numbers Ps 125 as Ps 124 (LXX one behind MT), and the **Dead Sea Scrolls** (11Q5) preserve the psalm with text aligned to the MT.

## Lens 2: The Literature Itself

The psalm divides into **three strophes**: confidence (vv. 1–2), the theological problem (v. 3), and petition with contrast and blessing (vv. 4–5). The strophic shape moves from settled trust through acknowledged tension to the prayed-for resolution.

Robert Lowth's parallelism is intricate. **Synonymous parallelism** with simile structures vv. 1–2: "those who trust... like Mount Zion" / "as the mountains surround... so the LORD surrounds." The double simile builds a single theological argument by stacking geographical-political analogies. **Synthetic parallelism** governs v. 3, where the second line ("lest the righteous stretch out their hands to do wrong") gives the *purpose* — the moral-formational reason — for the assertion in the first line. **Antithetic parallelism** structures the petition-and-contrast of vv. 4–5: "Do good, O LORD, to those who are good and to those who are upright in their hearts / But those who turn aside to their crooked ways the LORD will lead away with evildoers." The contrast between *tovim* (good) / *yesharim* (upright in heart) and *ha-mattim aqalqalotam* (those who turn aside to their crooked ways) frames the psalm's moral universe.

The **wordplay** in v. 5 is deft. The participle *ha-mattim* (the turners-aside) shares the *n-t-h* root with the noun *mattaim* (the inclined ones); the noun *aqalqalotam* (crookednesses) is built on the root *q-l-l* (which can mean both "to twist, be crooked" and, in another pointing, "to be light, contemptible"). The phrase *aqalqalot* appears elsewhere in Judges 5:6 (the "winding paths" of the days of Shamgar) and Isa 42:16 (the "crooked places" the LORD will make straight). The phrase names not just moral failure but moral *deviation* — the turning of a road from straight to twisty.

The psalm's **chiastic-like envelope** opens with Mount Zion (v. 1), develops the Jerusalem-mountain-encirclement (v. 2), names the threat (v. 3), petitions for the good (v. 4), warns of the crooked (v. 5a), and closes with the *shalom al-Yisrael* benediction (v. 5b). The geographical-mountain language at the start finds its political-moral counterpart at the end: the people surrounded by mountains and the people surrounded by *shalom* are the same people.

The **temporal phrase** *me-attah ve-ad-olam* (מֵעַתָּה וְעַד־עוֹלָם, "from this time forth and forevermore") at v. 2 is a recurring Songs of Ascents formula (cf. Pss 113:2; 115:18; 121:8; 131:3). The phrase places the theological claim outside any specific historical settlement — the LORD's surrounding presence is not limited to a particular regime, government, or political condition.

## Lens 3: The Larger Story (Jewish and Christian Trajectories)

Within Book V's editorial sequence, Ps 125 follows Ps 124's communal thanksgiving with a meditation on the conditions of ongoing trust. Ps 124 looked back at past deliverance; Ps 125 looks at present security under continuing pressure. The post-exilic community has escaped Babylon, returned to Yehud, but lives under foreign administration — Persian or Hellenistic. The *shevet ha-resha* of v. 3 names that present condition, and the psalm's confidence is precisely *not* the confidence that the foreign rule will end immediately, but the confidence that it will not last forever and that those who trust in the LORD are stable amid it.

In **Jewish reception**, Ps 125 has been treasured as a foundational text on *bitachon* (trust). **Rashi** (11th c.) reads the Mount Zion simile as an analogy: just as Zion's foundation is divinely established and cannot be uprooted, so the trusting souls are anchored in God. **Ibn Ezra** emphasizes the geographical realism — anyone who has stood in Jerusalem knows the mountains around it — and treats the simile as drawn from observable nature. **Radak** (David Kimchi) develops the moral-political reading of v. 3: foreign domination tempts the righteous to compromise (lest they "stretch out their hands to do wrong"), and the psalm prays for the deliverance that prevents that compromise. **Midrash Tehillim** on Ps 125 multiplies the imagery — Zion as foundation, mountains as protection, scepter as rule — and develops the moral typology of the *yesharim* (upright). The phrase *shalom al-Yisrael* has become a standard Jewish liturgical benediction, particularly in the *Birkat Kohanim* and at the close of the *Amidah*. Modern Jewish commentators — **Buber**, **Heschel**, **Levenson**, **Sarna** — situate the psalm within the theology of the *land-promise* and the *covenantal endurance*. Levenson's work on the relationship between Sinai and Zion is particularly relevant: Ps 125 holds Zion not as a territorial claim alone but as the visible center of covenantal life.

In **Christian reception**, Ps 125 has been a constant resource for pastoral teaching on faith and stability. **Augustine**'s *Enarrationes* on Ps 124 (Latin numbering) reads Mount Zion allegorically as the church, the city of God whose foundations are eschatologically secure even as the historical church passes through tribulation. **Cassiodorus** systematizes the allegorical reading. **Aquinas** treats the simile as a model of the theological virtue of hope. **Luther**'s expositions on the Songs of Ascents take Ps 125 as a foundation-text for Reformation pastoral theology — the trusting soul is anchored not in church-political stability but in God himself; the foreign-rule motif of v. 3 mapped onto the Reformation's experience of the church under various political pressures. **Calvin**'s 1557 commentary develops the psalm as a teaching on perseverance: the wicked rule will not rest forever on the heritage of the righteous, but the interim requires the *shalom-al-Yisrael* trust. The psalm has been set to music in every Christian tradition; the Genevan Psalter's setting traveled across Reformed Europe. Modern critical scholarship — **Hermann Gunkel**, **Sigmund Mowinckel**, **Claus Westermann**, **Hans-Joachim Kraus** (BKAT), **Frank-Lothar Hossfeld** and **Erich Zenger** (Hermeneia), **John Goldingay** (Baker), **Gordon Wenham** (the Psalter's shape), **Walter Brueggemann** (the trust-in-tension), **Patrick Miller**, **William Brown**, and **Beth Tanner** — converges on the psalm's status as a wisdom-tinged trust psalm anchored in Zion theology. **Eugene Peterson**'s *A Long Obedience in the Same Direction* devotes a chapter to Ps 125 under the title "Security."

The **New Testament** does not directly cite Ps 125, but its theology of Zion-stability and divine surrounding informs Hebrews 12:22–28 (the Christian community has come to Mount Zion, the heavenly Jerusalem, the unshakable kingdom); 1 Peter 1:5 (those guarded by God's power); the eschatological vision of Rev 21–22 (the New Jerusalem secure forever). The phrase *shalom al-Yisrael* echoes through Paul's blessings on the Israel of God (Gal 6:16) and through the church's continuing prayer for Israel's peace.

## Lens 4: The Theological Intent

**Tension 1 — How does Mount Zion's symbolic geography work for readers far from Jerusalem and far from a Davidic kingdom?** Ps 125 grounds the trust-claim in a specific geographical-political fact: Mount Zion in the city of Jerusalem in the kingdom of Judah/Yehud. For readers across two and a half millennia of diaspora and dispersion, including most contemporary Christian and Jewish readers, that geography is not a daily reality. **Three positions** stand in the literature. **(1) The literal-historical reading** (continued in much rabbinic and orthodox Jewish tradition, and in Catholic and Orthodox Christian pilgrimage piety): the Mount Zion of the psalm is the actual hill, and contemporary readers participate in its theology by maintaining the tradition of facing Jerusalem in prayer (the Jewish *kavanah* toward Zion), pilgriming there when possible, and praying for its peace (cf. Ps 122:6). **(2) The eschatological-typological reading** (Augustine, much patristic tradition, modern Reformed): the Mount Zion of the psalm points forward to the heavenly Jerusalem of Heb 12 and Rev 21; the present church is *en route to* that mountain, and the psalm's stability-claim is true at the eschatological level. **(3) The translational-symbolic reading** (Brueggemann, Goldingay, Peterson): the Mount Zion image works as a symbol of *whatever-is-stable-in-God* and can be meditated upon by readers wherever they are; the simile's force does not require a literal pilgrimage but a real trust. *Steelman for each:* The literal reading honors the specificity of the biblical text and the continuing reality of Jerusalem as a place. The eschatological reading honors the trajectory of biblical theology toward final consummation. The translational reading honors the psalm's portability — its survival in Jewish and Christian liturgy across every continent. *Where this leaves us:* The Mount Zion image is at once concrete (a real hill) and theologically expansive (the place where heaven and earth meet, the navel of the world); it does not work only in one register. Readers who pray Ps 125 are praying with a real mountain in mind, but the mountain's meaning is *cosmic-theological* and therefore available wherever the trusting soul is.

**Tension 2 — What does *shalom al-Yisrael* (peace upon Israel) include and exclude?** The closing benediction has had different readings across communities. In **Jewish liturgical tradition**, the phrase blesses the ethnic-covenantal Israel — the people descended from Jacob and gathered around the covenant. In **Christian tradition**, the phrase has been variously read: **(1) The replacement reading** (much patristic tradition): the church is the new Israel, and *shalom al-Yisrael* is now spoken over the believing community of all nations. **(2) The supersessionist-minus reading** (Reformation Lutheran and Reformed traditions, more nuanced): the church participates in Israel's blessings without displacing ethnic Israel. **(3) The two-covenant reading** (some 20th-century Christian thought, especially post-Holocaust): Israel and the church each have their own legitimate covenantal status, and the *shalom* prayer applies to both. **(4) The Pauline reading** (Rom 9–11; Gal 6:16): there is a single Israel of God whose ultimate composition includes both ethnic-Israelite faithful and grafted-in Gentile believers; *shalom al-Yisrael* prays for that whole. *Steelman for each:* The replacement reading honors the NT's claim that Christ has fulfilled the law and that the church is the body of Christ. The supersessionist-minus reading honors the continuing election of Israel (Rom 11:28–29). The two-covenant reading honors the post-Holocaust ethical urgency. The Pauline reading honors the apostle's own exegesis. *Where this leaves us:* The phrase *shalom al-Yisrael* prays for the peace of the people God has covenanted with — a people whose definition Christian and Jewish traditions read differently, but whose existence neither denies. To pray Ps 125 is to pray peace upon a people that is at once a real ethnic-covenantal community and a theological category open to grafting and expansion. The prayer does not have to be settled in its ecclesiology to be sincere in its peace.

**Where this leaves us:** Psalm 125 names the security of those who trust in the LORD by analogy to the most stable feature of Israelite geography — Mount Zion and the encircling mountains of Jerusalem. The Songs of Ascents pilgrim has confessed exile (Ps 120), traveled the road (Ps 121), arrived at the gates (Ps 122), turned eyes upward (Ps 123), rehearsed past rescue (Ps 124), and now sits on the steps and *looks at the mountains*. The mountains around Jerusalem are visible from anywhere in the city; the psalm takes the visible fact and theologizes it: as the mountains *physically* surround Jerusalem, so the LORD *theologically* surrounds his people.

The wickedness has not gone away — the *shevet ha-resha* still presses on the land. But the psalm makes a quiet promise: that pressure will not rest permanently, and the trusting are not destabilized by it. The petition for the good and the warning against the crooked at vv. 4–5 hold the moral universe in tension; the closing *shalom al-Yisrael* refuses to let the warning be the last word. Peace is the benediction.

The psalm leaves us with a question shaped by the contrast between visible threat and invisible encirclement: *what mountains do you need to see today, and what encirclement can you trust under?*

What in your life feels like a *shevet ha-resha* — a wickedness pressing on what is meant for the righteous — that you need to bring under the *shalom al-Yisrael* this week?
