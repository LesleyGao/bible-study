# Hosea 2: Ishi, Not Baali — The Wilderness Wooing

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

Chapter 2 is the marriage metaphor at full burn. The chapter is structured as a divine *rib* — a covenant lawsuit — in which YHWH is plaintiff, Israel is defendant, and the children are summoned as witnesses against their mother. The legal-ritual form is documented across the eighth-century prophets: Isaiah 1:2-20, Micah 6:1-8, Amos 4:1-13 use the same genre. Behind the *rib* lies the ancient Near Eastern treaty tradition: the suzerain-vassal treaties of the Hittite empire (preserved in cuneiform tablets from Boğazköy, c. 1400-1200 BCE) and the Neo-Assyrian *adê* treaties (the Esarhaddon Succession Treaty, found at Tell Tayinat in 2009, dated 672 BCE, reconstructed by Lauinger and others) follow a stable pattern: historical prologue, stipulations, witness invocation, blessings, curses. The eighth-century Hebrew prophets read Israel's covenant in this idiom, and the *rib* is the lawsuit form that activates the curses. Hosea 2's accusation, sentence, and remarkable reversal are the courtroom drama of YHWH's marriage to Israel.

The historical background is the syncretism that pervaded the northern kingdom. *Ba'al* (בַּעַל, "lord, master, husband, owner") was the name of the Canaanite storm-god documented in the Ugaritic texts (recovered from Ras Shamra, 1929 onwards, by Claude Schaeffer). The Baal Cycle (KTU 1.1-1.6, the longest mythological text from Ugarit) presents Baal as the rain-and-fertility god whose annual death and revival explained the agricultural cycle: dry summer was Baal's death at the hands of Mot ("Death"); the autumn rains were his return. The Baal religion's economic logic was direct: if you wanted grain, wine, and oil, you propitiated the storm god who controlled the rain. The northern kingdom's farmers, descendants of Israelites who had entered Canaan over centuries and intermarried with the indigenous population, kept Yahweh as the national god of war and exodus *and* hedged their agricultural bets with Baal. Verse 8's bitterness — "she did not know that it was I who gave her the grain, the wine, and the oil" — names the cognitive error: Israel attributed agricultural fertility to Baal when the LORD had been the giver. Verse 16's "you will call me 'My Husband' (*ishi*) and no longer 'My Baal' (*ba'ali*)" is a wordplay on the dual meaning of *ba'al* (which can mean either "husband" or "Baal-the-god"); the play forces the audience to confront the linguistic slippage that had enabled the religious slippage.

Material culture saturates the lawsuit. Verses 5 and 9's catalog — bread, water, wool, flax, oil, wine, drink — is the standard list of agricultural goods that constituted the eighth-century Israelite economy: cereals (wheat and barley), wine from terraced vineyards (the agricultural revolution of the Iron Age IIA expansion documented by Lawrence Stager and others), olive oil from pressed olives, wool from sheep, flax for linen. The Samaria Ostraca (sixty-three inscribed potsherds recovered by George Reisner's Harvard expedition in 1908-1910, dated to the early eighth century) record exactly these goods being delivered to the Samarian royal house: "wine of Kerem-Yehoali," "fine oil of Yashar," "ten jars of wine," "ten jars of oil." The chapter's economic specificity reflects the actual agricultural inventory of the kingdom Hosea was addressing.

Verses 11's "her feasts, her new moons, her Sabbaths" name the Israelite festival calendar: the three pilgrimage feasts (Pesach/Unleavened Bread, Shavuot/Weeks, Sukkot/Booths), the monthly new-moon observance (1 Samuel 20:5), and the weekly Sabbath. In the northern kingdom these were celebrated at the rival sanctuaries Jeroboam I had established at Bethel and Dan (1 Kings 12:28-33), with calf images that the Deuteronomistic historian regards as idolatrous and that Hosea will denounce in chapters 8 and 13. The chapter's threat to "put an end" to these feasts is the threat that the northern kingdom's entire ritual infrastructure will be dismantled.

Verse 15's "Valley of Achor" (*Emek Akhor*, עֵמֶק עָכוֹר), "valley of trouble," refers to the place where Achan was stoned in Joshua 7:24-26 after his theft of the devoted things from Jericho. The valley is associated with covenant violation and judgment. Hosea's promise to make the Valley of Achor a "door of hope" (*petach tikvah*, פֶּתַח תִּקְוָה) is the radical reversal: the place of judgment becomes the entrance to renewal. Modern Israel adopted *Petah Tikvah* as the name of the city founded in 1878 — the first modern Jewish agricultural settlement in Ottoman Palestine — explicitly drawing on this verse.

Five Hebrew terms dominate the chapter. *Rib* (רִיב, v. 2), "lawsuit / dispute," is the genre-naming verb: "plead with your mother, plead." The lawsuit is the covenant's mechanism for naming and adjudicating violation. *Patah* (פָּתָה, v. 14), "allure / entice / persuade," is the verb of seductive speech — used of Delilah's manipulation of Samson (Judges 16:5), of false prophets (1 Kings 22:20-22), and now of YHWH's seduction of Israel back into the wilderness. The verb's slightly transgressive coloring is part of the verse's force. *Madbar* (מִדְבָּר, v. 14), "wilderness," is the place of Israel's first encounter with YHWH (Exodus 19, Deuteronomy 1-2); to bring her back to the wilderness is to restart the relationship at its foundational scene. *Aras* (אָרַשׂ, vv. 19-20), "betroth," is the technical legal term for the first stage of marriage in Israelite law (Deuteronomy 20:7, 22:23-29), distinct from full marriage (*nasa* or *laqach*). The threefold "I will betroth you" — in righteousness/justice, in *hesed*/mercy, in faithfulness — is the chapter's most theologically loaded series, recited daily by observant Jewish men when wrapping the leather *tefillin* straps around the fingers (the practice originating in the Geonic period and codified in the *Shulchan Aruch*). *Yada'* (יָדַע, v. 20), "know," is one of Hosea's signature verbs — used elsewhere of sexual knowledge (Genesis 4:1) and of covenant knowledge of YHWH; the chapter ends with the promise that the betrothed will *know* the LORD.

## Lens 2 — The Literature

Hosea 2 is among the most carefully constructed chapters in the prophetic corpus. The chapter is structured as a chiasm or, more loosely, as a sequence: accusation (vv. 2-13), turning (vv. 14-15), restoration (vv. 16-23). The accusation's catalog of judgments is matched, item by item, by the restoration's catalog of reversals. Where verse 9 said "I will take back my grain," verse 22 says "the earth shall answer the grain." Where verse 11 said "I will put an end to all her mirth," verse 15 says "she shall answer as in the days of her youth." Where verse 13 said "she... forgot me," verses 19-20 say "I will betroth you to me in faithfulness." The literary structure performs the theological argument: the same God who accuses is the God who restores.

The chapter's central genre is the *rib* (covenant lawsuit), but it incorporates several sub-genres. Verses 2-13 are accusation in mixed prose-poetry. Verses 14-15 are a wilderness-wooing poem with strong narrative-romantic coloring. Verses 16-20 are the betrothal oracle in elevated prose. Verses 21-23 are the answering-oracle, structured as a chain of agricultural responses culminating in the renaming of the children. The chapter's shifts of genre track its shifts of mood: from forensic accusation to seductive promise to formal betrothal to creation's chorus.

The voice structure is unusual. Verses 1-2 begin with the children addressed as audience ("Plead with your mother"). Verses 3-13 are God speaking about Israel in third person ("she," "her") through the legal complaint. Verse 14 opens "Therefore, behold, I will allure her" — God still speaks of Israel in third person but to an unspecified audience. Verses 16-20 shift to second person address: "you will call me 'My Husband.'" The shift from third person to second person is the chapter's grammatical pivot — the lawsuit ends, the betrothal begins, and the addressee changes from witnesses-against-Israel to Israel herself.

The wordplay on *ba'al* in verse 16 is one of the most elegant Hebrew puns in the prophets. The Hebrew word *ba'al* operates on three registers simultaneously: (1) the common noun "husband, master" (used in Genesis 20:3 of Abraham as Sarah's *ba'al*); (2) the proper name of the Canaanite storm god; (3) the verbal root "to possess, to be lord over." Israel had been calling YHWH *ba'ali* — meaning, in the Hebrew lexicon, "my husband" — but the same word also meant "my Baal." The slippage in language enabled the slippage in worship. Verse 16's reversal — call me *ishi* (my man / my husband) instead — is a linguistic exorcism: removing the contaminated word from the marriage.

The covenant with creation in verses 18-23 is a striking expansion of the betrothal. The covenant is made not only with Israel but "with the beasts of the field, the birds of the heavens, and the creeping things of the ground" (v. 18). The threefold zoological catalog echoes the Genesis 1:24-26 creation account — the chapter is treating the betrothal as a re-creation. Verses 21-23's chain of responses — the LORD answers the heavens, the heavens answer the earth, the earth answers the grain, the grain answers Jezreel — is a poetic image of the cosmic order restored in the marriage's wake. Brevard Childs reads this as the chapter's eschatological vision: the renewed marriage between God and Israel reverberates through all of creation.

The Hebrew text of Hosea 2 is significantly more stable than that of later chapters. The Septuagint generally tracks the Masoretic Text, with some divergences in vocabulary. The chapter's textual difficulties are localized — verse 6's "thorns" / "wall" sequence, verse 16's vocative pattern — but do not affect the chapter's structural argument. Dead Sea Scroll fragments (4QXIIc, 4QXIId) preserve portions of chapter 2 close to the MT.

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

Hosea 2 lives at the canon's most theologically generative metaphor — God as husband, Israel as wife — and at its most ethically contested.

### Jewish Interpretive Trajectory

Targum Jonathan to Hosea 2 expands the chapter into explicitly historical-political language. The "lovers" of v. 5 are identified as Egypt and Assyria; the wilderness wooing of v. 14 is the future restoration after exile; the betrothal of v. 19 is the messianic age. The Aramaic paraphrase has demystified the marriage metaphor and made it a chronicle of Israel's political history with YHWH.

Eikhah Rabbah and Pesikta de-Rav Kahana cite Hosea 2:14 ("I will allure her, and bring her into the wilderness") as a foundational text for Jewish exilic theology. The wilderness is not punishment but pedagogy; the LORD's withdrawal from Israel into the wilderness is preparation for renewed encounter. The theology of *galut* (exile) as theological discipline rather than mere punishment owes much to this verse.

Rashi (11th c.) reads the chapter as direct prophecy of the events of Hosea's own time — the Assyrian destruction (vv. 9-13) followed by the eventual restoration (vv. 14-23). For Rashi, the betrothal of v. 19 still awaits messianic fulfillment. Ibn Ezra is more philological, attentive to the Hebrew puns. Radak (David Kimchi, 12th-13th c.) develops the *ba'al/ish* wordplay extensively: the chapter's most important theological work is in v. 16's relinquishment of *ba'al* even as a husband-word, because language carries theological residue.

The threefold "I will betroth you" of vv. 19-20 became liturgically central. The Geonim (early medieval Babylonian Jewish authorities, c. 7th-11th c.) instituted the practice of reciting these verses while wrapping the *tefillin* (phylactery) strap around the middle finger each weekday morning. The wrapping is performed three times — once for each "betroth" — and the verses are recited in the order: "I will betroth you to me forever; and I will betroth you to me in righteousness, and in justice, and in steadfast love, and in mercy; and I will betroth you to me in faithfulness, and you shall know the LORD." The daily liturgical embodiment of Hosea 2:19-20 makes this verse possibly the most-recited prophetic text in observant Jewish life.

Modern Jewish scholarship has wrestled with the marriage metaphor's gender politics. Marvin Sweeney (*The Twelve Prophets*, Berit Olam) defends the metaphor's appropriateness in its eighth-century context while acknowledging its contemporary difficulties. Edward Greenstein and others have explored the *patah* (entice) language of v. 14 as the chapter's most disturbing-and-redemptive moment: God uses seductive speech, the verb of false prophets and Delilah, to bring Israel back. The verb's transgressive coloring is the point.

### Christian Interpretive Trajectory

Patristic readings developed the marriage metaphor Christologically. Origen's *Commentary on Hosea* (largely lost) read the chapter's betrothal as the church's marriage to Christ. Cyril of Alexandria, Theodore of Mopsuestia, and Jerome all develop similar readings. The motif of the church as bride of Christ (cf. Ephesians 5:22-33, Revelation 19:7, 21:9) drew theological power from Hosea 2's marriage metaphor.

Augustine (*City of God* 18.28) treats the chapter as prophecy of Christ's relationship with the church, with strong supersessionist overtones: the Synagogue is the unfaithful wife, the Church is the renewed bride. Bernard of Clairvaux's eighty-six sermons on the Song of Songs (1135-1153) draw repeatedly on Hosea 2's wilderness wooing and betrothal language to develop a theology of mystical union: the soul as the bride brought into the wilderness, allured by divine speech, betrothed in *hesed*. Bernard's spiritual erotic reading would shape medieval and counter-Reformation Catholic mysticism (Teresa of Ávila, John of the Cross).

Calvin's *Commentary on the Twelve Minor Prophets* gives Hosea 2 an extensive reading. Calvin treats the betrothal as fulfilled provisionally in the New Testament's gathering of the church and ultimately in the eschatological wedding feast. He is unembarrassed by the marriage metaphor's gendered structure; his reading treats the chapter as theological doctrine in poetic form. Luther's reading is similar but emphasizes the chapter's law-gospel dialectic: vv. 2-13 are the law's accusation, vv. 14-23 are the gospel's restoration.

Modern Christian commentary has been forced to engage the feminist critique of the marriage metaphor. Renita Weems's *Battered Love* (1995), Phyllis Trible's *Texts of Terror* (1984), Yvonne Sherwood's *The Prostitute and the Prophet* (1996), and Gale Yee's *Poor Banished Children of Eve* (2003) all argue that the chapter's metaphor — even in its restoration phase — codes female sexuality as the locus of communal sin and male-divine forgiveness as the agent of restoration. The stripping imagery of v. 3 ("I will strip her naked... and kill her with thirst") cannot be read past; the threats of public shaming and violence are part of what the chapter says. Christian commentary that ignores this critique has been increasingly judged inadequate (J. Andrew Dearman's NICOT commentary, John Goldingay's volumes on the Twelve, Marvin Sweeney's work all engage the critique substantively).

The covenant with creation in vv. 18-23 has had a remarkable late-twentieth-century reception in ecological theology. Walter Brueggemann's *Theology of the Old Testament* and *The Land* (1977), Norman Habel's *Earth Bible* project, and Ellen Davis's *Scripture, Culture, and Agriculture* (2009) all read the chapter's threefold covenant — with humans, animals, creation — as a foundational text for Christian ecological responsibility. The chapter does not separate human sin from creation's fate; the marriage's restoration is the cosmos's restoration.

### Where the Traditions Converge and Diverge

Both traditions take Hosea 2 as the central biblical articulation of God's marriage to his people. Both develop liturgical and devotional practices around the betrothal verses. Both have been forced by feminist critique to reread the metaphor's gendered structure. Both find in the cosmic-covenant ending (vv. 18-23) resources for ecological and creation theology.

They diverge on the betrothal's referent. Jewish tradition holds the betrothal as still-pending — a messianic-eschatological reality that will be realized when *teshuvah* is complete. Christian tradition has read the betrothal as already-realized in Christ's relationship with the church, with eschatological completion still ahead. The difference shapes how each tradition prays the verses: Jews recite them daily as anticipation; Christians read them as both already and not-yet.

## Lens 4 — The Theological Intent

The author's vision is the simultaneity of severity and seduction. The chapter does not soften the accusation; the stripping, the wall, the destruction of vines and feasts are real threats. But the chapter does not let the threats stand alone; the same chapter that threatens stripping is the chapter that promises wilderness wooing. The theological argument is that the threats and the seduction come from the same speaker.

Two genuine tensions emerge.

**Tension 1: Is the marriage metaphor redeemable?**

- *Catholic* (CCC 1611-1612, on marriage as covenant; *Mulieris Dignitatem* 1988): The metaphor is theologically fundamental but must be read with awareness of its eighth-century gender assumptions. The Church's nuptial theology (Christ-Church marriage) draws on Hosea 2 while interpreting the bride symbolically — the Church is the people, not specifically female persons; the feminine grammar is theological not anthropological.
- *Reformed* (Calvin, Bavinck, contemporary Tim Keller): The metaphor is the Bible's central image of God-people relationship and is non-negotiable. Critique should be directed at misuse, not at the metaphor itself.
- *Lutheran*: The marriage metaphor and the law-gospel dialectic are mutually reinforcing. Hosea 2 is one of Luther's most-cited prophetic texts.
- *Jewish (Sweeney, Greenstein, contemporary)*: The metaphor is part of the canonical heritage and must be read in its eighth-century context, with care not to import its gendered assumptions into contemporary halakhah or ethics. The threefold betrothal liturgy adapts the metaphor for the male-centered observer who recites it daily.
- *Feminist (Weems, Trible, Sherwood, Yee)*: The metaphor is structurally sexist — it codes female sexuality as the locus of sin, requires female stripping for male restoration, and uses male violence as the means of forgiveness. It cannot be redeemed by reversal because even the reversal keeps women as objects of male action. Christian and Jewish use of the chapter must acknowledge what the metaphor does.
- *Liberation / postcolonial (Sugirtharajah, Mandolfo)*: The metaphor risks legitimizing patriarchal violence (Weems's term: "Battered Love"). The chapter must be read with care that the divine bridegroom is not used as theological cover for human husbands.

**Steelmanning the position I find least intuitive**: the feminist critique. The temptation for the conventional reader is to say "but the metaphor is metaphorical — it's about God and the people, not about actual men and actual women." Weems and Trible's response is rigorous: metaphors *do work*. They shape what readers come to expect about God, about gender, about how relationships of power and forgiveness operate. The metaphor of God as patient husband enduring an unfaithful wife and bringing her back through stripping and shaming has, in fact, been used to underwrite human husbands' patience with "unfaithful" wives. The chapter's reception history includes this misuse. The feminist critique does not require abandoning the chapter; it requires reading the chapter with the misuse in view.

**Where this leaves us**: The chapter's metaphor cannot be either uncritically embraced or simply dismissed. It must be read with awareness of what it has done in history and of the divine pedagogy it imperfectly encodes.

**Tension 2: The wilderness as place of restoration.**

Verse 14: "I will allure her, and bring her into the wilderness, and speak tenderly to her." The wilderness — the place of testing, of want, of forty years of wandering — is here the place of restoration.

- *Catholic* (CCC 538-540, on Christ's wilderness temptation): The wilderness is the place where false dependencies are stripped and the soul is brought to its dependence on God. Hosea 2:14's wilderness is the spiritual desert that mystical theology has cultivated as the place of meeting.
- *Reformed*: The wilderness is where Israel's idolatries are stripped; the LORD provides for the people what Baal could not, in the place where Baal cannot reach. The theology is one of negative discipline — the wilderness as removal of competition.
- *Anabaptist / Mennonite*: The wilderness is where institutional religion is stripped; the renewed community emerges from the wilderness without the Temple, monarchy, or settled state.
- *Jewish (Pesikta de-Rav Kahana, Maharal, Hasidic readings)*: The wilderness is the foundational scene of Israel's encounter with YHWH. To return to the wilderness is to return to Sinai. The verse is reread every year on the Sabbath after Tisha B'Av: the destruction is the wilderness Israel must traverse to renewal.
- *Critical*: The wilderness motif is the eighth-century prophet's appeal to the founding myth of Israel's covenant — a myth that is theologically powerful but archaeologically contested. Whatever the historical wilderness was, the prophet uses it as the imaginative space of beginning again.

**Where this leaves us**: The chapter forbids the assumption that restoration happens in the place of comfort. The wilderness — material, social, spiritual — is the necessary terrain of return.

The unresolved question the author leaves: *What does it mean that God uses the verb of false prophets and Delilah — patah, to seduce — when seducing Israel back?* The chapter does not soften the verb. Whatever divine seduction is, it carries the moral risk of all seduction: that one cannot be wholly sure, in the moment, whether one is being saved or being deceived. The chapter trusts that the difference is real but does not pretend it is obvious.

## The Thread

- **Joshua 7:24-26** — The Valley of Achor where Achan was stoned for the theft of devoted things. Hosea 2:15 makes that valley a "door of hope." The reversal only registers if the reader knows the original story. Read together to feel how a place of judgment becomes a threshold of return.
- **Exodus 19-20 (Sinai)** — The wilderness where YHWH and Israel first met. Hosea 2:14-15's promise to bring Israel "into the wilderness" and have her "answer as in the days of her youth" is a return to Sinai's foundational scene. The chapter treats Sinai as the prototype of every future renewal.
- **Romans 9:25-26 / 1 Peter 2:10** — Paul and Peter quote Hosea 2:23 ("I will say to Not My People, 'You are my people'") as the inclusion of the Gentiles. The hermeneutical extension is significant: the verse originally promised reversal of judgment for Israel; Paul reads it as an opening for those who had never been Israel.
- **Revelation 19:7-9; 21:2-9** — "The marriage of the Lamb has come, and his Bride has made herself ready... I saw the holy city, new Jerusalem, coming down... prepared as a bride adorned for her husband." Hosea 2's betrothal is one of Revelation 19-21's most direct sources. When you reach the New Jerusalem's descent in Revelation 21, remember Hosea 2's threefold "I will betroth you" — the canonical thread runs from the unfaithful wife of Hosea to the adorned Bride of Revelation.
