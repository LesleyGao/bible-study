# Psalm 82 — Going Deeper

## Lens 1 — The Historical World Behind the Text

Psalm 82 is short — eight verses — but it is one of the most theologically dense and interpretively contested poems in the Hebrew Bible. The superscription is a bare *mizmor le-Asaph* (מִזְמוֹר לְאָסָף, "a psalm of Asaph"), placing it within the Asaphite cluster (Pss 73-83) in Book III of the Psalter, in the heart of the **Elohistic Psalter** (Pss 42-83), where the divine name *Elohim* dominates over *YHWH*. The Asaphites were Levitical chief musicians (1 Chr 16:4-7), and Psalm 82 carries their hallmark concern with cosmic justice and the relationship between divine kingship and human governance. The psalm is the next-to-last in the Asaphite collection, and its themes culminate at the end of the Elohistic Psalter itself — the divine name *Elohim* literally dominates the poem, appearing six times in eight verses.

Hermann Gunkel classified Psalm 82 as a **prophetic-judgment hymn** with elements of **divine-council vision**. The genre is unusual in the Psalter: most psalms address God; this psalm dramatizes God *speaking*, with the human poet narrating a heavenly courtroom scene. Sigmund Mowinckel placed the psalm in cult-prophetic liturgy — the kind of poetic vision that a temple prophet might speak at festival. Cyrus Gordon, Frank Moore Cross, and Mark S. Smith located the psalm in the broader ANE divine-council tradition, where the chief deity (*El* in Ugaritic, *Anu* in Mesopotamian, *YHWH* in Israelite) presides over a council of subordinate divine beings. Hossfeld-Zenger, Tate, and Goldingay all read the psalm as theologically and historically a window onto a moment in Israel's religious development when the language of divine plurality had not yet been fully resolved into the strict monotheism of later periods.

Dating is contested. The psalm's vocabulary — divine council, *bnei elyon* ("sons of the Most High"), the chief deity judging among lesser deities — has clear Late Bronze Age Canaanite parallels (the Ugaritic texts from Ras Shamra describe *El* presiding over the *bn-ilm*, "sons of the gods"). Some scholars therefore date the psalm to Israel's early monarchy or even earlier; others read it as deliberately archaizing language used in a later period to make a polemical point against pagan worship. The mainstream critical view (Mark S. Smith, Frank Moore Cross, Patrick Miller) places the psalm somewhere in the late monarchy, when Israel's *monolatry* (worship of one god alongside the assumption that other gods exist) was being intensified into the strict *monotheism* (only one god exists at all) that the post-exilic period would crystallize. Mark S. Smith's *The Early History of God* and *The Origins of Biblical Monotheism* argue that this shift was a centuries-long process and that Psalm 82 is a key textual witness to it. Michael Heiser's *The Unseen Realm* argues for an even earlier strata of belief that he calls the **divine council worldview**, in which Israel's God presides over real lesser divine beings who function as patrons of the nations.

Hebrew terms are loaded throughout. The opening line — *Elohim nitzav ba-adat-El be-qerev Elohim yishpot* (אֱלֹהִים נִצָּב בַּעֲדַת־אֵל בְּקֶרֶב אֱלֹהִים יִשְׁפֹּט, 82:1) — uses *Elohim* three times in a single verse with distinct referents: the first *Elohim* is Israel's God who stands; *adat-El* is "the council of *El*" (using the older Canaanite divine name *El*, not *YHWH*); and the second plural *Elohim* refers to the council members among whom God judges. The mixing of *Elohim* and *El* is striking and theologically charged. The verse's verbal structure is precise: God *stands* (*nitzav*, the posture of a prosecutor or judge taking his place) in the council and *holds judgment* (*yishpot*) among the assembled members. The crisis line at 82:6-7 — *ani amarti elohim atem u-vnei elyon kullkhem aken ke-adam temutun u-ke-achad ha-sarim tipolu* (אֲנִי אָמַרְתִּי אֱלֹהִים אַתֶּם וּבְנֵי עֶלְיוֹן כֻּלְּכֶם אָכֵן כְּאָדָם תְּמוּתוּן וּכְאַחַד הַשָּׂרִים תִּפֹּלוּ, "I said, 'You are gods, sons of the Most High, all of you; nevertheless, like men you shall die, and fall like any prince'") — uses both *Elohim* and *bnei Elyon*, the latter a clear divine-council technical term. The closing call — *qumah Elohim shofetah ha-aretz* (קוּמָה אֱלֹהִים שָׁפְטָה הָאָרֶץ, 82:8, "arise, O God, judge the earth") — anticipates the final cosmic judgment in which the council itself will be reformed.

A single *selah* punctuates the psalm at 82:2, after God's accusing question — a dramatic pause between the indictment and the demand. The Dead Sea Scrolls preserve the psalm in 11QPsa, with text largely matching the Masoretic Tradition. The Septuagint renders the psalm as Psalm 81 LXX, translating the plural *Elohim* of v. 1 as *theon* and the *Elohim atem* of v. 6 as *theoi este* — preserving the divine-plurality language that Greek readers would later carry into the New Testament. Ancient Near Eastern parallels are extensive: the Ugaritic *KTU* 1.2 describes *El* presiding over the council of the *bn-ilm*; Mesopotamian texts describe Anu and Enlil holding court over the *Annunaki*; the Egyptian *Ennead* offers a parallel divine council. Psalm 82 reads as Israel's distinctive transformation of this widespread ANE pattern — Israel's God is not first among equals but the only true God, and the other "gods" are demoted to mortality in v. 7.

## Lens 2 — The Literature

Lowth's parallelism is at its tightest in Psalm 82's eight verses. *Synonymous parallelism* opens the divine speech: "Give justice to the weak and the fatherless; / maintain the right of the afflicted and the destitute" (82:3) — two lines, four objects of justice (weak, fatherless, afflicted, destitute), one moral demand. *Synthetic parallelism* drives the descriptive section: "They have neither knowledge nor understanding, / they walk about in darkness" (82:5a) — the second line specifies the consequence of the first. *Antithetic parallelism* shapes the climactic verdict: "I said, 'You are gods'... / nevertheless, like men you shall die" (82:6-7) — divinity declared and mortality decreed in successive lines, the verb structure itself dramatizing the demotion.

The macro-structure is a **courtroom scene** in five movements. **Movement one** (v. 1) is the **opening description**: the narrator sets the stage — God stands in the council, holds judgment. **Movement two** (vv. 2-4) is the **divine indictment and instruction**: God speaks first, accusing the council members of injustice and commanding them to do justice for the vulnerable. **Movement three** (v. 5) is the **narrator's interjection**: a brief observation that the accused are too corrupt to understand, and that the foundations of the earth are shaking. **Movement four** (vv. 6-7) is the **divine verdict**: I said you are gods, but you shall die like men. **Movement five** (v. 8) is the **psalmist's closing prayer**: arise, O God, judge the earth — the human voice picking up after the divine voice has ended, calling on God to enact what he has just declared.

The psalm's structural genius is its threefold voice. The narrator speaks in vv. 1, 5, and 8; God speaks in vv. 2-4 and 6-7; and the boundary between them is interpretively crucial. Some commentators read v. 5 as part of the divine speech; others (the more common reading) read it as the narrator's commentary breaking into the courtroom scene. The decision affects the psalm's moral weight: in the first reading, God himself observes the council's incompetence; in the second, the human poet observes both the council's failure and the cosmic consequences (foundations shaken).

Inclusio binds the psalm. The opening *Elohim nitzav* ("God has taken his place," v. 1) is mirrored by the closing *qumah Elohim* ("arise, O God," v. 8). The psalm opens with God standing in the council and closes with God being summoned to stand for the earth. The standing is the psalm's structural verb, and its movement — from divine standing within the council to divine standing over the whole earth — traces the psalm's argumentative arc.

Chiastic structure organizes the divine speech. The indictment (v. 2 — "How long will you judge unjustly?") is mirrored by the verdict (vv. 6-7 — "you shall die like men"). The instruction (vv. 3-4 — give justice, rescue the weak) is mirrored by the descriptive failure (v. 5 — they have no knowledge, the foundations shake). The chiasm centers on the *gap* between what should be done and what is being done — the psalm holds the moral demand and the cosmic failure in mirror image at its heart.

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story (Jewish and Christian Trajectories)

Psalm 82 sits in **Book III** (Pss 73-89), and Gerald Wilson's canonical-shape reading places its divine-council judgment near the close of the Asaphite collection and the Elohistic Psalter. The psalm's vision of cosmic injustice and divine intervention prepares the reader for the final lament over the Davidic covenant in Psalm 89, where the same questions of cosmic faithfulness will be pressed in concrete historical-political terms.

The **Jewish trajectory** of Psalm 82 is dominated by the interpretive question of what *Elohim* means in vv. 1 and 6. The Targum Jonathan, Rashi, Ibn Ezra, and Radak all read the *Elohim* as **human judges** — Israelite magistrates whom Scripture addresses as *Elohim* metaphorically because they exercise divinely-delegated judicial authority. The Talmud (*Sanhedrin* 6b-7a) develops the same reading, linking the psalm to the rabbinic courts and using its language to describe the moral weight of judicial responsibility. Rashi reads the verse "you shall die like men" as God's warning that corrupt judges will be held to account; Ibn Ezra reads the *bnei elyon* of v. 6 as Israelite judges who carry the title because of their delegated office; Radak reads the council scene as a vision of God presiding over human magistracy. Nachmanides allows for a more layered reading — the human-judges interpretation as primary but with a subtext of angelic patronage of nations. *Midrash Tehillim* on Psalm 82 reads the psalm as a charter of judicial responsibility: judges must remember they will be judged.

The Holocaust gave the psalm a new and bitter resonance. Levenson reads the psalm's "all the foundations of the earth are shaken" (82:5) as a pre-modern articulation of what twentieth-century Jewish theology after Auschwitz would call the trembling of the world's moral architecture. Sarna emphasizes the psalm's passionate commitment to the weak and afflicted; Buber the dialogical structure of God speaking to corrupt powers; Heschel the prophetic-poetic indignation at injustice carried out in heaven's name; Avivah Zornberg the psychoanalytic reading of how power corrupts even the powers themselves. Liturgically, Psalm 82 is the **Shir Shel Yom for Tuesday** (in Mishnah *Tamid* 7:4 it is the daily psalm for the third day of the week) — every Tuesday in observant Jewish prayer, this psalm is recited as part of *Shacharit*. The choice ties the psalm to creation theology: on the third day of creation, dry land emerged and was given foundation; the psalm laments that those foundations now shake.

The **Christian trajectory** is dominated by **John 10:34-36**, where Jesus quotes Psalm 82:6 in a controversy with religious authorities. Accused of blasphemy for claiming to be the Son of God, Jesus responds: "Is it not written in your Law, 'I said, you are gods'? If he called them gods to whom the word of God came — and Scripture cannot be broken — do you say of him whom the Father consecrated and sent into the world, 'You are blaspheming,' because I said, 'I am the Son of God'?" Jesus's argument is famously slippery and has invited centuries of exegetical effort. The classical Christian reading (Augustine, Chrysostom, Aquinas) interprets the Johannine move as follows: if Scripture itself uses *Elohim* of human judges or angelic beings, then the title cannot be reserved so absolutely that Jesus's claim to it is automatic blasphemy; Jesus's point is *a fortiori* — if those who merely received God's word can be called gods, how much more the one whom God consecrated and sent. Augustine's *Tractates on John* and *Enarrationes in Psalmos* on 82 develop the reading carefully: the psalm establishes a category of "those who are called gods through grace," and Christ is the one who *is* God by nature, so the application to him exceeds the category even as it uses the category's language. Athanasius and Cassiodorus follow similar paths.

Daniel Boyarin, in *The Jewish Gospels*, reads John 10's appropriation of Psalm 82:6 as a sophisticated piece of inner-Jewish exegesis: Jesus is reading the psalm in a way that was already available within Second Temple Jewish thought, where divine-council language and messianic exaltation were intertwined. The Christological move depends on, rather than contradicts, the Jewish exegetical tradition. Aquinas reads the psalm as establishing the participation of creatures in divine attributes by grace, with Christ as the supreme participation by nature. Luther in the *Operationes in Psalmos* uses the psalm to develop his theology of the two kingdoms — earthly powers exercise divine authority and will be held to divine standards. Calvin's 1557 commentary handles the psalm with characteristic rigor, defending the human-judges reading and using v. 6 to ground his theology of magistracy.

The Heiser / divine-council school has produced a powerful modern Christian rereading. Michael Heiser's *The Unseen Realm* and *Supernatural* argue that the human-judges reading is anachronistic — driven by post-monotheistic discomfort with divine plurality language — and that Psalm 82 is straightforwardly a vision of God presiding over real lesser divine beings who function as patrons of the nations and who have failed in their cosmic responsibility. On this reading, the psalm is part of a much larger biblical theology of the heavenly host (Job 1-2; Deut 32:8-9; 1 Kgs 22:19-23) and the New Testament's doctrine of "principalities and powers" (Eph 6:12; Col 2:15). The reading is contested but increasingly influential in evangelical and Reformed scholarship.

Modern critical scholarship has produced extensive engagement. Gunkel set the form-critical category; Mowinckel located the psalm in cult-prophetic liturgy; Westermann analyzed the divine-judgment form; Kraus, Hossfeld-Zenger, and Goldingay all read the psalm as evidence of the divine-council worldview surviving in Israelite tradition. Mark S. Smith's work on the early history of Israelite religion treats Psalm 82 as a key text. Frank Moore Cross's *Canaanite Myth and Hebrew Epic* situates the psalm within ANE divine-council tradition. Patrick Miller, William Brown, and Beth Tanner have all engaged the psalm seriously — Miller emphasizing its prophetic-political weight, Brown its cosmic moral architecture, Tanner its theological audacity.

## Lens 4 — The Theological Intent

Two tensions are unavoidable, and the honest reading holds both rather than dissolving either.

**Tension one: divine-beings reading versus human-judges reading.** The interpretive crux of Psalm 82 is who the *Elohim* of vv. 1 and 6 are. Three positions on the field. (1) **Human-judges reading** (Targum, Rashi, Ibn Ezra, Radak, Calvin, much Reformed and rabbinic mainstream, Goldingay's preferred reading): the *Elohim* are Israelite magistrates addressed metaphorically by the title because of their delegated judicial authority — the psalm is a prophetic charter of judicial responsibility, and "you shall die like men" is the warning that corrupt judges will be held mortal-accountable. (2) **Divine-beings reading** (Heiser, Mark S. Smith, Frank Moore Cross, Cyrus Gordon, much critical-historical scholarship): the *Elohim* are real lesser divine beings — patron deities of the nations — and the psalm depicts Yahweh judging them for their failure to govern the nations justly; "you shall die like men" is the cosmic-mythic decree that these beings will be reduced to mortality. (3) **Layered reading** (Nachmanides, some patristic and modern theological readers): the psalm operates on both levels — the cosmic and the political are connected, and the corruption of human magistrates participates in (or reflects) the corruption of higher powers. Steelman across all three: the psalm uses divine-council language with theological seriousness, not as decorative metaphor; the language of divine plurality is part of biblical witness, not embarrassment; and whether the referents are angelic patrons of nations or corrupt earthly judges, the moral demand is the same — justice for the weak, the fatherless, the afflicted, and the destitute. **Where this leaves us:** the human-judges reading has carried much of Jewish and Reformed tradition and has the merit of straightforward ethical application; the divine-beings reading has the merit of taking the Hebrew text at face value and of integrating the psalm with the broader canonical witness to the heavenly host. Honest exegesis admits that the historical-original meaning is most likely the divine-beings reading — the ANE parallels, the precise vocabulary of *adat-El* and *bnei elyon*, the Ugaritic background — and that the human-judges reading, while exegetically defensible, is an inner-biblical rereading that became standard precisely because later monotheism found the divine-council language uncomfortable. Both readings have theological work to do. The cosmic reading expands the moral imagination; the political reading concretizes the moral demand. A mature theology can hold both.

**Tension two: the John 10:34-36 Christological appropriation and what Jesus is doing with the verse.** Three positions. (1) **Defensive-deflection reading** (some critical scholarship): Jesus is making a clever debater's move — if your scripture uses *Elohim* loosely, you cannot use that language as automatic disqualification of my claim — without actually grounding his claim in the psalm's substance. (2) **Participation-Christology reading** (Augustine, Aquinas, much classical Christian tradition): the psalm establishes that creatures can be called *Elohim* by grace; Christ is the supreme case, the one who participates in divinity by nature, and the *a fortiori* argument grounds the Christological claim in the psalm's logic. (3) **Inner-Jewish exegesis reading** (Boyarin, much modern post-supersessionist scholarship): Jesus is doing what Second Temple Jewish exegesis was already doing — reading the divine-council language and the messianic-exaltation traditions together — and the Christological claim is a particular instantiation of available Jewish exegetical moves rather than a Christian violation of the Jewish text. Steelman: Jesus's argument is not a deflection but a careful exegetical move that depends on the divine-plurality language Psalm 82 establishes; the Christological claim is grounded in the psalm's logic, not imposed on it; and the move is intelligible within Second Temple Judaism even if it is contested by some Jewish hearers. **Where this leaves us:** John 10's use of Psalm 82 is theologically careful and exegetically deep. Christians can hear the Christological claim without claiming the psalm was *only* about Christ; Jewish readers can hear the inner-Jewish exegesis without conceding that Christianity's particular conclusions follow. The psalm's divine-council language is generous enough to bear both readings without being exhausted by either.

A closing question. The psalm ends with the cry "Arise, O God, judge the earth, for you shall inherit all the nations" (82:8). The verdict against the corrupt members of the divine council has been pronounced, but the verdict has not yet been enacted. The poet calls on God to do what God has just declared. This gap — between divine declaration and divine action — is the gap in which all of biblical lament and all of Christian prayer lives. The corrupt powers are mortal; the foundations are shaken; the weak still wait for justice. Christians read John 10 and confess that in Christ the inheritance of the nations has begun; Jews read Tuesday's daily psalm and pray that God will yet arise and judge the earth. Both traditions are praying the same prayer the psalm itself prays. The corrupt powers, whatever they are, have not yet died. The cry "Arise, O God" is still the right cry. The psalm hands its readers the prayer; it does not pretend to have already received the answer.

## The Thread

- **John 10:34-36** — Jesus quotes Psalm 82:6 ("I said, you are gods") to defend his "Son of God" claim — the most consequential New Testament use of the psalm.
- **Deuteronomy 32:8-9** (in the Septuagint and Dead Sea Scrolls reading) — God divides the nations "according to the number of the sons of God," a parallel divine-council passage that Psalm 82 presupposes.
- **1 Kings 22:19-23** — Micaiah's vision of Yahweh enthroned with the host of heaven on either side — another Old Testament window onto the divine council Psalm 82 dramatizes.
- **Ephesians 6:12** — "we wrestle... against the principalities, against the powers... against the spiritual hosts of wickedness in the heavenly places" — the New Testament's continuation of the divine-council theology Psalm 82 articulates.
