# Leviticus 5: What a Poor Man Brings

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

Leviticus — Hebrew *Wayyiqrāʾ* (וַיִּקְרָא, "and he called") from 1:1 — continues from the tabernacle's completion in Exodus 40. Chapter 5 concludes the worshipper-manual that began in Lev 1, and forms a bridge into the priest-manual of Lev 6-7. Within the expiatory-offerings section (Lev 4-5), chapter 5 does two distinct things. Verses 1-13 extend the sin offering (*ḥaṭṭāʾt*) of Lev 4 into additional specific cases with a striking graded economy for the poor. Verses 14-19 introduce a new offering entirely — the guilt offering or reparation offering (*ʾāšām*) — which functions differently from the *ḥaṭṭāʾt* and handles a different category of wrongdoing. The Hebrew verse numbering differs from most English translations: in MT and the Vulgate, the guilt offering section runs 5:14-26, with Lev 6:1-7 in English equivalent to MT 5:20-26. This disparity matters because it tracks a real literary seam.

Critical dating follows the now-familiar shape. Wellhausen (*Prolegomena*, 1878) placed P post-exilic; Kaufmann, Haran, and Milgrom argued pre-exilic. Israel Knohl's *Sanctuary of Silence* (1995) distinguished P from H. Milgrom's *Anchor Bible Leviticus* (1991-2000) — the definitive treatment — devotes extended analysis to Lev 5 and argues that the distinct vocabulary of *ʾāšām* and its conceptual structure (reparation, the twenty-percent surcharge) preserve genuinely pre-exilic legal practice with analogues in ANE reparation law. The lexicographical argument, worked out over decades, has transformed scholarly understanding of the chapter. Jacob Milgrom's *Cult and Conscience* (1976) was the precursor.

The chapter's most important lexicographical question is the distinction between *ḥaṭṭāʾt* (sin/purification offering) and *ʾāšām* (guilt/reparation offering). Older commentators, and much popular Christian usage, treated these as near-synonyms or mild variants. Milgrom's central argument is that they are functionally distinct offerings with different theological remits:

- *Ḥaṭṭāʾt* (חַטָּאת) — purification offering. Cleanses the sanctuary from ritual pollution generated by sin. Blood is the central ritual element. The offering in Lev 4 and Lev 5:1-13.
- *ʾĀšām* (אָשָׁם) — reparation offering. Repays God for desecration of sacred property or fraud involving an oath. Monetary restitution (plus a twenty-percent surcharge) is central; the ram is an additional sacrificial "fee." The offering in Lev 5:14-26 (MT) / Lev 5:14-6:7 (English).

This distinction matters because the *ʾāšām* legislation is fundamentally different in structure from the *ḥaṭṭāʾt*. Where the sin offering graduates by sinner's status (priest, congregation, chief, commoner), the *ʾāšām* graduates by the damage done, and requires restitution plus a fine paid in addition to the animal offering. It is, in essence, a temple tax plus a sacrificial procedure, and it is the closest Israelite legal institution to modern tort-law reparation. Baruch Levine (*JPS Leviticus*), Jay Sklar (*Sin, Impurity, Sacrifice, Atonement*, 2005), and Nobuyoshi Kiuchi have all worked through the distinction, with Sklar arguing for a more synthetic view — *kipper* does both purgation and ransom in both offerings, but with different weightings.

The specific cases in vv. 1-4 are sociologically rich. Four types of inadvertent sin are named:

- **Withholding testimony** (v. 1): hearing a public adjuration (*ʾālâ*, an oath-curse) and failing to come forward with what one knows. The failure is sinful even when the sin is one of omission.
- **Touching an unclean animal carcass** (v. 2): ritual contamination without awareness. The *kî-neʿlam mimmennû* ("it was hidden from him") formula signals inadvertence.
- **Touching human uncleanness** (v. 3): presumably bodily discharges of the sort detailed in Lev 12-15.
- **Rash oath** (v. 4): swearing (*šābaʿ*) lightly to do something — good or ill — and then forgetting the oath or being unable to fulfill it.

These four cases are unified by a narrative pattern: the offender acts (or fails to act) without realizing the ritual or moral implication, the matter is later revealed, the offender acknowledges guilt (*ve-ʾāšēm*, "he realizes his guilt" — the verbal form of *ʾāšām*), and confesses (*ve-hitwaddâ*, v. 5). Confession is the only place in the worshipper-manual where verbal acknowledgment is required — a notable feature that Milgrom presses. The sacrifice does not operate mechanically; it requires the offender's conscious admission.

The graded economy of vv. 6-13 is the chapter's most distinctive feature. For the same four cases, the offering scales to the offender's means:

- A female lamb or goat (v. 6) — the standard commoner's *ḥaṭṭāʾt*.
- If he cannot afford a lamb: two turtledoves or pigeons, one as sin offering, one as burnt offering (vv. 7-10).
- If he cannot afford birds: a tenth of an ephah of fine flour (*ʿissarôn sōlet*, roughly 2.3 liters), without oil or frankincense (vv. 11-13).

This triple descent — flock animal, to birds, to flour — is Leviticus's most explicit statement that the sacrificial system is not reserved for the wealthy. The pointed removal of oil and frankincense from the poor man's flour offering (v. 11) is theologically telling: Lev 2's grain offering, which includes oil and frankincense, is a thanksgiving gift; this flour offering is a sin offering, which must bear the austerity of its purpose. Milgrom in his commentary calls this the clearest evidence that the priestly system was democratizing — not, as Wellhausen alleged, an elite post-exilic imposition, but a code deliberately structured to remain accessible to the poor.

The guilt-offering section (vv. 14-19, continuing through MT 5:20-26 / English 6:1-7) specifies two cases:

- Unintentional desecration of sacred things (*maʿal ba-qōdeš*, v. 15): using for ordinary purposes what belongs to God. The offering is a ram valued in shekels of the sanctuary, plus restitution of the desecrated property, plus a twenty-percent surcharge (*ḥamîšîtô*).
- A doubt case (vv. 17-19): the offender suspects he may have sinned but is not sure. The same ram is required, without restitution (since no specific damage is known). This "doubt offering" (*ʾāšām tālûy*) is developed extensively in rabbinic literature.

The fraud case (MT 5:20-26 / English 6:1-7) extends the *ʾāšām* to cases of fraud against neighbors involving oaths — deposits denied, robbery, extortion. Restitution, twenty-percent surcharge, and ram sacrifice are all required. The striking feature is that sin against neighbor (*ʿāšâ šeqer ba-ʿămîtô*, "dealing falsely with one's fellow") is treated ritually as desecration of God's sacred property: to defraud a neighbor via oath is to take God's name in vain, which is *maʿal* against God. Milgrom's analysis of this logic — that social fraud and sanctuary desecration are fungible categories — is one of his most important contributions.

ANE parallels. Hittite temple protocols (*Instructions for Temple Officials*, CTH 264) specify reparation for temple property with silver payments. The Laws of Hammurabi (e.g., §§ 5, 6, 9-13) treat oath-sworn fraud with graduated penalties. Egyptian temple law prescribes restitution for desecration of sacred things. What is distinctive in Lev 5 is the twenty-percent surcharge and its equal application to desecration of God's property and fraud against neighbor — Israel's code monetizes ritual damage while refusing to separate cultic from social categories. Simo Parpola's work on neo-Assyrian temple administration and Raymond Westbrook's *A History of Ancient Near Eastern Law* (2 vols, 2003) compile the comparative evidence.

Key terms to carry:

- *ʾĀšām* (אָשָׁם) — guilt; reparation offering. As verb (*ʾāšēm*): "he realizes his guilt."
- *Maʿal* (מַעַל) — trespass; desecration of the sacred. A technical term.
- *Ḥamîšîtô* (חֲמִישִׁיתוֹ) — its fifth; the twenty-percent surcharge.
- *Šegāgâ* (שְׁגָגָה) — unintentional sin, inadvertent error. Paired with the deliberate sin (*bĕyād rāmâ*, Num 15:30).
- *Hitwaddâ* (הִתְוַדָּה) — to confess (hithpael of *ydh*).

Manuscript evidence. 4QLevc (4Q25) preserves portions of Lev 5 with minor orthographic differences from MT. The LXX renders *ʾāšām* variously — sometimes as *plēmmeleia* ("error, blunder"), sometimes as *basanos* — with some confusion suggesting the translators struggled to render the technical term. This lexical uncertainty shaped patristic reception, which often conflated *ʾāšām* with *ḥaṭṭāʾt*. The Samaritan Pentateuch closely aligns with MT. A crucial LXX note: Isaiah 53:10's *ʾāšām tāśîm napšô* ("his soul will make a guilt offering") is rendered *peri hamartias* in LXX, which blurs the distinction that makes the Hebrew so striking — the Servant is specifically a *reparation* offering, with its implication of restitution.

## Lens 2 — The Literature

The genre is priestly ritual jurisprudence with two distinct sub-units. Verses 1-13 extend the sin offering of Lev 4 into specific cases with a poor-man's provision; vv. 14-19 (plus MT 5:20-26) introduce the guilt offering and specify its cases. The seam between these two units is marked literarily by a new speech-introduction formula at v. 14 (*wayyedabbēr YHWH ʾel-mōšeh*, "the LORD spoke to Moses"), absent since 4:1. Milgrom treats this seam as editorial but as reflecting a genuine functional distinction, not as mere compilation.

The first unit (vv. 1-13) is structured by its casuistic descent. Four offense cases (vv. 1-4) converge on a single ritual remedy (vv. 5-6) that is then scaled downward by economic status (vv. 7-13). The unifying refrain is *ve-ʾāšēm* / *ve-hôdâ* ("he realizes his guilt / he confesses") — the subjective acknowledgment that triggers the sacrificial process. The graded offerings are linked by the closing formula *vĕkipper ʿālāyw ha-kōhēn vĕ-nislaḥ lô* ("the priest shall make atonement for him and he shall be forgiven," vv. 10, 13) — the same formula as Lev 4.

The second unit (vv. 14-19) is briefer but more technically demanding. The formula "breach of faith" (*timʿōl maʿal*) and "holy things of the LORD" (*qodšê YHWH*) introduce the desecration case; the ram must be valued in *shekels of the sanctuary* (*bĕ-šeqel haqqōdeš*) — an administrative specification absent from the sin offering. This is the first sacrificial procedure in Leviticus with an explicit monetary component. Verses 17-19 treat the doubt case with a characteristic hesitancy in the Hebrew: *ve-lō-yādaʿ ve-ʾāšēm ve-nāśāʾ ʿăwōnô* — "he did not know, and he realizes his guilt, and he bears his iniquity." The grammar is deliberately uncertain.

Literary device: graded offerings reach their most developed form in Lev 5. The triple scaling (lamb / birds / flour) is unique in the sacrificial corpus. Milgrom's treatment is careful: the descent is not from "full" to "partial" sacrifice but from "full" to "functionally equivalent" — the flour offering is genuinely a *ḥaṭṭāʾt*, not a substitute for one. The phrase *ḥaṭṭāʾt hîʾ* ("it is a sin offering," v. 11) is the text's insistence on equivalence: the poor man's flour carries the same ritual weight as the wealthy man's lamb.

Textual variants include a minor LXX expansion at v. 6, rendering "female from the flock, a lamb or a goat" with an extended specification. 4QLev fragments preserve the chapter in close agreement with MT. Mary Douglas in *Leviticus as Literature* (1999) treats the chapter as a pivotal unit in what she reads as the book's ring-structure, marking the transition from individual to corporate concerns — though her structural argument is not universally accepted. Wenham (NICOT) treats the two units as distinct but editorially united. Hartley (WBC) emphasizes the bridge function between Lev 4's graded sin-offering and Lev 6-7's priestly procedures.

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

**Jewish Interpretive Trajectory.** Second Temple reception of Lev 5 is focused on the specific case law and the economic democratization. Philo (*Special Laws* 1.235-238) treats the poor-man's flour offering as evidence of divine clemency: God accepts what the worshipper can afford, not what the altar nominally requires. Josephus (*Antiquities* 3.230-234) gives procedural detail. The Temple Scroll (11Q19) elaborates the *ʾāšām* procedure significantly for the Qumran community's reconstructed temple. Jubilees touches the oath-breaking provisions (Jub 7:20-21). Tobit 4:15's negative golden rule may draw on Lev 5:20-26's fraud prohibition.

Rabbinic treatment is encyclopedic. Mishnah Keritot (on the thirty-six offenses requiring *kārēt*, excision) intersects with Lev 5 on the boundary between inadvertent and deliberate sin. Mishnah Shevuot (oaths) develops the testimony and rash-oath cases extensively. Mishnah Meilah (sacrilege) elaborates the *maʿal* category from v. 15. The *ʾāšām tālûy* (doubt offering) of vv. 17-19 receives a full tractate — tractate Keritot chapters 4-5 — and becomes one of the richest rabbinic discussions of moral and ritual epistemology: what counts as "realizing one's guilt" when one is uncertain? Sifra provides verse-by-verse halakhic exegesis. The Bavli on Kerithot works through the *ʾāšām* cases in procedural detail.

Rashi (on 5:1) comments extensively on the testimony case, reading the Torah's concern with withholding testimony as foundational to Jewish legal culture: the community cannot function if witnesses fail to appear. Rashi on 5:11 emphasizes the equity of the flour offering — God's sanctuary does not discriminate by wealth. Ibn Ezra connects the rash-oath case to Eccl 5:5's warning against vows. Ramban (Nachmanides) develops the *ʾāšām* at length, reading it as the clearest pre-Christian Jewish warrant for substitutionary sacrifice: the offender, having committed a specific, measurable damage, pays it back in full and then offers the ram as the cost of the offender's own life that the damage had forfeited. Ramban's reading of the twenty-percent surcharge is theologically weighted: God takes restoration seriously; honest repentance pays more than it owes. Maimonides codifies Lev 5 in *Hilchot Shegagot* and *Hilchot Gezelah ve-Avedah* (Laws of Robbery) — the legal infrastructure for the *ʾāšām* in a restored Temple.

Nehama Leibowitz (*Studies in Vayikra*) emphasizes the moral seriousness of the chapter: confession, restitution, and sacrifice are ordered steps that cannot be shortcut. Jonathan Sacks (*Covenant and Conversation: Leviticus*) reads the graded economy as a charter of inclusion — Leviticus's quiet insistence that the cult belongs to the poor as much as to the rich. Avivah Zornberg treats the rash-oath case psychoanalytically, as the moment language escapes the speaker. Baruch Levine's JPS volume is the premier Jewish commentary in English.

**Christian Interpretive Trajectory.** Patristic engagement with Lev 5 is narrower than with Lev 4, partly because the distinction between *ḥaṭṭāʾt* and *ʾāšām* was muted by LXX lexicography. Origen in *Homilies on Leviticus* (Hom. 2) reads the poor-man's flour offering allegorically: the humble soul brings what it has, and God accepts. Cyril of Alexandria (*Glaphyra*) treats the oath-breaking case in connection with the Sermon on the Mount's oath teaching (Matt 5:33-37). Augustine touches Lev 5's restitution requirement in *Letter 153* to Macedonius on the non-forgiveness of sins without restitution — a passage that becomes foundational to Catholic penitential theology. Chrysostom and Theodoret follow.

The most theologically consequential Christian engagement with Lev 5 is through Isaiah 53:10. The Servant "makes his soul an *ʾāšām*" — the technical Hebrew term for the reparation offering. The intertextual move is striking: the Servant is not a sin offering (*ḥaṭṭāʾt*) but a guilt offering (*ʾāšām*), carrying the logic of restitution plus surcharge. The Servant pays more than is owed; the offering is not merely purification but restoration with interest. Jewish and Christian exegesis diverge sharply on Isa 53, but both traditions recognize the *ʾāšām* grammar. For Christian atonement theology, Isa 53 becomes the bridge between Lev 5 and Hebrews 9-10.

Medieval exegesis develops the *ʾāšām*-substitution line. Aquinas in *Summa Theologiae* III q. 48 a. 2 ("whether Christ's passion brought about our salvation by way of satisfaction") draws on the *ʾāšām* logic, arguing that Christ's death pays the debt owed by human sin with a "satisfactio superabundans" — a satisfaction that more than restores what was lost. The twenty-percent surcharge of Lev 5 is the precise OT precedent for "satisfactio superabundans": repentance pays more than mere restoration. Anselm in *Cur Deus Homo* (1098) had already developed this conceptually; Aquinas anchors it in the Levitical text.

Reformation exegesis varies. Calvin's *Commentary on the Last Four Books of Moses* reads Lev 5 carefully, emphasizing the graded offerings as evidence of God's pastoral care for the poor. Calvin is particularly taken with the fraud provisions of vv. 20-26: that sin against neighbor is *maʿal* against God means that the neighbor's property is part of God's sacred economy. Luther's treatment is more occasional but also emphasizes the neighbor-God connection. John Owen in *The Death of Death* (1648) develops the *ʾāšām*-substitution typology at length as the OT foundation of penal substitutionary atonement.

Critical scholarship since Wellhausen has pivoted on Milgrom's lexicographical work on *ʾāšām*. The distinction between sin offering and guilt offering, once treated as stylistic variation, is now recognized as substantive. Sklar's *Sin, Impurity, Sacrifice, Atonement* (2005) is the most developed critical monograph. Kiuchi's *The Purification Offering* (1987) takes a differing view. Mary Douglas frames the chapter anthropologically: the economic scaling shows that ritual access is universal in Israel's cosmological imagination. Among contemporary theological commentators, Ephraim Radner (Brazos) reads Lev 5 figurally with attention to Isa 53; L. Michael Morales (*Who Shall Ascend*) places the chapter in the worship-ascent theology of Leviticus; Sklar's NIVAC volume is pastorally accessible; Wenham (NICOT) and Hartley (WBC) work the historical-grammatical middle.

**Where they converge and diverge.** Both traditions recognize the democratizing logic of the graded offerings — the cult belongs to the poor as much as to the rich. Both recognize the *ʾāšām* as a distinctive category involving restitution and surcharge. Both recognize confession as a required step. The divergence lies in what the *ʾāšām* is for. For Jewish interpretation, the guilt offering is a legal-ritual mechanism for restoring damage — monetized, specific, and thoroughly this-worldly in its economic logic; in the Temple's absence, *tĕšûvâ* (repentance) replaces the ritual, and the principle of restitution is preserved (Maimonides, *Hilchot Teshuvah* 2). For Christian interpretation, the *ʾāšām* logic is universalized and Christologized: Christ's death is the *ʾāšām*-with-surcharge that repairs the cosmic damage of sin, with the twenty-percent "interest" of Aquinas's *satisfactio superabundans*.

## Lens 4 — The Theological Intent

**Tension 1: Does the twenty-percent surcharge mean God profits from sin?** The *ḥamîšîtô* (one-fifth) is a real problem. If the offender defrauds his neighbor of one hundred shekels, he returns one hundred twenty. The neighbor receives more than was stolen. In the desecration case (v. 16), the sacred property is restored with an additional fifth paid to the priest. The priest — or the wronged party — benefits materially from the offense. Is this just?

The Jewish halakhic reading, codified in Bavli Bava Kamma and Maimonides' *Hilchot Gezelah ve-Avedah*, takes the surcharge as a principled component of genuine repentance. Repentance is not zero-sum restoration; it must overcompensate, because the act of wronging someone damages more than what was materially taken — it damages trust, dignity, social fabric. The twenty-percent is not profit but symbolic acknowledgment that full restoration exceeds literal payback. Ramban develops this at length: the surcharge is the offender's cost for having been the kind of person who would commit the offense. Maimonides reads it as a deterrent that also reshapes character.

Milgrom's critical reading is economic-ritual: the surcharge is the ANE reparation standard for trespass against sacred property, imported into Israelite legal practice and extended to neighbor-fraud (because, in the Levitical cosmology, fraud against neighbor via oath is desecration of divine property). The surcharge is not primarily theological but institutional; it fits neatly with Hittite and neo-Assyrian temple reparation practice. Baruch Levine concurs: the economic logic is ANE standard, with Israel distinctive only in the fungibility between cultic and social spheres.

A Catholic reading through Anselm and Aquinas theologizes the surcharge. Anselm's *Cur Deus Homo* argues that satisfaction must exceed mere restoration because sin offends infinite honor — the twenty-percent is a finite icon of the infinite "more" required. Aquinas's *satisfactio superabundans* makes this precise: Christ's death does not merely undo sin; it overfulfills divine justice, producing a surplus of grace. Ratzinger connects this to the Eucharistic logic of received-and-returned gift. Reformed theology (Calvin, Owen) reads the surcharge as teaching that sin is more than damage; it is insult, and full satisfaction must acknowledge the insult with something above restoration. Lutheran theology tends to emphasize that only Christ's infinite surplus of satisfaction truly answers the debt. Orthodox theology (Schmemann, Zizioulas) is typically less interested in surcharge economics and more in restoration-to-communion, though Athanasius's *On the Incarnation* has resonances with the surplus logic.

The steelman, rarely voiced, is Milgrom's institutional-historical reading: the surcharge is not a theological statement about sin but an ANE economic convention. The tradition's metaphysical elaborations (Anselm, Aquinas) retroject later theological concerns into what was originally a legal fee. This reading is less intuitive because it deflates atonement theology to its ANE substrate, but it has philological rigor.

**Where this leaves us:** the twenty-percent surcharge is simultaneously institutional convention and theological freight. Its meaning in the Levitical text is restitution-with-symbolic-overplus; its uptake in the Christian tradition through Isa 53 and Aquinas extends it into the cosmic grammar of Christ's death. Both readings can be held; the text rewards both.

**Tension 2: Why does the poor man's sin offering of flour omit oil and frankincense, when Lev 2's grain offerings require them?** The omission (v. 11) is textually explicit and deliberate. A *ḥaṭṭāʾt* of flour, unlike a voluntary *minḥâ* of flour, may not carry oil or frankincense. What is this saying about the nature of the poor man's sin offering?

The Jewish halakhic reading (Sifra, Rashi, codified in Maimonides *Hilchot Maaseh HaKorbanot*) treats the omission as preserving the sin offering's distinctive austerity. Oil and frankincense are festal ingredients; they belong to offerings of thanksgiving and devotion, not to offerings of expiation. The flour *ḥaṭṭāʾt* must look like a sin offering, not like a grain offering. Rashi glosses: "it is a sin offering — so that it not look like a gift offering." Ramban develops this: the poor man's offering must carry the same grief and seriousness as the rich man's lamb; festal adornments would belie the occasion.

Milgrom's reading is procedural: the flour *ḥaṭṭāʾt* is functionally a substitute for animal sacrifice, and its austerity marks its expiatory character. The omission ensures that the token portion burned on the altar is unmistakably a sin offering, not a gift. Anthropologically (Mary Douglas), the omission signals ritual category: sin offerings and gift offerings must be kept visibly distinct, even when the raw materials overlap.

A Christian reading through the patristic tradition (Origen, Cyril) treats the omission typologically: the sin offering cannot be adorned because it represents the stark reality of sin, without festal garnish. Aquinas touches this in ST I-II q. 102 a. 3, reading the austerity as preparing for the sin-offering's typological fulfillment in Christ, whose sacrifice is similarly stripped (cf. Matt 27:46, the cry of dereliction). Calvin reads the omission as teaching that the offerer's sin must be owned honestly — no hiding behind the festal trappings of worship. Luther similarly.

The steelman is the ritual-category reading of Douglas and Milgrom together: the omission is primarily categorical, not moral. The cult must keep its ritual grammar intact; sin offerings cannot look like grain offerings, even when they are made of flour. The theological elaborations (austerity of sin, honest repentance) are downstream of the category-maintenance logic.

**Where this leaves us:** the omission of oil and frankincense is a ritual-semiotic act that also carries theological freight. The sin offering must look like what it is, even when pressed into the poor man's materials. Both traditions preserve this — Jewish interpretation in the halakhic distinction between *ḥaṭṭāʾt* and *minḥâ*, Christian interpretation in the austere shape of penitential liturgy.

**Unresolved question:** if Isa 53:10's Servant "makes his soul an *ʾāšām*," and if the *ʾāšām* requires specific monetary restitution plus twenty-percent surcharge, what is the cosmological "restitution" that Christ's death accomplishes, and to whom is the twenty-percent surcharge paid? Christian atonement theology has sometimes pressed the legal-economic logic (Anselm, Aquinas, Owen) and sometimes dissolved it into communion-restoration (Athanasius, Schmemann), without fully reconciling the two. The question of what the *ʾāšām* grammar commits Christian theology to is still open.

## The Thread

Three non-obvious cross-references worth following:

- **2 Kings 12:16** — the silver brought as *ʾāšām* and *ḥaṭṭāʾt* to the temple in Jehoash's reign is kept by the priests, not put into the temple repair fund. The Deuteronomistic historian preserves the Levitical procedural distinction, with the *ʾāšām* funds belonging to the priests as a matter of institutional memory. This is one of the strongest pieces of evidence that the Lev 5 legislation was operative in pre-exilic Israel.
- **Numbers 5:5-8** — the restitution law for fraud without living relatives specifies that if the wronged party has no next of kin, the restitution plus twenty-percent goes to the priest. This is a direct application of Lev 5:20-26, showing the *ʾāšām* framework at work in a case where the victim is unavailable.
- **Isaiah 53:10** — *ʾim-tāśîm ʾāšām napšô* ("when his soul makes a guilt offering"). The use of *ʾāšām* rather than *ḥaṭṭāʾt* is exegetically loaded: the Servant is not a purification offering but a reparation offering, bringing the surcharge logic of Lev 5 into the heart of Israel's most developed vicarious-suffering text. The fact that LXX renders it more generically *peri hamartias* obscures in Greek what the Hebrew makes specific — one of the places where translation has flattened a theologically critical distinction.
