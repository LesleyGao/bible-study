# Acts 11: The Defense, the Naming, and the Birth of Antiochene Christianity

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

Acts 11 unfolds against a Mediterranean stage that Luke handles with surprising geographical precision. The chapter takes Peter from Caesarea Maritima — Herod the Great's gleaming port city, seat of Roman procurators, the place where Cornelius's household had just received the Spirit — back up to Jerusalem, then opens a second front nearly three hundred miles north in **Antioch on the Orontes**. The dating belongs to the early-to-mid forties of the first century, under the emperor **Claudius** (41–54 CE), whose reign forms the political backdrop for both the famine prophecy of verses 27–30 and the crackdown that will follow in chapter 12. Claudius's expulsion of Jews from Rome in 49 CE (mentioned later in Acts 18:2 and corroborated by Suetonius *Claudius* 25.4, "*impulsore Chresto*") sits just over the chronological horizon; Luke is narrating events from a moment when the Jewish-Christian movement was still concentrated in the eastern Mediterranean and the Roman state had not yet learned to distinguish the new sect from its parent body.

Antioch on the Orontes was, by the standards of the first century, an enormous city. **Glanville Downey**'s magisterial *A History of Antioch in Syria from Seleucus to the Arab Conquest* (Princeton, 1961) established the standard scholarly portrait: a Seleucid foundation of 300 BCE that had grown by the Roman period into the third-largest city in the empire, behind only Rome and Alexandria, with a population variously estimated between 150,000 and 500,000. The city sat at the junction of the Orontes River and the great east-west land routes, with its port at Seleucia Pieria, sixteen miles downstream. Strabo (*Geography* 16.2.4–5) describes it as a tetrapolis — four quarters successively walled — with broad colonnaded streets that Herod the Great himself helped pave. The French archaeological excavations of the 1930s under the Princeton-led Antioch Expedition uncovered the famous mosaic pavements, the hippodrome, and traces of the colonnaded main street, though the ancient city center has been deeply buried by alluvium and the modern Turkish city of Antakya.

Crucially for our chapter, **Magnus Zetterholm**'s *The Formation of Christianity in Antioch: A Social-Scientific Approach to the Separation between Judaism and Christianity* (Routledge, 2003) reconstructs the layered Jewish community of the city. Antioch had a Jewish population that Josephus describes as exceptionally large (*War* 7.43–45), enjoying special privileges granted by the Seleucids and confirmed by the Romans. The synagogues attracted Gentile sympathizers — God-fearers who attached themselves to Jewish worship without full proselyte conversion — and it is precisely this porous boundary that the Cypriot and Cyrenian believers in Acts 11:20 exploit when they "spoke to the Hellenists also."

Five Greek terms anchor the chapter's historical world. First, **Ἑλληνιστάς** (*Hellēnistas*, v. 20) — "Hellenists." The textual tradition is genuinely divided here between *Hellēnistas* (Greek-speaking Jews) and *Hellēnas* (ethnic Greeks); the Alexandrian witnesses are split, and the sense of Luke's narrative — that this represents the first Gentile mission proper — favors the latter reading even where the harder text reads the former. Second, **Χριστιανοί** (*Christianoi*, v. 26) — "Christians" — the first New Testament occurrence. Third, **ἐκκλησία** (*ekklēsia*, vv. 22, 26) — "assembly, church" — used here for the Antiochene community as a settled institution. Fourth, **προφῆται** (*prophētai*, v. 27) — "prophets" — itinerant figures whose authority derives from charismatic gift rather than office. Fifth, **πρεσβυτέρους** (*presbyterous*, v. 30) — "elders" — the first appearance in Acts of what will become a stable structure of local governance, here mentioned almost in passing as the recipients of Antiochene relief funds.

The famine prophecy attributed to Agabus deserves historical attention. Suetonius (*Claudius* 18) records "*assiduae sterilitates*" — repeated crop failures — under Claudius. Tacitus (*Annals* 12.43) describes a particular grain shortage in Rome around 51 CE. **Josephus** (*Antiquities* 20.2.5; 20.5.2) describes a severe famine in Judea around 46–48 CE during the procuratorships of Cuspius Fadus and Tiberius Alexander, recording how Queen Helena of Adiabene purchased grain from Egypt and figs from Cyprus to feed the population. Luke's "great famine over all the world" is hyperbolic — *oikoumenē* refers to the inhabited Roman world — but the underlying fact of recurrent supply crises under Claudius is firmly attested.

## Lens 2 — The Literature

Chapter 11 functions as a hinge in Luke's architecture, and recognizing the hinge is essential for reading the chapter rightly. **Robert Tannehill**, in *The Narrative Unity of Luke-Acts: A Literary Interpretation* (Fortress, 1990), volume 2, treats this as a deliberate narrative deceleration. Luke has just told the Cornelius story at length in chapter 10. He now retells it — through Peter's own mouth — at considerable additional length in 11:1–18. This recapitulation is not redundancy but Lukan rhetoric. By forcing his audience to revisit the same events from a defensive posture, Luke makes the theological breakthrough of Cornelius the explicit, formally-acknowledged property of the Jerusalem church. The narrative move is identical in form to the triple narration of Paul's Damascus road experience in chapters 9, 22, and 26: when something matters most, Luke tells it more than once.

The structural pivot is even more dramatic. Up through 11:18, the Petrine apostolic narrative has dominated. From 11:19 onward, the camera begins to pan toward Antioch. Saul, last seen retreating to Tarsus in 9:30, is fetched back into the narrative by Barnabas at 11:25 — and from this point on, Luke will progressively transfer narrative weight from Peter to Paul until, by chapter 13, the Antiochene mission becomes the dominant frame. The "first called Christians at Antioch" notice at 11:26 is therefore doing double work: it names the new movement and simultaneously announces a new geographical and ecclesial center.

**C. K. Barrett**'s ICC commentary observes the careful Lukan construction by which Antioch is *introduced* but not allowed to *displace* Jerusalem. Barnabas is sent *from* Jerusalem (11:22), and the Antiochene relief is sent *to* Jerusalem (11:29–30). The mother-daughter relationship is preserved even as the daughter outgrows the mother. **Daniel Marguerat** in *The First Christian Historian: Writing the "Acts of the Apostles"* (Cambridge, 2002) calls this a "gradient of legitimation": Antioch's authority must always be derivative until the Council of chapter 15 ratifies its mission as equal in standing.

The major historical-critical problem the chapter sets in motion is the **Acts–Galatians chronology**. Galatians 1–2 records exactly two Pauline visits to Jerusalem before the Council: the first three years after his conversion, a fifteen-day stay with Peter (Gal 1:18); the second "after fourteen years" with Barnabas and Titus (Gal 2:1–10), described as a private revelation-prompted visit in which the pillars extended the right hand of fellowship. Acts records *five* visits before the apostolic conference of chapter 15: 9:26–30 (post-conversion); 11:30 (the famine relief, here in chapter 11); 12:25 (return from the relief mission); and the Council itself (15:2). Which Galatians visit corresponds to which Acts visit?

**John Knox**, in *Chapters in a Life of Paul* (rev. ed., Mercer, 1987), argued that Paul's two visits in Galatians correspond to Acts 9:26–30 and Acts 15, with the famine visit of 11:30 either fictional or chronologically misplaced. **F. F. Bruce**, in his NICNT commentary and in his *Paul: Apostle of the Heart Set Free* (Eerdmans, 1977), defended the older identification of the Galatians 2 visit with the famine relief visit of Acts 11:30, arguing that this dates Galatians before the Council and resolves Paul's silence about the Decree. **Stanley Porter**, in *When Paul Met Jesus* and elsewhere, has revived the case for taking Acts seriously as historical witness while admitting Lukan compression. **Daniel Marguerat** treats the chronological discrepancy as a real Lukan editorial intervention: Luke knows Paul's letters (or knows *of* them) and is producing a smoother, more ecclesially-coordinated narrative than the historical Paul would have recognized. **Richard Bauckham**'s *The Book of Acts in Its Palestinian Setting* (Eerdmans, 1995) takes the famine visit as historical and notes the chronological compatibility with Josephus's 46–48 CE famine dating.

There is a final literary feature worth flagging: the Western Text. The so-called Western recension of Acts — represented chiefly by Codex Bezae (D) — runs roughly 8.5 percent longer than the Alexandrian text. In chapter 11 the Western text adds picturesque detail to the Cornelius retelling and slightly amplifies the Antiochene narrative. **Eldon Epp**'s *The Theological Tendency of Codex Bezae Cantabrigiensis in Acts* (Cambridge, 1966) argued that the Western expansions exhibit an anti-Judaic tendency. **Bruce Metzger**'s *A Textual Commentary on the Greek New Testament* treats the Alexandrian text as primary and the Western as secondary expansion. Luke's text in chapter 11 is unusually stable across the traditions, but the textual situation will become acute in chapter 15.

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

The Cornelius defense and the Antiochene founding stand at the center of the most contested narrative in Christian theology: the question of how Gentiles enter the people of God. Two readings — Jewish and Christian — have travelled side by side across two millennia and now, increasingly, converge.

The **Christian patristic tradition** read Acts 11 as the divinely-engineered abolition of the boundary between Jew and Gentile. **John Chrysostom**'s *Homilies on Acts* (Homilies 24–25 cover chapter 11) turns the chapter into a meditation on the prudence of Peter's defense and the providential timing of Antioch's founding; Chrysostom, preaching in Antioch itself in the 380s, was acutely conscious of the chapter's local resonance. **Bede** in his *Expositio Actuum Apostolorum* draws out the typology of the great sheet as the catholic church drawing in clean and unclean alike. **Augustine** invokes the chapter in his anti-Donatist writings as evidence that the church was always meant to be a *corpus mixtum* of every nation. **Origen**'s *Contra Celsum* uses the Christian-naming at Antioch to defend the movement's universal scope against Celsus's charge of provincialism.

The **Reformation** read the chapter through the lens of justification. **John Calvin**'s *Commentary on the Acts of the Apostles* devotes substantial attention to Peter's defense, drawing the Reformed conclusion that the Spirit's free gift to Cornelius "before circumcision" demonstrates the priority of grace over ceremonial law — a reading that would become decisive for chapter 15. **Luther** drew similar conclusions in his *Lectures on Galatians*, though without a sustained Acts commentary of his own.

Twentieth-century scholarship reframed the chapter sociologically and theologically. **Ernst Haenchen**'s *The Acts of the Apostles: A Commentary* (German 1956, English ET 1971) read the recapitulation as Lukan apologetic: Luke knew the Jewish-Christian opposition to Pauline universalism and was rewriting history to give it formal Petrine-Jerusalem ratification. **Hans Conzelmann**'s Hermeneia commentary on Acts (German 1963, English 1987) framed the Antiochene founding as the institutional birth of Gentile Christianity. **F. F. Bruce**, **C. K. Barrett**, **Joseph Fitzmyer** (Anchor Bible), **Luke Timothy Johnson** (Sacra Pagina), **Beverly Gaventa** (Abingdon), and **Craig Keener**'s monumental four-volume Hendrickson commentary all develop this reading with varying emphases. **Eckhard Schnabel**'s ZECNT volume gives close attention to the missiological logic. **David Peterson**'s Pillar commentary draws the theological consequences for the doctrine of the church. **Richard Pervo**'s Hermeneia commentary, by contrast, reads the whole sequence as Lukan historical fiction — a second-century novel of origins. **I. Howard Marshall**'s TNTC offers the evangelical mainstream synthesis.

Pentecostal scholarship has read the chapter as paradigmatic. **Roger Stronstad**'s *The Charismatic Theology of St. Luke* and **Robert Menzies**'s *Empowered for Witness* take 11:15 — "the Holy Spirit fell on them just as on us at the beginning" — as evidence that Spirit-baptism is a recurring, repeatable empowerment-event, not merely the universal initiation Reformed theology has often described. Liberation theology, particularly in **Justo González**'s *Acts: The Gospel of the Spirit* (Orbis, 2001), reads the Antiochene founding as the birth of a culturally-mixed, economically-redistributive community whose first recorded action is to send relief to the poor in Jerusalem.

The **Jewish interpretive trajectory** approaches Acts 11 from a different angle. **Adele Reinhartz**, **Daniel Boyarin** (especially *Border Lines: The Partition of Judaeo-Christianity*, 2004), **Amy-Jill Levine**, **Lawrence Schiffman**, and **Pamela Eisenbaum** (*Paul Was Not a Christian*, HarperOne, 2009) have collectively reframed the early Jesus movement as an *intra-Jewish* phenomenon whose boundaries with Judaism were not yet drawn in the 40s CE. **Matthew Thiessen**'s *A Jewish Paul: The Messiah's Herald to the Gentiles* (Baker Academic, 2023) and **Paula Fredriksen**'s *Paul: The Pagan's Apostle* (Yale, 2017) argue that the Cornelius episode and the Antiochene mission do not represent Christianity's break from Judaism but rather a particular Jewish-eschatological expectation working itself out: the gathering of the Gentiles *as Gentiles* in the last days, a vision rooted in Isaiah 56 and Zechariah 8. On this reading, Peter's defense is not a Christian universalism overcoming Jewish particularism; it is one Jewish messianic faction settling, on its own terms, the question of how the eschatological pilgrimage of the nations is to be received.

The **naming at Antioch** has its own contested interpretive history. The suffix *-ianos* is Latin (*-ianus*), used in Roman administrative and political contexts to designate partisans or followers — *Caesariani*, *Pompeiani*, *Herodiani*. **F. F. Bruce** treats the term as likely originating with outsiders, possibly Roman authorities, who needed an administrative label for this distinct sect within Antioch's Jewish community. Tacitus's later use (*Annals* 15.44, written c. 116 CE) of *Christiani* in describing Nero's persecution confirms that the term had become a recognized Roman classification by the mid-60s. Whether the original naming at Antioch was pejorative, neutral, or affectionate has been debated since the fathers; Chrysostom takes it as honorific, while modern scholars like **Justin Taylor** have argued for its administrative origin. **Magnus Zetterholm** locates the naming in the social dynamics of Antioch's mixed Jewish-Gentile community: the new label was needed precisely because the old categories — *Ioudaioi*, God-fearers, proselytes — no longer captured what was happening on the ground.

## Lens 4 — The Theological Intent

**Tension 1: Whose initiative is the Gentile mission?** The chapter is structured to make the answer ambiguous in productive ways. Peter narrates the Cornelius episode as wholly God's initiative — the vision came unsought, the Spirit fell unbidden, and Peter's role was reduced to non-resistance ("who was I that I could stand in God's way?"). But verses 19–20 narrate the parallel breakthrough at Antioch as the action of unnamed Cypriot and Cyrenian believers, scattered by the persecution over Stephen, who simply began preaching to Greeks. Was the Gentile mission initiated by special divine intervention to Peter (the Cornelius account), or did it arise organically through scattered laity making contextual judgments at the city level (the Antioch account)?

The Reformed-evangelical reading (Calvin, Bruce, Peterson) takes both events as God's initiative through different means — sovereign vision in the one case, providential dispersion in the other. The Pentecostal reading (Stronstad, Menzies) emphasizes the parallelism of Spirit-events: the Spirit drives both Peter and the unnamed Hellenists. The Catholic reading (especially as developed in the conciliar documents of Vatican II) takes the chapter as evidence that ecclesial authority and charismatic initiative must work together — Peter's vision must be ratified by Jerusalem, but Jerusalem must be willing to send a Barnabas to discern what the Spirit is doing through unnamed believers at Antioch. Steelmanned, each reading captures something the others miss: the Cornelius account *is* a divine intervention narrative; the Antiochene founding *is* a lay-driven contextual judgment; and both *are* held under apostolic ratification. **Where this leaves us**: Acts 11 refuses to choose between divine initiative, apostolic authority, and lay agency. The breakthrough comes through all three operating simultaneously, and any ecclesiology that suppresses one of the three is reading against the chapter.

**Tension 2: What is the relationship between Jerusalem and Antioch?** The chapter establishes Antioch as the second center of the movement but leaves the constitutional relationship undefined. Three positions have shaped the tradition. The **Roman Catholic position**, articulated most clearly in *Lumen Gentium* and developed by **Yves Congar** in *Tradition and Traditions* (Burns & Oates, 1966), sees Acts 11 as evidence of a hierarchical structure in which apostolic see (Jerusalem, then by Petrine succession Rome) ratifies and authorizes mission churches. The **Eastern Orthodox position**, articulated by figures like **John Zizioulas** in *Being as Communion* (St Vladimir's, 1985), reads the same data as evidence of a conciliar-eucharistic ecclesiology in which each local church is fully church but bound to the others through synodality. The **Free Church / congregational position** reads Antioch's relative autonomy — the lay-initiated mission, the local discernment, the eventual sending of Paul and Barnabas without Jerusalem's prior authorization in chapter 13 — as evidence that local communities possess the same authority as the Jerusalem church, with translocal coordination being voluntary and consultative rather than constitutive.

Steelmanned: the Catholic reading captures Luke's deliberate refusal to let Antioch act without Jerusalem's blessing (Barnabas is sent, the relief is returned, the Council will be held). The Orthodox reading captures the genuine reciprocity of the relationship (it is not one-way deference). The Free Church reading captures the bottom-up character of the Antiochene founding and the structural fact that Jerusalem follows rather than leads the breakthrough. **Where this leaves us**: Acts 11 sketches a relationship that later church history will systematize in incompatible ways. The chapter itself preserves the productive tension between centralized authority and local agency without resolving it. *Unresolved question*: When Luke writes "Christians" at 11:26 in the passive voice ("were called"), does he intend the name as divinely-given, outsider-imposed, or community-embraced — and does the answer have constitutional weight for how Christian identity is established and policed?

## The Thread

Acts 11 reaches forward and backward across the canon. The vision of the great sheet (recapitulated at 11:5–10) gathers up the dietary laws of **Leviticus 11** and **Deuteronomy 14** and reads them under a new eschatological key. The Antiochene relief sent by Barnabas and Saul (11:29–30) anticipates the great Pauline collection theology developed in **Romans 15:25–28**, **1 Corinthians 16:1–4**, and **2 Corinthians 8–9**, where Gentile churches contribute to the Jerusalem poor as a tangible enactment of the unity of the body. The naming at Antioch (11:26) connects to Peter's later usage in **1 Peter 4:16** ("if anyone suffers as a Christian, let him not be ashamed"), where the name has already become a recognized public designation under persecution. And the Agabus famine prophecy (11:27–28) prepares the reader for Agabus's later prophecy of Paul's binding at **Acts 21:10–11**. The chronological problem the chapter introduces — Pauline visits to Jerusalem — will need to be read against **Galatians 1:18–2:10**, the most acute parallel in the New Testament canon, and the question of how to harmonize Acts 11:30 with Galatians 2:1 will travel with us through chapters 12 and 15.
