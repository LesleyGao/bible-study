# 2 Samuel 1: The Lying Amalekite and the Song of the Bow

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

Second Samuel opens in the political vacuum left by Saul's death on Mount Gilboa, an event whose archaeological and political reverberations frame the entire David narrative. The chapter's setting at Ziklag — a Philistine vassal town in the Negev given to David by Achish of Gath (1 Sam 27:6) — locates David in a precarious geopolitical position. Anson Rainey identified Ziklag tentatively with Tel Sera (Tell esh-Shari'ah) in the western Negev, though Tel Halif and Tel es-Seba remain candidates. The town's status as a Philistine grant matters: David is technically a Philistine retainer at the moment news arrives that the Philistines have broken Israel's army at Jezreel. The dramatic geography is fundamental — Gilboa lies in the north, Ziklag in the deep south. The Amalekite messenger has traveled roughly 80 miles through hostile territory in three days.

The Amalekites themselves are not a neutral ethnic marker here. They are Israel's archetypal enemy from Exodus 17, the people Saul was commanded to exterminate in 1 Samuel 15 and whose sparing cost him the throne. David has just returned (1:1) from striking down an Amalekite raiding party that had burned Ziklag (1 Sam 30). For an Amalekite "sojourner" (*ger*) to claim he killed the LORD's anointed produces an irony that ancient hearers would not miss. As Robert Alter notes in *The David Story*, the narrator builds the Amalekite's claim on a foundation of accumulated symbolic weight: the very nation Saul was supposed to have annihilated returns at the end to claim authorship of his death.

Material culture details ground the scene. The "crown" (*nezer*) and "armlet" (*ets'adah*) the Amalekite brings (1:10) reflect Late Bronze and Iron Age I royal regalia known from Egyptian iconography and Levantine finds. The *nezer* is not the later Israelite *keter* but a diadem associated with consecration; the *ets'adah* is an upper-arm bracelet of the type recovered from Tell es-Sa'idiyeh and other Iron Age tombs. These objects function as transferable tokens of sovereignty — exactly what the Amalekite hopes will purchase his future.

Five Hebrew terms drive the chapter:

1. **קִינָה** (*qinah*) — formal lament, the genre of 1:17–27. The qinah meter (3+2 stresses) is the diagnostic dirge cadence found also in Lamentations and parts of Amos. Hedwig Jahnow's 1923 *Das hebräische Leichenlied* remains foundational on the form.

2. **מָשִׁיחַ יְהוָה** (*meshiach YHWH*) — "the LORD's anointed" (1:14, 16). The construct phrase is doctrinally weighted: David's argument for executing the Amalekite is that no oil-anointed king can be killed without bloodguilt, even by the king's own request.

3. **גֵּר** (*ger*) — "sojourner" (1:13). The Amalekite identifies himself as a resident-alien, not a foreigner proper. This complicates the legal status of his execution and intensifies the ironic reversal: a *ger* in Israel who kills the *meshiach*.

4. **צִנּוֹר** does not appear here but **צְבִי** (*tsevi*) — "glory/gazelle" (1:19) does. The Hebrew is famously ambiguous: "Your gazelle, O Israel, on your high places lies slain" or "Your beauty/glory." McCarter and Cross-Freedman read *tsevi* as an archaic poetic image of Saul as the swift, doomed deer.

5. **בָּמָה** (*bamah*) — "high place" (1:19, 25). Both cultic and topographic; here primarily geographic (the heights of Gilboa) but with cultic resonance — Saul died on a place that should have been holy.

Archaeologically, Mount Gilboa itself remains lightly excavated, but the broader Beth-Shean / Jezreel system has been worked extensively (Yigael Yadin, Amihai Mazar, Norma Franklin). The Philistine occupation of Beth-Shean in the late eleventh century — including the public display of Saul's body on its walls (1 Sam 31:10) — is consistent with the Philistine pottery and architectural horizon documented at Tel Beth-Shean Stratum VI (Mazar excavations 1989–96). Whether the Amalekite scout was plausibly on Gilboa is harder to demonstrate; but Amalekite mobility through the Negev highlands and Edomite frontier is itself well attested in late-second-millennium and early-first-millennium texts.

## Lens 2 — The Literature

The chapter is bipartite. Verses 1–16 are prose narrative in the apologetic mode of the History of David's Rise (Rost 1926; refined by McCarter, *II Samuel*, Anchor Bible). Verses 17–27 are the famous *Song of the Bow*, formal lament poetry of unusual antiquity.

The prose section is built on a courtroom structure: report, interrogation, cross-examination, sentence, execution. The Amalekite's testimony unravels under David's questioning. The narrator has already given the reader the truth in 1 Samuel 31 — Saul fell on his own sword, his armor-bearer following him in death — and so the Amalekite's account comes pre-marked as a lie. The literary effect is dramatic irony of the harshest kind: the Amalekite expects reward; the reader expects judgment. McCarter and Halpern (*David's Secret Demons*) read the Amalekite either as a deliberate competing tradition (one source had Saul fall on his sword; another had a finishing blow) or as simple opportunistic fabrication. Halpern's apologetic reading is decisive: the chapter exists in part to show that David did not kill Saul, did not authorize Saul's killing, and indeed punished any claim to have done so. The Amalekite is the apologetic foil — the false claimant whose execution proves David's loyalty to the throne he is about to inherit.

The Song of the Bow (1:19–27) is among the most-discussed poetic units in the Hebrew Bible. The qinah meter dominates. Cross and Freedman (*Studies in Ancient Yahwistic Poetry*, 1975) argue from archaic features — orthography preserved beneath later updating, parallelism patterns, vocabulary — that the lament is genuinely early, possibly Davidic in origin. Their position has held up well; even minimalist scholars rarely deny the poem's antiquity. The lament is preserved as drawn from "the Book of Jashar" (*sefer ha-yashar*, 1:18), an evidently lost early Israelite poetic anthology also cited in Joshua 10:13 and (via the LXX) at 1 Kings 8:53. The Book of Jashar functioned as a curated repository of national poetry; the Samuel author treats his readers as needing only a citation, suggesting that his audience knew the source.

Devices throughout: the threefold refrain *eikh naflu gibborim* — "How the mighty have fallen" (1:19, 25, 27) — frames and divides the poem. The merism "swifter than eagles, stronger than lions" (1:23) yokes speed and power. The negative invocation upon Gilboa (1:21) — denial of dew, rain, and offerings — is a curse-pattern with parallels in Ugaritic (Aqhat's curse on the cities near where his son fell). Cross drew the parallel directly. The address to Philistine cities Gath and Ashkelon (1:20) is taunt-inversion: instead of Israel singing *Saul has slain his thousands and David his ten thousands* (1 Sam 18:7), the Philistine daughters threaten to celebrate Saul's death, and the lament asks even that song be silenced.

Textually, Samuel is the most variable book in the Hebrew Bible. **4QSam-a** (4Q51) frequently agrees with the LXX Vorlage against MT, and Cross's hypothesis of a "Palestinian" textual family has shaped the field. In 2 Samuel 1, the lament has a number of meaning-affecting variants. At 1:21, the MT has *bly mshuwh ba-shamen* — "not anointed with oil" — but Cross and others read with the LXX a different syntactic break that produces *bly mshuwh, mageinim ba-shamen* — "shield not anointed, shields with oil." The vocalization affects whether Saul's shield (singular) or the warriors' shields (plural) are in view. At 1:18 ("teach the sons of Judah the bow"), the MT *qeshet* ("bow") is read by some scholars as a secondary insertion; the LXX has a different reading, and McCarter follows a reconstructed original referring not to the bow as instrument but to the song being titled "The Bow." The variation at 1:19 — *ha-tsevi yisra'el al-bamoteikha halal* — has produced perhaps a dozen English renderings, since *tsevi* may be "glory," "beauty," "gazelle," or even (per some) a place name.

Within the book's argument, chapter 1 sits at the conclusion of Rost's "History of David's Rise" (1 Sam 16 – 2 Sam 5). It is the hinge: Saul is dead, the throne is open, but David is not yet king. The lament establishes David's grief as authentic and his loyalty as proven. Polzin (*David and the Deuteronomist*) sees the lament as the Deuteronomist's careful framing of the transition: David honors what he replaces. Fokkelman's literary analysis (vol. 2 of *Narrative Art and Poetry in the Books of Samuel*) maps how the poem's three "How the mighty have fallen" refrains structure a private grief that is also a public political document.

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

In the canonical arc, this chapter functions as the formal end of Saul's tragedy and the first stage of David's accession. The Davidic covenant of 2 Samuel 7 is still six chapters away; the chapter establishes David's moral standing in the most contested terrain — his treatment of his predecessor.

**Jewish interpretive trajectory.** Josephus (*Ant.* 7.1) recasts the Amalekite's story in the direction of plausibility: he flatly accepts the Amalekite's account of finishing Saul off, and explains David's execution of him as punishment for raising a hand against a king. Rabbinic tradition is more skeptical. The *Talmud* (*b. Yebamot* 77b) and Rashi treat the Amalekite as a liar whose own mouth condemned him. Radak (David Kimhi) reads David's question "How is it you were not afraid?" as forensic — the Amalekite's testimony is presumptively true for purposes of judgment, but the rabbinic tradition itself doubts the underlying claim. Ralbag (Gersonides) reflects on the moral question: even if the Amalekite told the truth, finishing off the LORD's anointed at the king's request is bloodguilt. Abarbanel, writing in the late fifteenth century, develops this into a full apologetic for David: the Amalekite's plea — "I was sure he could not live" — is itself the confession of an unjustified killing, since mercy-killing is not in the killer's prerogative when the victim is the king. Yair Zakovitch's modern Hebrew commentary tracks the multiple narrative layers and the apologetic shape. Moshe Garsiel (*The First Book of Samuel: A Literary Study*, with comparative work in 2 Samuel) emphasizes the chapter's role in the broader Saul-David typology. Avivah Zornberg, more meditatively, has read the lament as David's first encounter with the political theology of grief — what it costs to mourn the king who tried to kill you.

**Christian interpretive trajectory.** Patristic readings cluster around two themes: David's mercy-as-justice and the typology of the anointed one. Origen (*Homilies on Samuel*, fragmentary) reads the Amalekite's execution as a figure of the soul's rejection of false claims to spiritual authority. Augustine, in *City of God* book 17, treats David's accession as a stage in the unfolding of the earthly city's relation to the heavenly — Saul's failed kingship becoming the condition for the prophetic kingship that points to Christ. Ambrose's *De Officiis* (book 1) draws on the Song of the Bow as a model of magnanimity: the Christian leader, like David, mourns enemies as well as friends, and rejects political windfalls that come at the cost of innocent blood. Chrysostom's homilies on David emphasize the king's grief as moral instruction. Gregory the Great in the *Moralia in Job* reads the lament's refrain as a meditation on the universal vulnerability of even the strongest. Aquinas, in the *Summa Theologiae* (II-II, q. 64), uses the chapter as a test case in his analysis of when killing the dying is murder — concluding with the rabbis that consent does not extinguish the offense. Calvin's sermons on 2 Samuel (preserved in the *Supplementa Calviniana* series, recovered and edited in the twentieth century by the Genève reformers) treat David as a mirror for godly magistrates: a prince who refuses to capitalize on the death of a rival demonstrates that political authority must be received, not seized. Luther's marginal comments on the lament emphasize its sincerity over its politics.

Critical scholarship has emphasized the apologetic function: Wellhausen first identified the chapter as part of David's house propaganda; Cross refined the source-critical picture; McCarter's Anchor Bible commentary is the standard treatment, building on McCarthy and on the Rost source-hypothesis. A. A. Anderson's WBC volume on 2 Samuel develops the apologetic reading further. Brueggemann's commentaries treat the chapter as the Deuteronomist's careful management of David's image — and ask whether the management is too careful. Polzin (*David and the Deuteronomist* vol. 3) argues for a deliberate Deuteronomistic shaping. Halpern (*David's Secret Demons*, 2001) reads the apology forensically: David benefits, David never lifts a hand, David always punishes those who do — a pattern across chapters 1, 4, 16, and 21 that begins to look like authorial defense. McKenzie (*King David: A Biography*, 2000) follows a similar line. Eugene Peterson (*Leap Over a Wall*) reads the lament devotionally; Ellen Davis and John Goldingay treat David's grief as theologically genuine within an apologetic frame.

Feminist criticism is substantial here. Cheryl Exum, in *Tragedy and Biblical Narrative*, attends to the women who structure the lament — the Philistine daughters, the daughters of Israel — and notes how the public/political grief uses women as media of memory. Phyllis Trible's broader work on the function of women in the David narratives applies. Mieke Bal's *Murder and Difference* treats Saulide and Davidic narrative women as foils; Athalya Brenner's edited volumes include sustained engagement with the lament. Alice Bach reads the Song of the Bow as a male-coded grief whose final verse displaces women entirely (1:26).

That final verse — "your love to me was extraordinary, surpassing the love of women" — is the most contested in the chapter. The **homoerotic reading**, developed at length by Theodore Jennings (*Jacob's Wound*, 2005), Silvia Schroer, and Susan Ackerman, takes 1:26 plus the earlier scenes of Jonathan's love for David (1 Sam 18:1–4, 20:41) as evidence of an erotic bond that the narrator did not erase. The **covenantal-political reading**, defended by Halpern, McCarter, Alter, Brueggemann, and David Damrosch, reads the language as the formal vocabulary of treaty love — *ahavah* in the David-Jonathan narrative carries the sense of covenant loyalty familiar from Hittite and Assyrian treaty texts (Moran 1963 on covenant *ahavah*). Saul Olyan splits the difference: the language of love is conventional treaty language, but the conventional language can also do erotic work in particular contexts. Rabbinic tradition spiritualizes the verse (the love of Torah-companions). Patristic tradition reads it as the love of a holy friendship (Aelred of Rievaulx's twelfth-century *Spiritual Friendship* uses David and Jonathan as a model). Contemporary scholarship has not converged. The most honest reading admits that the verse can sustain either interpretation and that the forced resolution in either direction is a category mistake about ancient affect.

## Lens 4 — The Theological Intent

**Tension 1: Was the Amalekite's execution just?** The chapter offers David's reasoning explicitly — the Amalekite condemned himself with his own mouth (1:16). But three positions complicate this.

*Position A: The execution was strict justice.* The Amalekite, by his own testimony, killed the LORD's anointed; the law of regicide is unambiguous; Saul's status as *meshiach YHWH* is not nullified by his rejection in 1 Samuel 15. This is Aquinas's reading and the standard rabbinic position. Halpern's apologetic frame agrees that the execution is *legally* justified; he just notes that it is also politically convenient.

*Position B: The execution was apologetic theater.* Brueggemann and Polzin steelman this: David benefits enormously from Saul's death and from the death of the witness who could later testify to having been rewarded by David. Whether the Amalekite was telling the truth or lying, his execution removes both possibilities from public conversation. The chapter's careful framing — David weeping, fasting, lamenting before executing — is precisely the framing the apologist needs.

*Position C: The execution dramatizes the inviolability of sacral kingship even at the cost of mercy.* This is the Calvinist reading and, in a different idiom, Eugene Peterson's: the office of the LORD's anointed is not extinguished by failure or even by the king's own consent to die; David's act establishes the political theology that will protect him later (1 Sam 24, 26 — the cave and the camp scenes — function in retrospect).

The steelman of position B is sharpest. The Deuteronomist is not naive about David's interests, and the chapter's structure does serve them. But the steelman of A and C is that the chapter establishes a doctrine — sacral kingship is not at the disposal of the king himself — that constrains David in subsequent chapters as well as protecting him. He cannot kill Ish-bosheth either (chapter 4), and the same logic returns when Joab kills Abner (chapter 3). The doctrine cuts both ways.

*Where this leaves us:* The apologetic function and the theological seriousness are not opposites. The Deuteronomist crafted a text that both protects David and binds him. The Amalekite's execution is theologically generative: it makes regicide unthinkable in a way that will matter when David is the regicide-target.

**Tension 2: How can David genuinely lament the man who tried for years to kill him?** The Song of the Bow expresses unfeigned grief for Saul, which strains psychological credulity if read flatly.

*Position A: The grief is performance.* Halpern's hardest reading: the lament is political theater, beautifully crafted, sincerely composed, and entirely strategic. David needs Saul mourned by Saul's own future replacement. This does not require David to be cynical; it requires the political and personal to be inseparable.

*Position B: The grief is real because the bond was real.* This is Alter's and Peterson's reading. Whatever Saul became, David served Saul, played for Saul, married Saul's daughter, befriended Saul's son. The lament refuses to flatten the relationship into its end-state. The grief is the truth that political enmity could not destroy.

*Position C: The grief is liturgical and exceeds David's psychology.* Brueggemann moves here: the qinah is a public act of national mourning that David, as future king, is obliged to lead. His private feelings are not the question. Israel needs Saul mourned, and David is the only figure in position to do it without insincerity *or* triumphalism.

*Where this leaves us:* The most compelling reading combines B and C. The grief is real and the lament is a public office. The two are not in tension; they are mutually reinforcing. The Deuteronomist gives us a David capable of holding both at once.

**Unresolved question:** What does the chapter do with the fact that the lament is preserved at all? The "Book of Jashar" citation acknowledges that this poem entered Israel's curated memory — was taught to Judah, was carried as national property. The strange political effect is that David's grief over Saul becomes pedagogically required for every Judahite generation. What does it mean for a polity to require its founder's lament for the man he replaced to be memorized?

## The Thread

The Amalekite returns at the end of the Pentateuchal-historical arc. Saul was commanded in 1 Samuel 15 to destroy Amalek, and his sparing of Agag cost him the throne; in 2 Samuel 1, an Amalekite *ger* claims authorship of Saul's death. The narrative is closing a loop opened in Exodus 17 and Deuteronomy 25.

The Song of the Bow's refrain "How the mighty have fallen" is drawn on later in the book — and in Israel's memory more generally — as a template for political grief. Compare 2 Samuel 3:33–34 (David's lament for Abner) and 18:33 (David's lament for Absalom). The chapter establishes a Davidic vocabulary of public mourning that the rest of 2 Samuel will use four more times.

The doctrine of the inviolable *meshiach* echoes forward to chapter 4 (David's execution of Rechab and Baanah for killing Ish-bosheth) and chapter 21 (the Gibeonites' demand for Saulide blood, which David grants — but only after careful theological hedging). The chapter establishes the rule whose hardest test will be the Gibeonite affair.

Flag forward: the homoerotic/covenantal question at 1:26 will not be resolved in 2 Samuel and will be reread through every later treatment of David and Jonathan in Jewish, Christian, and modern queer-theological reception. The text does not settle the question; it preserves it.
