# Joshua 24: Choose This Day — And Three Graves at Shechem

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

The setting is Shechem, the biblical city whose archaeological identification with Tell Balâṭah is secure and which was one of the most significant urban centers of Middle and Late Bronze Age Canaan. G. Ernest Wright's Drew-McCormick excavations at Tell Balâṭah from 1956 through the 1960s (and Edward Campbell's continuation) exposed substantial Middle Bronze IIC fortifications with a cyclopean wall and a massive migdal-temple — likely the Temple of El-Berith ("God of the Covenant") mentioned in Judges 9:46, where the Shechemites take refuge from Abimelech. The site sits in the pass between Mount Ebal and Mount Gerizim, a natural amphitheater for large assemblies, which is presumably why Joshua 8:30-35 locates the earlier Ebal-Gerizim covenant ceremony there and why chapter 24 returns to the same region for the climactic covenant renewal. The "terebinth that was by the sanctuary of the LORD" (24:26) recalls the ancient oak of Moreh near Shechem where Abraham built his first Canaanite altar (Gen 12:6-7), and Jacob's altar at Shechem (Gen 33:18-20) where he buried the foreign gods under the oak (Gen 35:2-4). The chapter is reaching back across the patriarchal narrative to the foundational cultic site.

Shechem's political significance continues past Joshua. Abimelech's brief kingship (Judg 9) is centered there. Rehoboam goes to Shechem for his coronation and loses the northern kingdom there (1 Kgs 12). The post-exilic Samaritan community builds its rival temple on Mount Gerizim. Jesus's conversation with the Samaritan woman at Sychar (John 4:5-6) happens "near the field that Jacob had given to his son Joseph" — the same field referenced in Joshua 24:32, where Joseph's bones are buried. The continuity of site across two millennia is unusual in biblical geography.

The material culture of the ceremony fits an Iron I or early Iron II Israelite covenant ritual. Standing stones (*maṣṣebôt*) are attested throughout Canaanite and early Israelite practice; Tell el-'Umayri in Transjordan has produced Iron I standing-stone contexts. The "large stone" Joshua sets up under the terebinth (24:26) functions as a witness-object in the same way the commemorative altar of chapter 22 did — physical memory-storage for a verbal covenant. Late Bronze Hittite treaty tablets were typically deposited in temples with periodic public readings; Joshua 24's combination of stone-deposit and book-deposit (24:26) echoes both Hittite practice and Deuteronomy 31:26.

Figure disambiguation matters at three points. Phinehas here (24:33, implicitly, as the owner of Gibeah where his father Eleazar is buried) is Phinehas ben Eleazar, the grandson of Aaron and the zealot of Numbers 25 — not Phinehas ben Eli of 1 Samuel 1-4. Eleazar son of Aaron (24:33) is the high priest throughout Joshua, distinct from Eleazar son of Abinadab who kept the Ark in 1 Samuel 7:1. Joshua son of Nun (24:29) is not any of the other Joshuas in the biblical record. "Shechem" at 24:32, "the sons of Hamor father of Shechem," references the Shechem episode of Genesis 34 and Hamor the Hivite — the city takes its name from the person.

Five key terms carry the chapter's theological freight. First, *bāḥar* (בָּחַר), "choose" — the verb of election. It appears seven times in Deuteronomy and is usually YHWH's action (Deut 7:6-7; 14:2). In Joshua 24:15 ("choose this day whom you will serve") and 24:22 ("you have chosen the LORD"), the verb is reversed: the people are invited to choose YHWH. This chiastic use of *bāḥar* is theologically striking. Second, *ʿābad* (עָבַד), "serve, worship" — the chapter's master verb, appearing 17 times. The choice is not between believing and not believing but between serving and serving. Everyone serves something. Third, *ʿēd* (עֵד), "witness" (24:22, 27) — the same forensic term that dominated chapter 22. Here the witnesses are the people themselves ("you are witnesses against yourselves") and the standing stone. Fourth, *bĕrît* (בְּרִית), "covenant" (24:25) — the chapter's formal genre-term. The cognate verb phrase *kārat bĕrît* ("cut a covenant") reflects the ancient Near Eastern practice of ratifying treaties by cutting sacrificial animals. Fifth, *qannôʾ* (קַנּוֹא), "jealous" (24:19) — a divine attribute-word used in the Decalogue (Exod 20:5). The chapter makes the jealousy of YHWH a logical consequence of covenant: an exclusive relationship entails exclusive demand. Joshua's warning that "you are not able to serve the LORD, for he is a holy God" (24:19) is one of the Hebrew Bible's most counterintuitive moments — the leader talks the people *out* of covenant before letting them in.

## Lens 2 — The Literature

The genre is a *covenant renewal ceremony* with an embedded *Heilsgeschichte* recitation and a *covenant lawsuit* structure. The chapter is the classical exemplar of the form George Mendenhall identified in his landmark 1954 essay "Covenant Forms in Israelite Tradition" in the *Biblical Archaeologist*. Mendenhall compared the fourteenth- and thirteenth-century BCE Hittite suzerainty treaties with biblical covenants and identified six recurring elements: (1) preamble identifying the great king; (2) historical prologue rehearsing the king's past benefactions; (3) stipulations demanding loyalty; (4) provisions for deposit and periodic reading of the document; (5) list of witnesses; (6) blessings and curses. Klaus Baltzer's *Das Bundesformular* (1960, ET *The Covenant Formulary*, 1971) systematized the typology. Moshe Weinfeld's *Deuteronomy and the Deuteronomic School* (1972) pressed the comparison into the Deuteronomic texts specifically. Klaus Koch's form-critical work (*Was ist Formgeschichte?*) provided the methodological underpinning.

Joshua 24 is the classic exemplar because it maps the form almost perfectly. Preamble: "Thus says the LORD, the God of Israel" (24:2), naming the suzerain. Historical prologue: 24:2-13, a sweeping rehearsal from Abraham's Mesopotamian origins through the conquest — one of the longest salvation-history recitals in the Hebrew Bible. Stipulations: 24:14, "fear the LORD and serve him in sincerity and in faithfulness... put away the gods... choose this day whom you will serve." Document deposit: 24:26, "Joshua wrote these words in the Book of the Law of God." Witnesses: 24:22, "you are witnesses against yourselves"; 24:27, the stone "has heard all the words of the LORD that he spoke to us" and "shall be a witness." Blessings and curses: implicit in 24:19-20. The correspondence is too close to be coincidence; the chapter is deliberately formulated as a suzerainty treaty with YHWH as the great king.

The historical prologue itself is worth close reading. It begins with a startling admission: "Long ago, your fathers lived beyond the Euphrates, Terah, the father of Abraham and of Nahor; and they served other gods" (24:2). This is the Hebrew Bible's most candid statement that the patriarchs were not born monotheists — that Abraham came out of polytheistic Mesopotamia. The admission is then balanced by the even more startling reference to "the gods that your fathers served... in Egypt" (24:14) — suggesting that the wilderness generation, too, carried idols. The LXX of 24:14 reads slightly differently, but the basic admission is intact. No other passage in the Pentateuch or Joshua says this as bluntly.

The literary structure is a dialogical drama in three rounds. Round one (24:14-18): Joshua invites, the people accept. Round two (24:19-21): Joshua refuses the easy acceptance, warns them of YHWH's jealousy, and the people accept again. Round three (24:22-24): Joshua extracts a formal self-witnessing oath, the people commit a third time. The chapter stages consent as a process, not a single utterance. Von Rad and others have noted that this three-round structure parallels legal-procedural oaths in the ancient Near East, where repetition guards against later repudiation.

The textual variants in Joshua 24 are more significant than in chapters 22 or 23. The LXX contains an expansion at 24:33a (lacking in the MT) describing how the Israelites carried off the Ark to Phinehas's town after Eleazar's death — a harmonization with the Phinehas-at-Shiloh traditions. The LXX also has different verbal forms in the historical prologue in several places. Qumran fragment 4QJosh^a preserves a text closer to LXX in some readings and reorders some material, including the sequence of events around the early altar-building of Joshua 8:30-35; scholars including Eugene Ulrich and Emanuel Tov have argued that 4QJosh^a may preserve an older textual tradition than MT. These variants matter because they show that Joshua 24's composition history was live into the late Second Temple period.

In the book's macro-structure, chapter 24 is the climax. Martin Noth argued in 1943 that Joshua 24 was the original ending of the Deuteronomistic History's Joshua material — the cities of refuge (ch 20), the Levitical cities (ch 21), the altar dispute (ch 22), and the first farewell (ch 23) were all secondary additions reframing the core. Lothar Perlitt's *Bundestheologie im Alten Testament* (1969) challenged the claim of a pre-Deuteronomic Shechem amphictyony underlying the chapter, arguing that covenant theology itself is a Deuteronomic construct. The debate persists: whether Joshua 24 preserves a very old Shechem tradition (so Noth, Mendenhall) or is a late Deuteronomic composition projecting covenant theology backward (so Perlitt).

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

**Jewish trajectory.** Second Temple literature reveres Joshua 24. The Qumran *Apocryphon of Joshua* (4Q378, 4Q379) preserves expanded speech material that likely draws on the covenant ceremony. Philo treats the Shechem covenant in his typically allegorical mode — the soul's triple renunciation of the passions. Josephus (*Antiquities* 5.1.28-29) paraphrases the chapter, emphasizing Joshua's rhetorical effectiveness and his burial at Thamna (Timnath-serah). The Samaritan tradition, preserved in the Samaritan Pentateuch and later Samaritan chronicles, treats Joshua 24's Shechem ceremony as foundational to their claim that Gerizim is the true chosen place — and indeed, the weight of evidence in the text itself (Shechem as Abraham's first altar, Jacob's altar, Joseph's burial) makes the Samaritan reading textually defensible in a way later polemic often obscured.

Rabbinic tradition mines the chapter for covenant theology. *Sotah* 11a-13b treats the collective covenant moment as paradigmatic. *Bava Batra* 117b reads Joshua's modest grave at Timnath-serah as a theological statement — the conquest leader chose obscure hill country rather than a royal tomb; *Tanhuma* and *Bereshit Rabbah* amplify the theme. *Pirkei Avot* tradition treats 24:15 ("as for me and my house") as the prototype of household commitment amid communal apostasy. The Mechilta and *Sifre* treat the three-round dialogue as the legal pattern for how covenant is contracted.

Rashi on 24:2 reads Abraham's journey out of Ur as the type of every later conversion. Ibn Ezra is more philological, noting that "beyond the River" consistently means beyond the Euphrates. Ramban treats the Shechem covenant as a renewal of the Sinai covenant at a new generational moment. Radak on 24:19-20 addresses the pastoral challenge of Joshua's warning by reading it as a test of seriousness. Abarbanel, writing after the Spanish expulsion, treats Joshua 24 as the archetype of every later covenant renewal in Jewish history. Maimonides in the *Mishneh Torah* cites Joshua 24:22 as the classical source for the voluntary nature of covenant — covenant cannot be coerced, even by the covenant-maker.

Modern Jewish readers have engaged the chapter deeply. Martin Buber in *The Prophetic Faith* treats Joshua 24 as the pivotal moment of Israelite self-constitution. Heschel treats the divine jealousy of 24:19 as the sign of YHWH's personal involvement. Jon Levenson (*Sinai and Zion*, 1985) treats Joshua 24 as exemplifying the dialectic between the Mosaic covenant (conditional, mutual) and the Davidic/Abrahamic covenants (unconditional, divine). Nahum Sarna and Moshe Weinfeld wrote substantially on the chapter's covenant form. James Kugel tracks its Second Temple reception. Avivah Zornberg reads the Shechem dialogue as encounter with resistance. Halpern and Rachel Havrelock (*River Jordan*) treat the chapter as the Jordan-crossing's proper endpoint — the tribes are not fully Israel until they have gathered and chosen.

**Christian trajectory.** Origen's *Homilies on Joshua*, particularly the final homilies (he preaches 26 in all), treat Joshua 24 as the climactic moment of the book and of the book's typology. For Origen, Joshua son of Nun — the *Iēsous* of the Septuagint — prefigures Jesus Christ, and the Shechem covenant prefigures the baptismal covenant by which the Christian chooses Christ. Origen's final homily ties the three-round dialogue of Joshua 24 to the three-fold baptismal renunciation of Satan. Jerome's Latin translation preserves the covenant vocabulary that shapes Western theology. Augustine in *City of God* 17.1 and elsewhere treats the Shechem covenant as a prefiguration of the eternal covenant in Christ and notes the theological depth of 24:2 — that Abraham was called out of idolatry, not out of natural piety. Chrysostom's homilies on related texts emphasize 24:15's "as for me and my house" as the Christian householder's prerogative. Theodoret of Cyrus in his *Questions on Joshua* treats the chapter as the exemplar of covenant piety.

Medieval readings extend these trajectories. Aquinas cites Joshua 24:15 as a proof-text for the voluntary character of religious commitment under revealed covenant. The *Glossa Ordinaria* reads Shechem as the Church, the stone as Christ the cornerstone, the oak as the cross. Hugh of St. Victor integrates the chapter into his threefold scheme of covenant (natural, written, incarnate).

Luther treats chapter 24 as the Old Testament's clearest instance of law-and-promise theology — the historical prologue is gospel, the stipulations are law, and Joshua's warning in 24:19 drives the hearer back to gospel. Luther reads "you are not able to serve the LORD" as a proto-Pauline confession of incapacity. Calvin's sermons on Joshua climax at chapter 24; his Shechem-covenant reading has shaped every subsequent Reformed covenantal tradition. Menno Simons reads 24:15 as the foundational text for believer's baptism — covenant is individual choice, not inherited status.

Critical scholarship on Joshua 24 is immense. Noth 1943 identified the chapter as the original climax of the Deuteronomistic Joshua material. Von Rad (1951) treated it as the pre-monarchic sacral league's self-definition. Mendenhall 1954 placed Joshua 24 at the center of Hittite-treaty comparisons; Baltzer systematized the form. Cross 1973 integrated the chapter into dual-redaction DH. Brevard Childs's canonical approach read it as the theological fulcrum of the Hexateuch. Nelson's OTL (1997), Dozeman's Anchor Yale (2015), and Butler's WBC treatments develop the covenant-formulary approach. Lothar Perlitt's *Bundestheologie im Alten Testament* (1969) pressed the counter-case: covenant theology is a Deuteronomic construct and Joshua 24's "old" covenant is not as old as Mendenhall thought.

Contemporary readers are varied. Brueggemann (*Theology of the Old Testament*, 1997) treats Joshua 24 as the decisive act of Israel's self-constitution. N. T. Wright's *The New Testament and the People of God* uses Joshua 24 to reconstruct Second Temple covenant theology. Goldingay's *Old Testament Theology* treats it as covenant renewal par excellence. Hess, Wenham, and David Howard Jr. provide evangelical treatments; Fretheim reads it through divine pathos. Ellen Davis treats the land-gift language (24:13) as agrarian-theological. Hawk's *Every Promise Fulfilled* takes the chapter as the DH's final statement. Rowlett's *Joshua and the Rhetoric of Violence* reads the ceremony as a discourse of central authority. On the postcolonial and liberation side, Warrior's 1989 "A Native American Perspective" presses the question: the Canaanite voice is entirely absent from the historical prologue, and its absence is constitutive. Dube and Kwok Pui-lan amplify it: the people "witnessing against themselves" are the winners of a conquest whose losers have no voice at the ceremony.

The chapter ends with three graves: Joshua at Timnath-serah (24:29-30), Joseph's bones at Shechem (24:32), Eleazar at Gibeah of Phinehas (24:33). Joseph's bones fulfill the vow of Genesis 50:25 nearly half a millennium later — an act of memory spanning Pentateuch and Joshua together.

## Lens 4 — The Theological Intent

**Tension one: chosen-ness and human choice.** *Position one (prior election)*: the entire historical prologue (24:2-13) is about what YHWH has done. Abraham did not choose; he was taken. The wilderness generation did not choose; they were brought out. The conquest was not won; it was given. "Choose this day" at 24:15 is therefore not the foundation of the covenant but its consequence — Israel chooses only what it has already been chosen for. Augustine and later Calvin press this reading. *Position two (genuine choice)*: the chapter's rhetorical structure insists that the choice is real. The three rounds of dialogue, the self-witnessing oath, the stone-as-witness-against-the-people, all presuppose that the choice could have gone otherwise. Maimonides's reading of 24:22 as evidence of covenant voluntariness is serious. Menno Simons and the Anabaptists press this reading. *Position three (the steelman — least intuitive)*: the either/or framing misreads the chapter. The historical prologue and the moment of choice are not two different theologies of covenant; they are the same theology described from the inside and the outside. From the outside (YHWH's perspective), the entire history is YHWH's action and the choice is the last link in a chain. From the inside (Israel's perspective), the history is already given and the only thing still unsettled is whether the community will take it up. Both descriptions are true; neither can be reduced to the other. The chapter refuses the modern dichotomy between divine sovereignty and human freedom by staging a ritual in which both are simultaneously operative and neither is a threat to the other. *Where this leaves us*: the chapter does not tell us how to resolve the problem philosophically. It tells us that the covenant is cut at the point where divine action and human choice meet in a public, witnessed, irrevocable commitment. The theological work happens in the ritual, not in the metaphysics.

**Tension two: covenant as freedom and covenant as constraint.** *Position one (freedom)*: the entire covenant rhetoric of Joshua 24 is liberatory. The historical prologue is an exodus narrative — from Terah's idolatry beyond the Euphrates, from Egypt's bondage. The covenant binds Israel to the God who liberates. Joshua 24 is the founding document of political and spiritual freedom. *Position two (constraint)*: Joshua's warning at 24:19-20 is not softened. "You are not able to serve the LORD... he is a jealous God; he will not forgive your transgressions or your sins." The covenant is a binding; it comes with covenant curses that will later be enacted in the exile. Any reading that celebrates only the freedom without the weight of obligation is naïve. *Position three (the steelman — least intuitive)*: the chapter's structural insistence on Joshua's *discouraging* of the people (24:19) is the hinge of its theology. Joshua is not testing them; he is telling them the truth about what they are doing. The covenant is not a light thing. It is a binding to a God whose holiness consumes carelessness. And yet the covenant is the only mode of relation that makes Israel what it is. The chapter's genius is to present both dimensions — the liberating history and the binding future — without resolving them into each other. A reader who takes only the "choose this day" moment and not the "you are not able" warning has read half the chapter. *Where this leaves us*: the covenant is offered and chosen freely, but what is chosen is something that will consume careless service. The only safe covenant is the one you enter with your eyes open. Joshua's role in the ceremony is to make sure the eyes are open.

The ethical difficulty the chapter cannot evade is the absence of the Canaanite. Joshua's historical prologue says at 24:8-13, "I gave them into your hand... I destroyed them before you... I sent the hornet before you... I gave you a land on which you had not labored and cities that you had not built." The people living on that land, eating the fruit of those vineyards, had names and histories. The covenant ceremony at Shechem is built on the Canaanite Temple of El-Berith whose worshippers are gone. Warrior's question is unavoidable: does the voice of the Canaanite appear, even once, in this covenant? It does not. The chapter's theological power and its ethical weight are both real; they do not cancel each other, but they must be held together.

**One unresolved question**: If the covenant at Shechem is as voluntary as Joshua's triple extraction of consent suggests, why does the book of Judges open immediately with Israel doing what Joshua explicitly warned against — and what does that say about whether the ritual at Shechem actually worked?

## The Thread

Genesis 35:2-4 has Jacob telling his household to "put away the foreign gods that are among you" at Shechem, and Jacob buries the idols under the oak — almost certainly the same oak under which Joshua sets the witness-stone in 24:26. The chapter is rebuilding the altar Jacob built. 1 Samuel 12 is Samuel's farewell speech, consciously modeled on Joshua 24 — including the witness formula ("you are witnesses this day," 1 Sam 12:5) and the historical prologue. 2 Kings 23, Josiah's covenant renewal, is the DH's own echo of Joshua 24, the Josianic community choosing what their ancestors chose at Shechem. And Hebrews 11:22 singles out Joseph's dying command about his bones — the vow Joshua 24:32 finally keeps — as an act of faith spanning four centuries.
