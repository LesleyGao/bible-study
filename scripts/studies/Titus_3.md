# Titus 3: The Washing and the Boundary

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

The civic context of Titus 3 is the everyday Roman political order experienced by a Christian community on Crete in either the mid-60s (if Pauline) or the early second century (if pseudonymous). Crete was a senatorial province governed jointly with Cyrenaica from a proconsular seat at Gortyn, with magistrates, tax collectors (*publicani* and their successors), and a regular legionary or auxiliary presence rotating through the eastern Mediterranean. Civic life was structured by *boulai* (city councils), magistrates of the older Greek style adapted to Roman administration, and an active imperial cult. The instruction to be "submissive to rulers and authorities, to be obedient" (3:1) presupposes the lived reality of a small, occasionally suspect religious group navigating provincial bureaucracy, civic festivals, and informal social pressure. The same instruction appears with theological grounding in Romans 13 and with apologetic edge in 1 Peter 2:13-17. On Crete specifically, Christian communities would have had to negotiate sacrificial obligations attached to civic festivals, oaths sworn by the *genius* of the emperor, and litigation in courts that sometimes treated religion as a *superstitio*.

Material culture on Crete in the first and second centuries documents the specific civic-society institutions Titus 3 addresses. Cretan inscriptions preserve euergetistic dedications honoring local benefactors and the imperial family; the same inscriptional vocabulary — εὐεργέτης, σωτήρ, χρηστότης, φιλανθρωπία — flows directly into Titus 3:4. Adolf Deissmann's *Light from the Ancient East* documented this saturation of civic vocabulary by imperial-cultic terms; Werner Jaeger in *Early Christianity and Greek Paideia* traced the Christianizing reuse. The archaeology of early Christian baptism is uneven for first-century Crete, but extensive evidence from the broader eastern Mediterranean — the Dura-Europos baptistery (mid-third century), the Aleppo and antiquarian Syrian *piscinae*, North African baptismal pools (Bulla Regia, Sufetula), and second-century literary descriptions in the *Didache* 7 and Justin's *First Apology* 61 — collectively show that the *loutron* of 3:5 was understood very early as immersion or affusion in a dedicated installation. The early baptisteries are typically rectangular or hexagonal stepped pools, sometimes octagonal in later centuries to evoke the eighth-day resurrection symbolism.

The authorship and dating debate runs through this chapter as through the others. The vocabulary case is especially pointed in Titus 3: φιλανθρωπία *philanthrōpia* (3:4) is a Hellenistic political-virtue term that appears nowhere else in the Pauline corpus; παλιγγενεσία *palingenesia* (3:5) appears in the New Testament only here and at Matthew 19:28 (where it has eschatological-cosmic reference); ἀνακαίνωσις *anakainōsis* (3:5) appears only here and at Romans 12:2. The credal-baptismal fragment of 3:4-7 is densely packed with Hellenistic moral-philosophical and Stoic vocabulary that critical scholars (Hans Conzelmann and Martin Dibelius in the Hermeneia commentary; Lewis Donelson; Norbert Brox) have read as evidence of late-first- or early-second-century composition. Defenders of Pauline authorship — William Mounce, I. Howard Marshall, Philip Towner, George Knight, Luke Timothy Johnson, Donald Guthrie, Jerome Quinn — argue that Paul, like any literate first-century writer, drew on the available vocabulary and that the credal fragment may itself be a pre-Pauline tradition Paul has incorporated.

Textual witnesses for Titus 3 include Codex Sinaiticus (א), Codex Alexandrinus (A), and the Bezan tradition where extant. Papyrus 32 (P32, c. 200), which preserved 1:11-15 and 2:3-8, did not extend to chapter 3. Codex Vaticanus (B) — one of the two great fourth-century uncials — lacks the Pastorals altogether; the New Testament text breaks off at Hebrews 9:14, and Titus, Timothy, and Philemon are absent. Bart Ehrman in *The Orthodox Corruption of Scripture* and elsewhere has noted the Vaticanus absence as suggestive of canonical ambivalence in some Eastern circles, while Daniel Wallace and Bruce Metzger have argued it most likely reflects a damaged exemplar rather than ideological exclusion. The textual stability of 3:5's λουτρὸν παλιγγενεσίας is firm; no significant variant reading has emerged.

Five Greek terms anchor the chapter. χρηστότης *chrēstotēs* (3:4) — "kindness," a virtue word with Stoic-philosophical and imperial-cultic resonance. φιλανθρωπία *philanthrōpia* (3:4) — "loving-kindness toward humanity," a Hellenistic political-virtue term unique to Titus in the Pauline corpus. λουτρὸν παλιγγενεσίας *loutron palingenesias* (3:5) — "washing of regeneration," the only New Testament occurrence. ἀνακαίνωσις *anakainōsis* (3:5) — "renewal." αἱρετικὸν ἄνθρωπον *hairetikon anthrōpon* (3:10) — "a divisive person," etymological root of the English "heretic."

## Lens 2 — The Literature

The chapter divides into four units. Verses 1-2 form a brief paraenetic catalogue on civic conduct, structurally parallel to the household instructions of chapter 2 but pivoted outward to the public square. Verses 3-7 form an extended *once-but-now* contrast: "we ourselves were once foolish … but when the goodness and loving-kindness of God our Savior appeared, he saved us." The structure echoes the *einst-jetzt* schema common in early Christian baptismal paraenesis (cf. Eph 2:1-10, 1 Pet 2:9-10). Most commentators (Mounce, Marshall, Towner, Knight, Quinn, Johnson) recognize 3:4-7 as a pre-Pauline or Pauline credal-baptismal fragment embedded in the paraenesis, marked by its rhythmic syntax, its dense theological vocabulary, and the formulaic "the saying is trustworthy" (πιστὸς ὁ λόγος) that immediately follows in 3:8 — a formula functioning here, as at 1:9 within the Pastorals' broader pattern, to mark and certify a deposit of received tradition.

Verses 8-11 turn from the credal core back to instruction: insistence on good works (3:8), avoidance of foolish controversies (3:9), and disciplinary instruction concerning the αἱρετικὸν ἄνθρωπον (3:10-11). Verses 12-15 form the personal closing — Artemas, Tychicus, Zenas, Apollos, Nicopolis — with the form of a Greco-Roman travel-and-greetings *postscriptum*.

Greco-Roman parallels for the credal fragment of 3:4-7 are most readily found in the imperial-cult inscriptions praising emperors for their χρηστότης and φιλανθρωπία. Plutarch's *Moralia*, particularly "On Brotherly Love" and "How a Young Man Should Listen to Poetry," uses *philanthrōpia* extensively as a civic virtue. The Christianizing of these terms — applying them to "God our Savior" — is a deliberate vocabulary contest, as Werner Jaeger argued and as more recent work by Frederick Norris and Frances Young has developed. The genre of 3:1-2 fits within the *politeia*-paraenesis tradition (cf. Pliny's correspondence with Trajan, Plutarch's *Praecepta gerendae rei publicae*). The *hairetikon anthrōpon* discipline of 3:10-11 echoes the synagogue *cherem* tradition and prefigures the developed church-discipline literature of the second and third centuries.

Within the letter's argument, Titus 3 completes the arc. Chapter 1 diagnosed the disordered condition of Cretan church life. Chapter 2 set out the household ethics grounded in the *epiphaneia* of grace. Chapter 3 extends the frame to civic life, locates the whole in baptismal-soteriological grounding, and provides the disciplinary instrument for boundary-keeping when paraenesis fails. The letter as a whole reads as a *mandata principis* commission to an officer in a province (Luke Timothy Johnson), with each chapter addressing a sphere of pastoral oversight.

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

Within the canonical New Testament arc, Titus 3:4-7 stands among the most concentrated baptismal-soteriological summaries in the corpus, comparable in compression to Ephesians 2:8-10 and Romans 6:3-4. The verse 3:5 is the only New Testament occurrence of *loutron palingenesias*; the phrase enters the church's baptismal liturgy by the second century and shapes the patristic doctrine of baptism with extraordinary durability.

**Christian Interpretive Trajectory.** John Chrysostom's homilies on Titus treat 3:4-7 as the heart of the letter and read *loutron palingenesias* as a clear designation for baptism, with regenerative effect mediated by the Holy Spirit. Cyril of Jerusalem's *Catechetical Lectures* (mid-fourth century) cite Titus 3:5 throughout the baptismal preparation. Ambrose's *De Mysteriis* and *De Sacramentis* link the verse to the threefold immersion. Augustine, in his anti-Pelagian writings (*De Peccatorum Meritis*, *De Baptismo*), reads 3:5 as a proof-text for baptismal regeneration and original sin's remission in baptism — a reading that became foundational for Western sacramental theology. Jerome's commentary on Titus engages the verse extensively and treats *palingenesia* as the technical term for the new birth conferred in baptism.

Medieval reception consolidated this reading. Aquinas's commentary on Titus and the *Summa Theologiae* III qq. 66-71 develop the sacramental theology of baptism with Titus 3:5 as a primary text; the Glossa Ordinaria preserves the patristic readings. The medieval canonical tradition drew on 3:10-11 — *hairetikon anthrōpon* and the "first and second admonition" — for the formal procedure of admonition, *latae sententiae* and *ferendae sententiae* excommunication, and the disciplinary framework codified in *Corpus Juris Canonici* and later in the 1917 and 1983 Codes (CIC canons 1331-1335).

Reformation reading split sharply. Luther's *Small Catechism* on baptism — "Baptism works forgiveness of sins, rescues from death and the devil, and gives eternal salvation" — reads 3:5 as a direct ascription of regenerative efficacy to the sacrament; Article IX of the Augsburg Confession formalizes this, and Robert Jenson in twentieth-century Lutheran systematics has carried it forward. Calvin's *Institutes* IV.15-16 reads 3:5 as a sign-and-seal: baptism does not effect regeneration but signifies and seals what God works through Spirit. Westminster Confession 28 and Heidelberg Catechism Q. 65-74 codify this Reformed view. Anabaptist tradition (Schleitheim Confession 1527, Article 1; Menno Simons; later the Mennonite and Hutterite confessions) rejected infant baptism and read *loutron palingenesias* as testimony rather than instrument — baptism follows regeneration as its outward sign, not as its means. The Eastern Orthodox tradition, articulated by Vladimir Lossky in *The Mystical Theology of the Eastern Church* and Alexander Schmemann in *Of Water and the Spirit*, reads baptism as φωτισμός *photismos* "illumination" and the entry into *theosis* — the gift conferred is incorporation into the body of Christ and the start of a lifelong process of deification.

Modern critical scholarship on baptism has worked the same text from multiple angles. George Beasley-Murray's *Baptism in the New Testament* (1962) defended a high-sacramental reading within a Baptist framework, arguing that the New Testament treats baptism as the locus of conversion-encounter rather than as a symbolic afterthought. James Dunn's *Baptism in the Holy Spirit* (1970) distinguished water-baptism from Spirit-baptism, reading the Pastorals' *loutron palingenesias* as referring primarily to the Spirit's work with water as accompanying sign. Geoffrey Bromiley's *Children of Promise* defended the Reformed sign-and-seal reading. John Webster's *Holy Scripture: A Dogmatic Sketch* situated the baptismal language within a broader theology of revelation and divine action.

**Jewish Scholarly Critique and Its Evolution.** Jewish engagement with Titus 3 has focused on three issues. First, the chapter's polemic against "foolish controversies, genealogies, dissensions, and quarrels about the law" (3:9) echoes 1 Timothy 1:4 and is widely read as a polemic against Jewish-Christian or proto-rabbinic teaching. Modern Jewish scholarship — Daniel Boyarin in *Border Lines*, Mark Nanos in his Paul-within-Judaism work, Paula Fredriksen in *When Christians Were Jews* — has pressed the question of whether such polemic represents Paul's own thought or a later stylized boundary-drawing typical of the Pastorals' development. Second, the *loutron palingenesias* vocabulary has been compared to Jewish proselyte baptism (*tevilah*) and to the Qumran lustrations; the comparison illumines the Jewish ritual-purity background against which Christian baptism arose, though the New Testament's specifically regenerative language has no precise Jewish parallel. Third, the chapter's call for submission to civil rulers (3:1) has been read by Jewish interlocutors against the backdrop of rabbinic discussions of the *dina d'malkhuta dina* principle (Samuel of Nehardea, 3rd century) and of Jewish negotiation with Roman authority before and after 70 CE.

**Where this leaves us:** The chapter's baptismal theology has divided Christians sharply for five centuries and remains a live point of confessional difference. Jewish-Christian dialogue on the chapter's anti-Jewish polemic and on its baptismal categories has matured considerably in the last fifty years; the dialogue is genuinely open without being conclusive.

## Lens 4 — The Theological Intent

**Tension 1: Baptismal regeneration.** *Loutron palingenesias* (3:5) is the single most contested phrase in Christian sacramental theology. The Catholic position, articulated in CCC 1262-1266 and grounded on Augustine's anti-Pelagian writings, holds that baptism truly causes the remission of original sin and personal sins, the infusion of sanctifying grace, the imprint of an indelible character, and incorporation into the body of Christ; CCC 1257 cites Mark 16:16 to affirm that "the Lord himself affirms that Baptism is necessary for salvation." The Lutheran position, articulated in Luther's *Small Catechism* and the Augsburg Confession, holds that baptism actually works forgiveness and new birth, with the Word and water together effecting what they signify. The Reformed sign-and-seal position, articulated in Calvin's *Institutes* IV.15-16, Westminster 28, and Heidelberg Q. 65-74, holds that baptism is a sign of God's covenantal grace which God ordinarily uses but to which God is not bound; the Spirit's work and the sacramental sign are conceptually distinct. The Anabaptist symbolic position, articulated in the Schleitheim Confession 1527, holds that baptism follows regeneration as its testimony, with no instrumental causal role. The Eastern Orthodox illumination-theosis reading, articulated by Lossky and Schmemann, holds that baptism is the entry into the body of Christ and the start of deification, with sacramental realism preserved without the Latin Augustinian framework of original-guilt remission.

The Anabaptist symbolic position deserves to be steelmanned. Its core claim is hermeneutic: that the New Testament's baptismal references, including 3:5, are pre-theological reflections on a community whose membership was visibly converted before baptism, and that the later patristic-medieval development of baptismal regeneration is a backward-reading of sacramental causation into texts whose original force was simpler — that the *loutron* signifies and seals a transformation already given by the Spirit. The Anabaptist reads 3:5 in the light of 3:8 ("those who have believed in God") and reasons that the believing-then-baptized order is structural to the New Testament. The position has the strength of taking the credal fragment's own grammar seriously: it is "according to his own mercy," "by the washing of regeneration *and* renewal of the Holy Spirit" — the *kai* may be epexegetic, with the *loutron* and the Spirit's work named as two angles on a single salvific moment. **Where this leaves us:** the verse's grammar permits multiple constructions of the relation between the *loutron* and the *anakainōsis*; the doctrinal traditions resolve the ambiguity differently because they import different prior commitments about the relation of sign and reality, of divine and human action, and of community to covenant.

**Tension 2: Heresy and the limits of the church.** *Hairetikon anthrōpon* (3:10) — "a divisive person" — is the etymological root of the English "heretic," from Greek *hairesis* "choice/sect/faction." The instruction to admonish the divisive person twice and then "have nothing more to do with him" stands at the head of an enormous Christian disciplinary tradition. The Catholic excommunication tradition (CCC 1463; CIC canons 1331-1335; *latae sententiae* incurred automatically by certain offenses, *ferendae sententiae* imposed by ecclesiastical sentence) takes 3:10 as foundational scriptural warrant. Reformed church discipline (Calvin's *Institutes* IV.12 — three-step procedure of private admonition, witnessed admonition, and ecclesial action; Westminster Confession 30; the *Three Marks of the True Church* in Belgic Confession 29 explicitly include discipline) reads 3:10 in tandem with Matthew 18:15-17. Anabaptist *Bann* (Schleitheim Confession Article 2 — admonition by the church before sharing the Lord's Supper) takes the chapter's discipline more seriously than most other traditions, with practices of shunning that have continued in Old Order Amish and conservative Mennonite communities. Eastern Orthodox practice substitutes extended canonical penances and graduated communion-fasting for the more juridical Western model.

The maximalist excommunicating position deserves to be steelmanned. Its core claim is that the church genuinely is a covenant community with discernible boundaries, and that the New Testament both authorizes and requires the community to identify and exclude those who actively divide it. The position notes that 3:10's procedure is patient (two warnings, not one) but ultimately decisive: the divisive person's exclusion is not punitive but protective, and it is required if the community is to remain itself. To refuse to exclude is, on this reading, to decline the task of being a community at all. The maximalist tolerant position — which holds that in a religiously plural age the church has no functional capacity to exclude and should rather seek conversion through patience and dialogue indefinitely — can be steelmanned in turn: it points to the historical abuses of excommunication (the Inquisition, the burning of heretics, the social violence of nineteenth-century shunning) and argues that the church's modern witness is better served by humility about its own boundary-judgments. **Where this leaves us:** the chapter's instruction is plain in form but contested in application. A modern church operating in a religiously voluntarist culture must work out whether 3:10's discipline applies to teaching, to behavior, or to both — and what authority remains to a community whose members can simply leave.

**One unresolved question:** If "the washing of regeneration" inducts the believer into the community of "heirs according to the hope of eternal life" (3:7), and if the community has the authority and indeed the duty to "have nothing more to do with" the divisive person (3:10), then what does the church mean today when it baptizes someone whom it can no longer credibly pretend it has the capacity — institutionally, sociologically, juridically — ever to discipline?

## The Thread

**Ephesians 2:8-10** is the closest undisputed-Pauline parallel to the *not by works* logic of 3:5. Reading the two together sharpens the question of whether 3:5's "washing of regeneration" specifies the means of the gracious salvation that Eph 2:8-10 describes, or whether the Pastorals have introduced a sacramental-instrumental dimension that Paul elsewhere only implies.

**Matthew 19:28** is the only other New Testament occurrence of παλιγγενεσία, where it has cosmic-eschatological reference: "in the regeneration, when the Son of Man sits on his glorious throne." The two New Testament occurrences of *palingenesia* — one personal-baptismal, one cosmic-eschatological — together suggest that the early Christian use of the term spans the same scale as Paul's "new creation" language in 2 Corinthians 5:17.

**Romans 13:1-7** is the closest canonical parallel to 3:1's instruction on submission to rulers; the difference of theological grounding (Romans roots submission in God's ordering, Titus simply commands it) is itself a marker of how the Pastorals work — compressed paraenesis where the undisputed Paulines argue.

**Acts 18:24-28** introduces Apollos, who appears in Titus 3:13 alongside Zenas the lawyer. The cross-reference is a small puzzle and a small datum: Apollos is on Crete and being sent on, suggesting either that the historical Paul is networking known co-workers (the Pauline-authorship reading) or that a later writer has named familiar figures to lend the letter the texture of Pauline movement (the pseudonymity reading).
