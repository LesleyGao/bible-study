# Matthew 7: The Golden Rule, the Two Ways, and the Two Builders

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

Matthew 7 closes the Sermon on the Mount with a string of arresting agrarian and architectural images that root the discourse in the lived material world of first-century Galilee. The "speck and log" of 7:3-5 (*karphos kai dokos*, κάρφος καὶ δοκός) draws on the carpenter's workshop — *karphos* is a splinter, sawdust, a piece of straw or chaff; *dokos* is a roof-beam, a building-timber. The image of someone with a roof-beam projecting from his eye trying to extract a splinter from his neighbor's is comic-grotesque and would have landed in a Galilean village whose builders worked daily with both. Jesus's own carpenter background (*tektōn*, τέκτων, Mark 6:3 / Matt 13:55) makes the metaphor self-referential.

The "dogs" and "pigs" of 7:6 are not pets in any modern sense. First-century Galilean village dogs were feral or semi-feral scavengers who ate what was thrown out (cf. Matt 15:26-27). Pigs were ritually unclean (Lev 11:7) and absent from observant Jewish villages but present in nearby Hellenistic cities like Sepphoris, the Decapolis, and Caesarea Philippi — the contrast is sharp, the language pejorative. "What is holy" (*to hagion*) likely refers to consecrated meat from Temple offerings (Lev 22:1-16; Exod 29:33), the holiest of common foodstuffs, which under Jewish law could be eaten only by priests in a state of ritual purity. Throwing it to dogs is the maximum desecration imaginable. Pearls (*margaritas*, μαργαρίτας) were the high-value Indo-Roman luxury good of the era, harvested from the Persian Gulf and Red Sea, traded to Rome through the Nabataean caravans — the Periplus of the Erythraean Sea (mid-1st century CE) details the trade. Pliny the Elder (*Natural History* 9.106-123) describes pearls as the most valuable of all gems.

The "narrow gate" and "hard way" (7:13-14) and the "wide gate" and "easy way" reflect the topography of Galilean walled settlements: the main gate is wide, well-trodden, busy; the postern or side-gate is narrow and used by few. The "false prophets in sheep's clothing" (7:15) — *en endymasin probatōn*, ἐν ἐνδύμασιν προβάτων — likely refers to the woolen mantle (*addereth*, אַדֶּרֶת, cf. 1 Kings 19:13, 19; 2 Kings 2:8, 13-14; Zech 13:4) that prophets traditionally wore as their professional dress. Wolves predating sheep was the constant peril of Galilean shepherding; archaeological remains of wolf-bones from Galilean sites confirm their continued presence in the period. The grape-from-thornbush and fig-from-thistle (7:16) images would have been instantly intelligible to villagers who cultivated grape and fig as the primary cash and subsistence crops alongside olive.

The two builders (7:24-27) describe a real Galilean engineering hazard. Wadis (*nahalim*) — the dry riverbeds of the Galilean uplands — fill catastrophically during winter rainstorms when runoff from limestone hills sluices through them in flash-floods. A house built on the sand-and-gravel floor of a wadi during the dry season would be swept away by the first winter flood; a house built on bedrock outcrop above the wadi survives. The Lukan parallel (Luke 6:48) sharpens the engineering — "he dug deep and laid a foundation on the rock" — likely the more original. The image is not metaphor invented for sermon use but a known building peril.

## Lens 2 — The Literature

Chapter 7 is the most loosely structured part of the Sermon. Where chapter 5 has the Beatitudes octave and the six antitheses pattern, and chapter 6 has the chiastic three-pieties unit and the Lord's Prayer, chapter 7 is a string of catchword-linked sayings: judgment / measure / speck and log; pearls and dogs; ask, seek, knock; the Golden Rule; two ways; false prophets and fruit; "Lord, Lord"; two builders. Davies/Allison and Luz both note that these sayings have parallels scattered across Luke 6:37-49 and Luke 11:9-13 and Luke 13:23-27 — the Lukan order is different, suggesting Matthew has gathered the material into a deliberate concluding section while Luke distributes it.

The Golden Rule (7:12) is positioned as the structural climax of the central body of the Sermon. Davies/Allison argue it forms an inclusio with 5:17 ("the Law and the Prophets" / "this is the Law and the Prophets"), bracketing the antitheses and the three pieties as the central panel. The Lukan parallel (Luke 6:31) places the Golden Rule mid-discourse without the "Law and the Prophets" tag — the Matthean tag is editorial, claiming the rule as Torah-summary in continuity with Hillel's similar claim.

The "two ways" form (7:13-14) is a known Hellenistic-Jewish moral pattern. The Qumran *Manual of Discipline* (1QS 3:13-4:26) lays out a two-spirits-two-ways scheme; the *Didache* opens "there are two ways, one of life and one of death, and there is a great difference between the two ways" (Did 1:1) and develops a six-chapter catechesis on the pattern; *Barnabas* 18-20 has a parallel two-ways tradition. The shared form suggests Matthew is drawing on common early Christian-Jewish catechetical material.

The "tree by its fruits" saying (7:16-20) is repeated by Matthew at 12:33 — Matthew has likely doubled the saying for compositional emphasis here at the Sermon's conclusion. The "Lord, Lord" warning (7:21-23) is paralleled in Luke 6:46 and Luke 13:25-27, distributed differently. The two-builders parable (7:24-27) closes the Sermon with the Bacon five-discourse formula of 7:28-29: "and when Jesus finished these sayings, the crowds were astonished... for he was teaching them as one having authority and not as their scribes." This is the first occurrence of the formula that recurs at 11:1, 13:53, 19:1, 26:1 — the structural marker of B.W. Bacon's pentateuchal thesis.

Textual variants in chapter 7 are minor. One is worth noting: in 7:14 some witnesses read *ti stenē* ("how narrow") as an exclamation rather than *hoti stenē* ("because narrow") — a grammatical shading that does not affect the sense.

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

The Golden Rule has the deepest cross-cultural and cross-traditional reception of any saying in the chapter. Hillel the Elder's negative formulation in b. Shabbat 31a is older than Jesus by a generation — when a Gentile asks to be taught the whole Torah while standing on one foot, Hillel replies: "*That which is hateful to you, do not do to your neighbor; this is the whole Torah, the rest is commentary — go and learn.*" Tobit 4:15 ("what you yourself hate, do not do to anyone") preserves an even earlier Jewish negative form. The Confucian *Analects* 12:2 and 15:23 give the negative form independently in Chinese tradition: "*do not do to others what you would not have done to yourself.*" The positive Matthean form ("whatever you wish that others would do to you, do also to them") is sometimes claimed as Jesus's distinctive achievement — most famously by Adolf von Harnack and the older liberal-Protestant tradition, who used the positive/negative contrast to argue for Christian moral superiority over Judaism. This reading has been thoroughly dismantled. Pinchas Lapide in *The Sermon on the Mount* and Amy-Jill Levine in *The Misunderstood Jew* note that the negative form is morally identical in substance — both demand the empathic projection of self into the other's position — and that the rabbinic tradition extensively develops the positive implication ("*v'ahavta l'reakha kamokha*," "love your neighbor as yourself," Lev 19:18, which Akiba called "the great principle of the Torah," m. Nedarim 9:4). The rabbinic and Christian traditions are working on the same Levitical ground.

Outside the Golden Rule, the chapter's reception is densely Christian. Augustine's *De Sermone Domini in Monte* gives the chapter sustained exposition; Chrysostom devotes seven of his ninety homilies on Matthew to chapter 7. The "judge not" of 7:1 became a major locus for monastic-ascetic teaching on humility — Cassian's *Conferences*, the *Apophthegmata Patrum*, John Climacus's *Ladder of Divine Ascent*. The "speck and log" image is a desert-father commonplace. The "ask, seek, knock" passage is foundational for Christian teaching on prayer — Tertullian, Cyprian, Origen, Augustine, Aquinas all engage it; Teresa of Ávila's *The Interior Castle* and John of the Cross develop the seeking-and-finding image into mystical theology.

The "narrow way" of 7:13-14 has had perhaps the longest sermonic life of any verse-pair in the chapter. It is the gateway-image of John Bunyan's *Pilgrim's Progress* (1678) — Christian enters by the wicket-gate at the start of his journey, walks the strait way, and the road's narrowness defines his pilgrimage. The Puritan tradition's "narrow way" preaching shaped American revivalism — Jonathan Edwards's "Sinners in the Hands of an Angry God" (1741) and the later First and Second Great Awakenings depend on the verse's force.

The "Lord, Lord" warning (7:21-23) is the single most disturbing passage in the Sermon. Luther's *anfechtung* — the spiritual terror of wondering whether one is truly elect — turns on this verse and on the apparent disjunction between confession ("Lord, Lord") and acceptance ("I never knew you"). Calvin's doctrine of election in *Institutes* 3.21-24 wrestles with the same problem; the Westminster Confession's chapter 18 on "Assurance of Grace and Salvation" is in part a response. Wesleyan theology develops the doctrine of "assurance" partly to address the verse's terror — the believer can know the Spirit's witness and need not live in perpetual *anfechtung*. Bonhoeffer's *Discipleship* makes the verse central: the gap between confessing Christ and following Christ is the gap of cheap grace versus costly grace, and "I never knew you" is the verdict that lands on cheap grace.

The two-builders parable (7:24-27) is the close that defines the Sermon's pastoral demand. Origen treats it as the Sermon's hermeneutical key — to *hear* the words of Jesus is one thing; to *do* them is another, and only the doing produces a foundation. Chrysostom emphasizes the same. The Anabaptist tradition (Schleitheim Confession; Menno Simons; Yoder; Hauerwas) reads it as warrant for the visible community of obedient discipleship — the church is the house built on rock, the form of life that does the Sermon. Bonhoeffer reads it as the Sermon's altar call: hearing without doing is the storm-swept house of the Christianity that has lost its substance.

Modern critical commentaries on chapter 7 to know: Hans Dieter Betz's *Sermon on the Mount* (Hermeneia, 1995); Davies/Allison's ICC vol. 1; Ulrich Luz's Hermeneia vol. 1 (the foundational reception-history); R.T. France NICNT; Craig Keener NICNT and *Sermon on the Mount* (2009); Glen Stassen and David Gushee's *Kingdom Ethics*. On the Golden Rule specifically, Jeffrey Wattles's *The Golden Rule* (Oxford, 1996) is the standard cross-cultural treatment.

## Lens 4 — The Theological Intent

**First tension: does "judge not" prohibit all moral judgment, or only hypocritical judgment? And how does 7:1-5 sit alongside 7:6 and 7:15-20, which clearly require discernment?**

The literalist-pacifist reading (Tolstoy; some Anabaptist; modern progressive Christianity): "judge not" means what it says — Christians do not stand in judgment over others. The plank-and-speck illustration intensifies the prohibition rather than qualifying it.

The hypocrisy-only reading (Augustine; Calvin; the magisterial Protestant tradition; D.A. Carson): the prohibition is on *hypocritical* judgment by people whose own faults are greater than those they judge in others. The plank-and-speck shows that the path is to remove one's own log first ("then you will see clearly to take the speck out") — judgment is permissible after self-examination. The need for discernment (7:6, 7:15-20) confirms that some moral evaluation is required.

The eschatological-deferral reading (Schweitzer; some Lutheran; Bonhoeffer in *Discipleship*): "judge not" means the disciple does not pre-empt the eschatological judgment of God. The disciple lives in the awareness that God alone is the final judge and that any human verdict on another is provisional, partial, and subject to its own coming evaluation.

The communal-discipline reading (Yoder; Hauerwas; the Anabaptist tradition): the prohibition is on the individual's solo verdict over a brother, not on the community's process of discernment and reconciliation (cf. Matt 18:15-17). The Christian community is precisely the body that does morally evaluate, but does so corporately, lovingly, with the Matthew 18 process and not with the unilateral plank-eyed verdict of 7:1.

Steel-manned: each catches something. The literalist preserves the verse's plain shock. The hypocrisy reading preserves the rest of the New Testament's clear endorsement of moral discernment. The eschatological reading preserves the verse's apocalyptic context. The communal reading preserves the difference between individual presumption and community process.

Where this leaves us: 7:1-5 is not a prohibition on moral evaluation; it is a prohibition on the censorious posture that judges others to elevate the self. The "measure you use" warning of 7:2 makes the test self-referential — what you condemn in others, you have made the standard by which you yourself will be judged. Most plank-eyed Christian moralism collapses under that test.

**Second tension: how should the "Lord, Lord" warning of 7:21-23 be heard pastorally — as terrifying alarm to nominal Christians, as call to assurance through fruit-bearing, or as warning against works-righteousness?**

The Puritan-revivalist reading (Edwards; the Great Awakenings; modern Calvinist preaching like John MacArthur, Paul Washer): the verse is the alarm against false assurance. Many who confess Christ and even perform charismatic ministry will be rejected because they were never truly regenerate. The pastoral application is rigorous self-examination and the cultivation of evidences of grace.

The Wesleyan-Arminian reading (Wesley; the holiness tradition; Methodist and Pentecostal: the verse warns that profession without sanctification is empty — but the Spirit gives assurance to the genuinely sanctified believer, and grace makes obedience possible. The verse is a call to entire sanctification, not to perpetual anxiety.

The Catholic reading (Aquinas; the broad Catholic tradition): the verse warns against the sufficiency of charismatic gifts (prophecy, exorcism, miracles) for salvation — what saves is union with Christ in faith and charity, lived out in sacramental life and works of love. Charismatic ministry is no substitute for sanctifying grace.

The Lutheran reading (Luther; modern Lutheranism): the verse warns against works-righteousness — those who say "Lord, Lord" and rely on their own works of prophecy and exorcism are precisely those rejected; the saved are those who do the Father's will, which Luther reads as faith in Christ. (This reading is awkward given the verse's plain emphasis on doing.)

The Bonhoeffer reading (*Discipleship*): the verse names cheap grace. The gap between confession and obedience is the test, and the gap collapses where grace is genuinely received.

The Anabaptist-discipleship reading (Yoder; Hauerwas): the verse confirms that the Sermon is the criterion of authentic Christian existence — those who hear and do are built on rock, those who hear and do not are built on sand. Doctrinal orthodoxy without the form of life is a house on sand.

Steel-manned: each catches something. The Puritan reading takes the terror seriously. The Wesleyan reading takes assurance seriously. The Catholic reading preserves the priority of charity and sacramental life. The Lutheran reading guards against works-righteousness. The Bonhoeffer and Anabaptist readings take the Sermon seriously as the form of Christian life.

Where this leaves us: the verse will not let any of these readings settle. It defies the works/faith binary as Reformation polemic constructs it (those rejected did mighty works in Jesus's name; those accepted do the will of the Father, which is the doing the Sermon describes). It defies the assurance/anxiety binary (the warning is real, and yet the Sermon as a whole presupposes a Father who knows, sees, gives good things, makes the sun rise on the just and unjust). The text will not be domesticated into either complacent assurance or perpetual terror. It demands the disciple live with the question.

**Unresolved question:** What does *anomian* ("lawlessness") in 7:23 mean? *Anomia* (ἀνομία) is a striking word in Matthew — it recurs at 13:41, 23:28, 24:12. In a chapter that has just insisted that "I have not come to abolish the Law" (5:17) and that grounds the Golden Rule in "the Law and the Prophets" (7:12), the rejection of those who do mighty works as "workers of *anomia*" cuts deep. Are they law-breakers in the Mosaic-halakhic sense? Or are they those who have failed to do the Sermon, the Torah-as-fulfilled-by-Jesus? The Matthean Christological recasting of Torah here is hard to pin down.

## The Thread

Matthew 7 connects backward to Deuteronomy 30 (the two ways of life and death; "I have set before you life and death... therefore choose life"), to Psalm 1 (the two paths of the righteous and the wicked), and to Jeremiah 21:8 (the way of life and the way of death). The two-builders parable echoes Ezekiel 13:10-16 (the prophets who built with untempered mortar that the storm sweeps away).

Forward: the *Didache* 1-6 develops the two-ways material into a sustained baptismal catechesis — every new Christian was being taught the Sermon's structure as the form of Christian life. *Barnabas* 18-20 carries a parallel two-ways tradition. The "Lord, Lord" warning is reopened in Matthew 25:11-12 (the parable of the ten virgins: "Lord, Lord, open to us... I do not know you") and Matthew 25:31-46 (the judgment of the nations on care for the least). The Golden Rule resurfaces in Matthew 22:39-40 (love of neighbor as the second great commandment summarizing the Law). James — perhaps the most Sermon-saturated text in the New Testament — repeats the doer-of-the-word emphasis (James 1:22-25), the warning about the tongue's judgment of others (James 3-4), the prohibition of oaths (James 5:12), and the warning against self-deception in religion that does not produce the fruits the Sermon names (James 1:26-27, 2:14-26). The Sermon's afterlife in James shows a community that took 7:24-27 seriously: hearing without doing builds on sand.

The Sermon ends with the most consequential single observation in the discourse: "*he was teaching them as one having authority, and not as their scribes*" (7:29). The crowds recognize what the rest of Matthew will spend twenty more chapters explaining: this is not another rabbi parsing tradition. The authority is direct. The five-discourse structure that begins here will end at 26:1 with the same formula closing the Olivet Discourse — and from there Matthew turns to the Passion that the Sermon's authority makes inevitable.
