# Numbers 35: So That the Manslayer May Flee

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

Numbers 35 sits in the plains-of-Moab closing section of the book whose Hebrew title is *Bĕmidbar* ("in the wilderness"). After the border description of chapter 34 comes the internal administration: forty-eight Levite cities carved out of the other tribes' territory, and six of those designated as cities of refuge (*ʿārê miqlāṭ*) where an accidental killer can flee from the kinsman-redeemer's (*gōʾēl haddām*'s) lawful vengeance. The chapter takes its place in a Pentateuchal triptych on refuge — with Exod 21:12-14 (altar asylum, incipient distinction between intentional and accidental killing) and Deut 19:1-13 (three, potentially six, cities across the Jordan). Josh 20 executes the program by naming the six cities.

Source-critical scholarship splits along familiar lines. Wellhausen and Noth read Num 35 as Priestly (P), dependent on Deut 19 but reformulated in priestly legal style, and date it exilic or post-exilic. Jacob Milgrom (JPS *Numbers*), while acknowledging Priestly shaping, argues for a pre-exilic core and reads the chapter as a coherent legal system with genuine antiquity. Kenneth Kitchen pushes earlier still. Jeffrey Stackert's *Rewriting the Torah* (2007) offers an influential recent reading: he argues that the asylum laws in Deuteronomy and the Holiness Code / Priestly materials (including Num 35) represent systematic revisions of earlier strata, with Num 35 as a priestly-holiness revision that shifts the center of atonement from the altar to the high priest's death. Pamela Barmash's *Homicide in the Biblical World* (2005) reads the chapter against the full ANE legal backdrop and concludes that Israelite homicide law distinctively routes justice through the community's protection of the accidental killer from his kinsmen, where Mesopotamian and Hittite law generally handled homicide through compensation paid to the victim's family.

ANE parallels are instructive. Hittite Laws §§1-6 distinguish between intentional and accidental killing and impose graded compensation rather than mandatory execution. The Code of Hammurabi (§§206-208) distinguishes intent from accident in striking-injury cases and assesses fines. Mari and Nuzi texts preserve evidence of temple asylum — a killer could flee to a sanctuary and negotiate a settlement. Greek temple asylum (*asylia*) at Olympia, Delphi, and elsewhere, and Roman *confugium* at the Temple of Romulus, represent a later Mediterranean development of the same instinct: sacred space protects the fugitive while adjudication proceeds. Walter Moberly (*Old Testament Theology*) notes what is distinctive about Num 35: the asylum is not at the altar (as in Exod 21 and the rest of the ANE) but in six specifically designated Levitical cities, spreading the asylum function geographically and decoupling it from the single central sanctuary.

Material culture glimmers through the terminology. The "iron object" (*kĕlî barzel*, v. 16), "stone tool" (*bĕ-ʾeben yād*, literally "with a stone that fills the hand," v. 17), and "wooden tool" (*bi-kĕlî ʿēṣ yād*, v. 18) span the toolkit of Late Bronze–Iron I Canaan: forged iron for elites, ground-stone implements for everyone, and shaped wood. These are not abstractions but recognizable weapons and tools. Archaeologically, the Levitical-city list of Josh 21 includes places with Iron I occupation (Shechem, Hebron, Kedesh in Galilee, Golan in Bashan), which fits a program at least plausibly rooted in the settlement period. Kitchen's and Hoffmeier's arguments for second-millennium realism find their best Num 35 footholds in this toolkit language and in the provision for "stranger and sojourner" (*gēr wĕ-tôšāb*, v. 15) — social categories well-attested in the second millennium.

Hebrew terms to hold. *Miqlāṭ* (מִקְלָט), "refuge," from the root *qlṭ* ("to take in, absorb") — a technical term in this chapter. *Gōʾēl haddām* (גֹּאֵל הַדָּם), "redeemer of blood" or "blood-avenger," the kinsman with family duty to pursue the killer. *Rōṣēaḥ* (רֹצֵחַ) — the term used for both "manslayer" (accidental) and "murderer" (intentional), with context (and intent) doing the distinguishing work. *Migrāš* (מִגְרָשׁ), "pastureland," the belt around each Levitical city. *Ṣĕdâ* (צְדִיָּה), "lying in wait," the mens-rea marker for murder (v. 20). And the sobering *ḥnp* (חָנֵף), "pollute," "defile" (v. 33) — the verb used throughout the Priestly-Holiness tradition for cultic contamination, applied here to the land itself. The land can be polluted by unatoned blood.

The figures: Eleazar the priest presides in the background (the "high priest anointed with the holy oil," v. 25, whose death ends the manslayer's exile, is Eleazar or his successor). Joshua son of Nun will execute the program in Josh 20. The "congregation" (*ʿēdâ*, v. 24-25) is the judicial assembly that adjudicates whether a killing was intentional.

## Lens 2 — The Literature

The chapter's genre is composite: apodictic case law (the conditional *kî*-and-*ʾim* formulas of vv. 16-23), statutory assignment (the Levitical cities of vv. 1-8), and priestly instruction framed as divine speech (vv. 9-15, 29-34). Philip Budd (WBC *Numbers*) diagrams the structure cleanly: Levitical cities (vv. 1-8), cities of refuge general principle (vv. 9-15), murder vs. manslaughter distinction (vv. 16-28), judicial rules (vv. 29-34). R. Dennis Cole (NAC *Numbers*) calls vv. 16-23 one of the Pentateuch's most precise case-law sequences — a mini-treatise on mens rea.

Literary devices include the threefold weapon-list (iron, stone, wood), the antithetical parallelism between intent-markers ("out of hatred," "lying in wait," "in enmity") and accident-markers ("without enmity," "without seeing him," "did not seek his harm"), and the chapter-closing theological appeal to YHWH's dwelling in the land (v. 34) as the reason why blood must be atoned. The inclusio is subtle: the chapter opens with cities given to the Levites (whose role is to handle holiness) and closes with the land's holiness threatened by unatoned blood. Cities and holiness frame each other.

Textual variants: 4QNum^b preserves portions of Num 35, and the consonantal text is very close to MT. LXX occasionally clarifies (rendering *gōʾēl haddām* consistently as *ho anchisteuōn tou haimatos*, "the nearest of kin regarding the blood"). The Samaritan Pentateuch makes minor harmonizations with Deut 19. The Temple Scroll (11Q19) cols 39-47, while primarily concerned with the temple courts, picks up Num 35's concern for bounded sacred zones; the Scroll's broader legal material treats the asylum cities too (cols 64-66 in the Scroll's reworking of Deuteronomy). Jeffrey Stackert's textual work traces how Num 35's formulation sits between Exod 21 and Deut 19 — not simply an earlier stratum made into a later one, but a distinct priestly-holiness formulation with theological priorities (the high priest's death as atoning, blood pollution of land) that neither the Covenant Code nor Deuteronomy emphasize.

Genre-wise the chapter is also notable for what it is not: it is not a narrative. Unlike Num 36 (Zelophehad's daughters, a case-law-generating story) or Num 27 (the daughters' original petition), Num 35 gives no occasioning event. The law is pre-emptive, anticipatory — a system before a case. Dennis Olson (*Death of the Old and the Birth of the New*) reads this as fitting the second-generation character of Numbers: the new generation receives a complete legal architecture for life in the land before entering it.

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

**Jewish reception.** Rabbinic tradition treated Num 35 as the primary source for one of the most elaborate tractates in the Mishnah. *Mishnah Makkot* 2 covers the six cities of refuge in granular detail: who qualifies (accidental killer, not intentional), the procedure for reaching the city (roads must be marked, obstacles removed, signposts at crossroads reading "Refuge! Refuge!"), life inside the city, the rules of residence, the function of the high priest's death as the boundary of exile. The mothers of high priests, according to m. Makkot 2:6, would provide food and clothing to the exiles so that they would not pray for the high priest's early death.

*Sifre Numbers* works through each verse with attention to the mens-rea language. *Bamidbar Rabbah* amplifies with midrashic illustrations. Rashi on v. 25 explains the high priest's death as atoning because "the high priest should have prayed for his generation that no such calamity occur." Ibn Ezra treats the terminology lexically. Ramban emphasizes the land-pollution theme of v. 33 as a distinctive Priestly doctrine: blood unatoned creates cultic defilement that ritual cleansing cannot repair — only the executed murderer's blood, or (in accidental cases) the high priest's death, effects resolution. Maimonides' *Hilchot Rotzeach U-Shemirat Nefesh* ("Laws of the Murderer and Protection of Life") codifies Num 35 in fifteen chapters of halakhic detail: who counts as an accidental killer, the duties of the court to assign the refuge city, the blood-avenger's regulated role, the configuration of roads to the cities, the conditions under which the manslayer may leave. The blood-avenger is neither eliminated nor unleashed: he is channeled. Nehama Leibowitz reads the chapter as a paradigmatic example of the Torah's method — taking an existing institution (kinsman vengeance) and regulating it until its most dangerous tendencies are checked.

Modern Jewish scholarship has been rich. Milgrom (JPS *Numbers* Excursus) traces the theological movement from altar-asylum to city-asylum as a decisive step in separating cult from criminal justice. Levine (*Anchor Bible*) catalogs the ANE parallels. Tikva Frymer-Kensky read the chapter's careful protection of the manslayer as evidence of a legal culture taking individual human life seriously as an irreducible value. Jonathan Sacks treated the cities of refuge as exemplary of how covenantal law translates moral principle into institutional form — protecting the weak (the accidental killer) from the strong (the enraged kinsman) requires infrastructure, not just sentiment. Aviva Zornberg reads the chapter in psychoanalytic register, attending to the emotional truth that the killer's exile until the high priest's death may be necessary for the *avenger's* healing as much as for the manslayer's protection.

**Christian reception.** Patristic interpretation almost universally read the cities of refuge typologically. Origen's *Homilies on Numbers* treats the six cities (three east, three west of Jordan) as a Christological figure: Christ is the refuge to which the sinner flees from the just avenger (sometimes read as the law, sometimes as death, sometimes as Satan). Bernard of Clairvaux in his sermons speaks of Christ's five wounds as "cities of refuge." Medieval commentators extended this Christological reading: the six cities prefigured the six days of creation (now the six ages of the Church's refuge), the wounds of Christ, or Christ's multiple saving offices. The reading found biblical warrant in Hebrews 6:18 — "we who have fled for refuge might have strong encouragement to hold fast to the hope set before us" — where the verb "fled for refuge" (*kataphygontes*) is the LXX vocabulary of the Num 35 / Deut 19 / Josh 20 asylum tradition.

Aquinas (*ST* I-II q. 105 a. 2) treats the cities of refuge under the judicial precepts of the old law, observing that they both protect the innocent and allow a proportionate role for family-based justice; natural law demands that the accidental killer be distinguished from the murderer, and the Mosaic law realizes this demand in a specific institutional form. Calvin in his *Harmony* commentary reads the six cities as both historical institution and theological figure: "Christ is the true city of refuge, to whom we must flee from the avenging judgment of God." Luther emphasized the high priest's death as figure of Christ's death ending the exile. The asylum motif runs through Reformation hymnody — "A shelter in the time of storm" — and into evangelical theology generally.

A parallel historical thread: the Christian doctrine of ecclesiastical asylum (*asylum ecclesiae*) — a fugitive's immunity within church precincts — developed in the fourth through sixth centuries (Council of Orange 441, Theodosian Code, Justinian's legislation) and drew on Num 35 as proof-text alongside classical temple-asylum traditions. Ecclesiastical asylum persisted through the medieval West and shaped the development of what became sanctuary, asylum, and refugee law. Modern refugee protection — the 1951 Convention, the UN High Commissioner for Refugees — can be traced, contingently and imperfectly, through this Christian (and pre-Christian) stream.

Critical and contemporary Christian scholarship: Wellhausen and Noth treated the chapter as late. Levine and Milgrom pushed back, as noted. Gordon Wenham, Cole, Budd, and Timothy Ashley write largely in commentary mode, integrating Num 35 into Numbers' larger theological shape. Terence Fretheim reads the chapter as a distinctive theological claim about blood and land — the soil itself carries moral weight. Walter Brueggemann (*The Land*) treats the cities of refuge as exemplary of what it means for a people to live on God's land: infrastructure for mercy is the cost of tenancy. Chris Wright (*Old Testament Ethics*) reads them as paradigmatic biblical social justice. Ephraim Radner attends to the sacramental character of the priestly office — the high priest's death bears atoning weight — as a pre-echo of the incarnational logic that will shape Christology. Miroslav Volf, in his work on forgiveness, exile, and embrace, draws on the cities of refuge as resources for contemporary ethics of reconciliation.

**Convergence and divergence.** Jewish and Christian traditions converge on the theological weight of the chapter — life is sacred, blood pollutes, institutions must channel rather than merely suppress vengeance, and atonement runs through the mediating priest. They diverge on referent. Rabbinic tradition preserved Num 35 as detailed halakhah (Maimonides' *Hilchot Rotzeach*), and the institution remained live in legal theory even after the cities themselves ceased to function. Christian tradition more quickly spiritualized the cities as type of Christ, while a parallel institutional stream — ecclesiastical asylum, modern refugee law, sanctuary cities — carried the institutional memory forward. Both traditions have fed the contemporary conversation on asylum, refuge, and the protection of the vulnerable: Miroslav Volf, Christian Smith, and Walter Moberly on one side; Tikva Frymer-Kensky, Jonathan Sacks, and Jewish refugee-law scholars on the other.

## Lens 4 — The Theological Intent

**Tension 1: What is the theological logic of the high priest's death as the boundary of the manslayer's exile (v. 25, 28)?**

Position A (substitutionary atonement): The high priest's death effects atonement for the accidental bloodshed. Just as the sacrificial system uses animal death to atone for unintentional sin, the high priest's death atones for unintentional killing. Rashi's reading ("the high priest should have prayed for his generation") leans this way — the high priest bears a corporate responsibility. Patristic and Reformation readings pull hard on this line toward Christological typology: the death of the great high priest ends the exile of sinners.

Position B (statute of limitations / reset): The high priest's death is a natural marker, not a theological mechanism. His death inaugurates a new high-priestly era; the old slate is wiped. Milgrom inclines toward a combination reading — the high priest's death has an atoning function specifically because he is the one on whose behalf the land and people are consecrated, so his death effects a societal reset.

Position C (land-and-polity reconciliation): Blood pollutes land (v. 33); unatoned bloodshed contaminates the space in which YHWH dwells (v. 34). The high priest, as the one who "dwells in" the sanctuary at the symbolic center of the land, becomes the person whose death is able to lift the specifically-land-directed pollution that accidental killing generates. Frymer-Kensky and Stackert, in different ways, read the theology as primarily ecological-cultic rather than primarily substitutionary.

Steelman for Position C (the least intuitive for readers formed on substitutionary-atonement reading): The chapter speaks in soil-terms. "You shall not pollute the land" (v. 33). "You shall not defile the land in which you live, in the midst of which I dwell" (v. 34). This is not the language of personal guilt imputed to a substitute; it is the language of a contaminated habitat restored by a specific cultic event. The high priest is the person most identified with the space where YHWH dwells, so his death is uniquely capable of releasing the pollution bound to that space. This is not anti-substitutionary — Christian typological readings can incorporate this land-and-dwelling logic — but it does resist collapsing the chapter's theology into later doctrinal categories.

**Where this leaves us:** All three readings have textual warrant, and the chapter's language is thick enough to hold all three at once. The safest reading integrates them: the high priest's death is a cultic event whose effect is both atoning (the pollution is lifted) and chronological (the exile ends with the passing of the era). The Christological appropriation in Hebrews works through this integration rather than flattening it.

**Tension 2: Where does justice live — in the community's adjudication, the kinsman's sword, or the asylum's geography?**

Position A (communal justice primary): The *ʿēdâ*, the congregation, judges (vv. 24-25). Proper procedure, two witnesses (v. 30), ransom forbidden (v. 31) — the community is the site of justice. The blood-avenger is tolerated only when communal procedure fails or when the manslayer leaves the asylum's boundary.

Position B (kinsman vengeance primary): The *gōʾēl haddām* has a genuine right — indeed a duty — to pursue the killer (vv. 19, 21). He acts not as a vigilante but as a legitimate agent of family justice. The chapter does not abolish his role; it regulates the conditions under which he can act. Barmash's comparative work shows that this is closer to typical ANE practice than modern readers assume.

Position C (sacred geography primary): Justice is neither purely communal nor purely kin-based; it is spatial. Six cities, named and bounded, create zones where justice is suspended while adjudication proceeds. Moberly emphasizes this geographical turn — refuge is place-based. The system depends on the sanctity of the six cities to hold the kinsman's justified-but-dangerous claim in abeyance.

Steelman for Position B (the most alien to modern readers): The Torah does not outlaw kinsman vengeance; it channels it. Modern readers trained on monopoly-of-force states tend to read the *gōʾēl haddām* as a regrettable holdover. But the chapter treats him as a legitimate agent with a real stake. If a relative is killed, the kinsman's response is not private bloodthirst but part of the moral order. The chapter's innovation is not to suppress this claim but to route it through communal adjudication and geographical asylum. This recognition of kin-obligation sits uncomfortably with liberal individualism, but without it the system makes no sense. Frymer-Kensky's point — that a culture committed to the irreducibility of human life must have a mechanism by which the death of a particular person generates an obligation to particular others — reframes the blood-avenger as a moral feature rather than a moral bug.

**Where this leaves us:** The chapter distributes justice across three loci — community, kin, geography — and refuses to let any one of them swallow the others. The congregation adjudicates. The kinsman retains a regulated claim. The asylum city protects. None alone suffices; each without the others would either under-protect the manslayer or under-honor the victim. Position C captures how the text architecturally makes this tripartite balance possible: spatial infrastructure holds the tension.

**Unresolved question:** What does the chapter's insistence on blood polluting land (v. 33) mean for a theology of place today — is the soil of a country morally charged by what happens on it, and if so, how does that relate to the modern secular state's claim to adjudicate bloodshed apart from any theology of land?

## The Thread

- **Genesis 4:10-12** — "The voice of your brother's blood is crying to me from the ground ... the ground ... shall no longer yield to you its strength." The first murder makes the land sterile. Num 35's pollution-of-the-land language (v. 33) picks up this primordial soil-and-blood thread.
- **Deuteronomy 19:1-13** — The parallel asylum instruction, with three (and possibly six) cities. Num 35 and Deut 19 both preserve the institution; the Bible preserves its layered witness without harmonizing them — Jeffrey Stackert's work on the rewriting pattern catches this.
- **Joshua 20:7-9** — The six cities are named: Kedesh, Shechem, Hebron (west of Jordan); Bezer, Ramoth-gilead, Golan (east). These are all Levitical cities. The program is executed.
- **Hebrews 6:18** — "We who have fled for refuge might have strong encouragement to hold fast to the hope set before us." The Christological typology appears in the New Testament itself: Christ is the refuge to whom the believer flees. The asylum motif crosses the testamental divide explicitly.
