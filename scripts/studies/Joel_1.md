# Joel 1: What the Locust Left

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

Joel 1 begins with the most ecologically vivid catastrophe in the prophetic corpus. *"What the cutting locust left, the swarming locust has eaten. What the swarming locust left, the hopping locust has eaten, and what the hopping locust left, the destroying locust has eaten"* (1:4). The verse names four Hebrew terms — *gazam* (גָּזָם), *arbeh* (אַרְבֶּה), *yeleq* (יֶלֶק), and *chasil* (חָסִיל) — for a single biological catastrophe: a locust plague that has stripped the land. The four terms have been read three ways: (1) four successive species or life-stages of the migratory desert locust *Schistocerca gregaria* (eggs, larvae, hopping nymphs, winged adults); (2) four poetic synonyms intensifying the destruction; (3) four allegorical ages or empires that successively devour Israel (the Targum's reading, developed by some patristic and medieval interpreters). Modern entomology and biblical scholarship favor reading 1 — the four terms describe successive developmental phases of the locust — but the polysemy was theologically generative across two millennia.

The desert locust *Schistocerca gregaria* is one of the most-studied agricultural plagues in human history. The 2020 East African locust outbreak (which devastated Ethiopia, Kenya, Somalia, and reached Iran and Pakistan) provides contemporary visualization of what Joel 1 describes: swarms of forty to eighty million locusts per square kilometer, each individual eating its body weight daily, capable of stripping a wheat field bare in hours. Documented Levantine locust plagues are recorded in nineteenth- and twentieth-century reports — particularly the 1915 plague during World War I, when the swarms in Palestine and Syria caused famine that killed an estimated 200,000 people. Photographs and eyewitness accounts from John D. Whiting's 1915 article in *National Geographic* ("Jerusalem's Locust Plague") give the closest modern approximation to what Joel describes.

The book's dating is among the most contested in Old Testament scholarship. Joel ben Pethuel (1:1) is otherwise unknown; the prophet is mentioned nowhere else in the canon. The book contains no explicit datable references to kings or empires. Three main positions have been argued: (1) early monarchic, perhaps ninth century BCE under Joash of Judah (Cyril of Alexandria, Calvin, some conservative evangelical readings); (2) pre-exilic, late seventh or early sixth century, contemporary with Jeremiah (some older commentators); (3) post-exilic, fourth or fifth century BCE, after the Second Temple's reconstruction (Hans Walter Wolff, James Crenshaw, Marvin Sweeney, the dominant modern critical view). The post-exilic dating rests on several considerations: Joel's references to a functioning Temple presuppose its rebuilding (515 BCE); the absence of any reference to the king of Judah suggests post-monarchy composition; Joel's use of vocabulary and themes from earlier prophets (Amos, Isaiah, Obadiah) suggests later collection; the eschatological-apocalyptic intensity of chapters 2-3 fits the post-exilic prophetic horizon. The book is now usually dated c. 400-350 BCE.

The Temple references in chapter 1 are loaded. Verse 9's "the grain offering and the drink offering are cut off from the house of the LORD" describes the daily *tamid* offering — the morning and evening grain-and-drink offering prescribed in Exodus 29:38-42 and Numbers 28:3-8. The locust plague has destroyed the grain supply needed for the daily offering, halting Temple worship. Verse 13's "go in, pass the night in sackcloth, O ministers of my God" calls the priests to extraordinary public mourning. The post-exilic Second Temple, rebuilt under Zerubbabel and Joshua (516-515 BCE), maintained the daily offerings; their interruption was a major communal crisis.

Verse 15's "the day of the LORD is near" introduces the book's signature theological category — *Yom YHWH*, the Day of the LORD. The phrase has earlier prophetic precedent: Amos 5:18-20 (the Day of the LORD as darkness, not light), Isaiah 2:12, 13:6-9, Zephaniah 1:7-18. Joel's contribution is to make the Day of the LORD the book's central organizing concept; chapters 1-3 work through its different aspects. The phrase denotes a decisive divine intervention in history — sometimes for judgment against Israel, sometimes for judgment against the nations on Israel's behalf, sometimes for cosmic transformation.

Five Hebrew terms govern the chapter. The four locust terms — *gazam, arbeh, yeleq, chasil* — name the catastrophe; *Yom YHWH* (יוֹם יְהוָה, v. 15), Day of the LORD, names the theological frame. *Tamid* (תָּמִיד, implicit at v. 9), the continual daily offering, names what the plague has cut off. *Mishtah* (מִשְׁתָּה, drinking-feast vocabulary, v. 5), and the agricultural triad of *dagan, tirosh, yitzhar* (grain, new wine, fresh oil — the standard Mediterranean agricultural triad of wheat, grapes/wine, olives/oil), name the economic-ritual collapse. *Betulah ba'alat ne'urim* (בְּתוּלָה בַּעֲלַת נְעוּרִים, v. 8), "virgin engaged to her bridegroom of youth," names the chapter's central image of grief: the engagement broken before the wedding.

## Lens 2 — The Literature

Chapter 1's literary structure is built around concentric circles of address. Verses 2-3: the initial summons to elders and inhabitants, with generational transmission ("tell your children"). Verses 4-12: the catalog of devastation moving outward through population groups (drunkards, vine-growers, priests, farmers, vinedressers). Verses 13-14: the priestly call to fasting and assembly. Verses 15-20: the cosmic frame — the Day of the LORD, the suffering animals, the prayer to YHWH.

The chapter's literary genre is communal lament, with elements of priestly summons and prophetic oracle. The Hebrew Bible's communal lament psalms (Psalms 44, 74, 79, 80, 89) provide the closest generic parallels. Joel's distinctive contribution is the *integration* of agricultural-ecological catastrophe with cultic-Temple disruption with theological-eschatological framework. The chapter does not separate "natural disaster" from "theological event"; the locust plague *is* the day of the LORD's drawing near.

Verse 4's locust catalog is a remarkable poetic device. The four terms in succession, with the formula "what X left, Y has eaten," compounds the destruction's totality. Each locust gets credit for its devastation; nothing is left over for the next. The verse's vocabulary uses verbal repetition (the verb *akhal*, "to eat," recurs four times) and the noun-pattern (each locust is named, each act of eating is named) to perform the relentless consumption.

Verse 8's "virgin wearing sackcloth for the bridegroom of her youth" deserves attention. The image is of an engaged young woman whose fiancé has died before the wedding. In ancient Israelite legal practice, betrothal (*erusin*) was the first stage of marriage — legally binding, requiring divorce or death to annul, but not yet involving sexual consummation or cohabitation. A bride who lost her *ba'al ne'urim* (husband of youth, fiancé) before consummation experienced one of the most severe griefs in the social-emotional vocabulary of the era. The image lands the chapter's theological force: the locust catastrophe is to the people what fiancé-death is to the betrothed virgin — irreversible loss at the threshold of joy.

The chapter's voice shifts give it depth. Verses 2-7: the prophet addresses Judah in second-person plural. Verse 6's "my land" is the LORD's first-person speech intruding into the prophet's address. Verses 8-14: the prophet continues, with v. 13's "ministers of my God" possibly a slip back into divine first-person voice or a use of "my" by the prophet identifying with the priestly class. Verses 15-18: the prophet's exclamation. Verses 19-20: the prophet's first-person prayer ("To you, O LORD, I call"). The shifts give the chapter the dramatic quality of multiple voices weaving together — community, priest, LORD, animals — in a single lament.

The Hebrew text of Joel 1 is largely stable. The four locust terms have been the subject of philological discussion (the precise differentiation of *gazam, arbeh, yeleq, chasil* remains debated), but the chapter's overall argument is preserved across textual witnesses.

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

Joel 1's reception history has clustered around the locust catalog and the Day of the LORD theology.

### Jewish Interpretive Trajectory

Targum Jonathan reads the four locusts allegorically as four kingdoms that successively devour Israel — Babylon, Persia, Greece, Rome. The reading became foundational for Jewish apocalyptic-eschatological thought; the four locusts joined the four beasts of Daniel 7 as the four-kingdom schema of human history. The Aramaic paraphrase shapes Jewish liturgical reading of Joel.

The Talmud (Megillah 31a) prescribes Joel 1 as a public reading during fasts, particularly fasts called in response to drought, plague, or famine. The chapter became part of Jewish liturgical response to communal disaster.

Rashi reads the four locusts as four successive insect species, but allows the allegorical reading as secondary. Ibn Ezra develops the entomological reading more rigorously, identifying the four terms with four developmental stages. Radak (David Kimchi) integrates both readings.

Maimonides (*Hilkhot Ta'aniyot* 1-4) draws on Joel 1 for his halakhic exposition of the laws of fasting. The chapter's call to "consecrate a fast, call a solemn assembly" (v. 14) becomes the foundational text for Jewish public fast practice. The Talmudic tradition prescribed fasts in response to drought, locust plagues, fire, and other communal calamities; Joel 1 is the textual basis.

The medieval and modern Jewish reading of Joel 1 has emphasized the chapter's communal-corporate theology. The catastrophe affects everyone — drunkards, priests, farmers, vinedressers, animals — and the response must be communal. The High Holy Days liturgy and the response to communal calamities both draw on Joel's structure.

### Christian Interpretive Trajectory

Patristic readings developed Joel 1 in two main directions. First, the four locusts were read allegorically as four heretical waves, four ages of the church, or four stages of spiritual decline. Origen, Cyril of Alexandria, Theodore of Mopsuestia, and Jerome all develop allegorical readings. Second, the chapter's call to fasting and lamentation became foundational for Christian liturgical penitence. The Latin liturgical tradition's penitential rites — particularly the Lenten fast — drew on Joel 1's vocabulary.

Calvin's *Commentary on the Twelve* gives Joel 1 a careful Reformed reading. Calvin treats the locust catastrophe as historical-literal: an actual locust plague the prophet observed and interpreted. He rejects the four-kingdoms allegorization as fanciful. Calvin's Reformed approach reads the chapter for its eighth-century or post-exilic Israelite meaning, with theological extension only by careful analogy.

The Reformation tradition's emphasis on public fasting in response to communal calamity drew on Joel 1. The Westminster Assembly's *Directory for the Public Worship of God* (1645) explicitly cites Joel 1 in prescribing public fast days; the practice became established in seventeenth-century Reformed Britain and New England. American colonial fast-day proclamations frequently cite Joel 1.

Modern Christian commentary (Brevard Childs, James Crenshaw, Douglas Stuart, John Goldingay) reads the chapter primarily for its post-exilic context, attentive to its agricultural-ecological specificity and its integration of natural disaster with theological framework. The chapter has had increasing reception in ecological theology — Norman Habel's Earth Bible project, Sallie McFague's work, and various contemporary creation-care movements draw on Joel 1's recognition of non-human creation's suffering (vv. 18-20).

### Where the Traditions Converge and Diverge

Both traditions take Joel 1 as foundational for the theology of communal fasting in response to calamity. Both develop the chapter's integration of human and non-human creation in the theological framework. Both engage the Day of the LORD vocabulary as the chapter's theological signature.

They diverge primarily on the four-locust question. Jewish tradition retained the allegorical four-kingdom reading alongside the literal entomological reading. Christian patristic-medieval tradition developed elaborate allegorical readings; Reformation and modern Christian readings tended toward the historical-literal interpretation.

## Lens 4 — The Theological Intent

The author's vision is the integration of ecological catastrophe and theological diagnosis. The locust plague is not merely "natural"; it is the leading edge of the Day of the LORD. The chapter does not separate the categories. The reader cannot isolate "natural causes" from "theological meaning"; the catastrophe and its meaning are one event.

Two genuine tensions emerge.

**Tension 1: Are natural disasters theological events?**

- *Catholic* (CCC 309-314, on divine providence; *Laudato Si'*): Natural events occur within the providential order; they have theological significance without being directly caused by God's punitive intent. Joel 1 names a particular plague as a particular sign in a particular moment.
- *Reformed* (Calvin, Edwards): Divine providence operates through second causes; the locust plague is a real natural event and a real divine action. The Reformed doctrine of providence integrates both.
- *Lutheran*: God's hand is in all things — the locust is the LORD's "great army" (cf. 2:25). The believer reads natural events theologically, while remaining humble about specific interpretations.
- *Anabaptist / Mennonite*: The chapter warrants a posture of theological reading of history while resisting confident specific claims about God's intent.
- *Liberation / ecological theology (Sallie McFague, Norman Habel)*: The chapter's recognition that "the beasts of the field pant for you because the water brooks are dried up" (v. 20) extends covenant beyond humans to the ecological order. The theological reading of disasters must include the suffering of non-human creation.
- *Jewish (Maimonides, Hilkhot Ta'aniyot)*: Communal calamity calls for communal *teshuvah*; the chapter's fast-and-assembly response is the proper Jewish ritual answer to disaster regardless of the specific causal-theological details.
- *Critical*: The chapter reflects ancient Israelite theological-ecological integration. Whether modern readers should read every disaster as divinely meaningful is a different question from whether ancient Israelites did.

**Steelmanning the position I find least intuitive**: the strict ecological-theology reading. The temptation for traditional theologians is to dismiss as ideological the suggestion that locust plagues are partly *human* responsibility (today's locust plagues are intensified by climate change driven by human industrial activity). But the chapter does name human responsibility — the locust plague comes in the wake of unspecified covenant failure (the chapter's call to fast and return implies failure that needs response). The contemporary ecological reading extends this: today's disasters often *are* in significant part the consequence of human action, and theological reading honestly held includes ecological ethics. The reading is uncomfortable for traditions that prefer to read disasters as directly providential without human agency, but the chapter's grammar (the fast-and-return response) supports it.

**Where this leaves us**: The chapter forbids reading natural disasters as either purely natural (without theological meaning) or as purely punitive (without ecological implication). The integration the chapter performs is theologically rich and ethically demanding.

**Tension 2: The role of the priest in lament.**

Verses 13-14: "Put on sackcloth and lament, O priests... consecrate a fast; call a solemn assembly."

- *Catholic / Orthodox*: The clergy's role in leading communal lamentation is fundamental; the liturgy provides the structures that allow the community to grieve before God. Joel 1's priestly summons is a model for the liturgy of communal mourning.
- *Reformed*: The pastor's role in calling the community to repentance is foundational; the principle of regulated worship includes regulated lamentation. Reformed fast-day services in the Puritan tradition follow this pattern.
- *Anabaptist*: Lament is communal but not primarily clerical; the gathered community's collective lamentation is what the chapter calls forth.
- *Liberation*: The verse warrants critique of religious leadership that has lost capacity to lament alongside the suffering. Clergy who cannot put on sackcloth have lost a key dimension of priestly vocation.
- *Jewish*: The verses establish the structure of Jewish public fast practice. The community gathers; the leaders call the assembly; the prayers and confessions are public.
- *Critical*: The chapter reflects post-exilic Second Temple priestly practice; the priest as the ritual leader of communal lament is a specific historical configuration.

**Where this leaves us**: The chapter does not allow individual private grief to substitute for communal lament. Disasters of communal scale require communal response.

The unresolved question the author leaves: *What happens after the fast?* The chapter ends with the prayer to YHWH (vv. 19-20). It does not narrate the LORD's response. The book continues into chapters 2-3, but chapter 1 itself ends in unresolved petition. The reader is left with the discipline of asking and the unfinishedness of the asking.

## The Thread

- **Exodus 10:1-20** — The plague of locusts on Egypt. Joel 1's locust catastrophe inverts the exodus pattern: the locusts that once afflicted Egypt now afflict Judah. Read together to feel the canonical reversal.
- **Amos 5:18-20** — "Woe to you who desire the day of the LORD! Why would you have the day of the LORD? It is darkness, and not light." Amos's earlier prophetic critique of facile expectations for the Day of the LORD is the textual ancestor of Joel's Day-of-the-LORD theology. Joel's chapters 2-3 will develop the full vision; Amos's verse is the first prophetic warning.
- **Psalm 80** — "Restore us, O God of hosts; let your face shine, that we may be saved." The communal lament psalms provide the closest generic parallels to Joel 1. Read them together to feel the canonical structure of communal lament.
- **Revelation 9:1-11** — The fifth trumpet's "locusts from the smoke" with "teeth like lions' teeth" (Rev 9:8). John's apocalyptic vision draws explicitly on Joel 1:6 ("its teeth are lions' teeth"). When you reach Revelation 9, remember Joel 1 — the prophet's locusts have become the apocalypse's army.
