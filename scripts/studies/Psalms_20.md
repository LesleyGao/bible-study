# Psalm 20: Chariots, Horses, and the Name

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

The superscription is brief: *To the choirmaster. A Psalm of David* (*lamenatzeach mizmor le-David*, לַמְנַצֵּחַ מִזְמוֹר לְדָוִד). Davidic ascription locates the psalm within the early Judahite royal collection, and its companion, Psalm 21, is widely read by Westermann, Hossfeld-Zenger, and Goldingay as a thanksgiving response to the intercession that Psalm 20 enacts. The pair forms a liturgical unit: Psalm 20 is prayed *before* battle; Psalm 21 is prayed *after* victory.

Form-critically, Hermann Gunkel classified Psalm 20 as a *Königspsalm*, a royal psalm — specifically a royal intercession (*Königsfürbitte*) in which the assembled people pray for their king. Sigmund Mowinckel made Psalm 20 central to his cult-functional theory, placing it explicitly in his "Royal Festival" *Sitz im Leben*: the king before a military campaign visits the temple, offers sacrifices, and the priestly-cultic community prays the intercession over him. The shift in voice within the psalm — from second-person plural address in vv. 1–5 ("May the LORD answer *you*") to first-person testimony in vv. 6–8 ("Now *I* know that the LORD saves his anointed") to communal closing in v. 9 ("save the king!") — fits a liturgical drama in which different voices speak from different positions within a sanctuary rite.

The cultic background has substantial textual support. The reference to "your offerings" and "your burnt sacrifices" (20:3) presupposes a sacrificial setting; the *selah* after v. 3 marks a liturgical pause, perhaps for the actual offering or for an antiphonal response; the language of sending help "from the sanctuary" and giving support "from Zion" (20:2) ties the prayer specifically to the Jerusalem temple. Mowinckel's reconstruction is not the only possibility — the psalm could function in any setting where a king or community prays before military trial — but the cultic reading remains the most economical explanation of the psalm's internal features.

Five Hebrew terms anchor the psalm. *Yeshu'ah* (יְשׁוּעָה, "salvation," 20:5, 6, 9) appears three times, the same root that becomes the proper name *Yeshua* (Jesus) — in this psalm the king is the *one who saves* and at the same time the *one for whom salvation is asked*. *Mashiach* (מָשִׁיחַ, "anointed," 20:6) names the king's covenantal status — the term that becomes *Christos* in Greek. *Shem-elohei-Ya'aqov* (שֵׁם אֱלֹהֵי יַעֲקֹב, "the name of the God of Jacob," 20:1) uses the patriarchal name with covenantal weight; the reference to *Jacob* rather than *Israel* may signal the older, more familial covenant theology behind the royal ideology. *Rekev* (רֶכֶב, "chariots") and *susim* (סוּסִים, "horses") in v. 7 name the elite military technology of the ancient Near East — the chariotry of Egypt and the cavalry of Mesopotamia. And *bashem-YHWH eloheinu nazkir* (בְּשֵׁם יְהוָה אֱלֹהֵינוּ נַזְכִּיר, "in the name of the LORD our God we will remember" — the verb *nazkir* in the Hebrew, often translated "we will boast" or "we will set up our banners") makes the *name* the counter-weapon to military hardware.

Ancient Near Eastern parallels illumine the chariot-horse polemic. Egyptian royal ideology centered the chariot. Pharaoh Ramesses II's monumental Karnak reliefs depict him in his chariot at Kadesh, single-handedly routing the Hittites; the chariot was the technology of imperial reach. Solomon's later trade in horses and chariots (1 Kgs 10:26–29) is presented in the Deuteronomistic History as a partial assimilation to imperial norms. Deuteronomy 17:16 explicitly forbids the Israelite king from multiplying horses or returning to Egypt for that purpose. Psalm 20:7's anti-chariot/anti-horse formula sits in a long Israelite tradition of skepticism toward foreign military technology — a skepticism that reads not as primitivism (Israel had its own chariots) but as theological priority: the *name of YHWH* is the structure within which any military technology must be subordinated.

Hosea 1:7 makes the same theological move: "I will save them by the LORD their God; I will not save them by bow or by sword or by war or by horses or by horsemen." Isaiah 31:1 condemns "those who go down to Egypt for help and rely on horses." Psalm 20:7 sits within this stream of prophetic-cultic anti-military-alliance theology.

Textually, fragments of Psalm 20 are preserved at Qumran. A fascinating textual artifact is the so-called Aramaic Psalm 20 found in Papyrus Amherst 63 — a late-first-millennium BCE papyrus containing an Aramaic version of part of Psalm 20 in a syncretistic religious context. The papyrus has been studied by Karel van der Toorn and others as evidence of how Psalm 20 (or a related composition) lived in diaspora Aramaic-speaking communities outside the canonical biblical context. The LXX numbers Psalm 20 as Psalm 19 — the by-now familiar one-behind shift continues in Catholic and Orthodox Bibles.

## Lens 2 — The Literature

The psalm divides into three movements distinguished by voice and orientation.

- **Verses 1–5:** Communal intercession addressed to the king — *May the LORD answer you... May we shout for joy over your salvation*.
- **Verses 6–8:** Confident testimony addressed to or about Yahweh — *Now I know that the LORD saves his anointed*; the chariots-and-horses contrast.
- **Verse 9:** Final communal petition — *O LORD, save the king! May he answer us when we call*.

The parallelism is varied and structurally significant. Lowth's three categories appear throughout. Synonymous, with intensification: "May the LORD answer you in the day of trouble! / May the name of the God of Jacob protect you!" (20:1) — the second colon names *who* the LORD is and what he does, making the first colon's general "answer" specific. Antithetic, structurally and theologically central: "Some trust in chariots and some in horses, / but we trust in the name of the LORD our God" (20:7). This is the psalm's poetic and theological hinge, where two ways of being in the world are opposed in compact verbal symmetry. The Hebrew reads literally "These [boast] in chariots, and these in horses, / and we — in the name of YHWH our God we will remember" — the verbal ellipsis in the first half (no main verb) makes the second half's *nazkir* do double duty, intensifying the contrast.

Synthetic and climactic: "They collapse and fall, / but we rise and stand upright" (20:8). The four verbs trace two opposing trajectories — *qara'u* (collapse), *nafalu* (fall), *qamnu* (rise), *vannit'odad* (stand upright). The chiastic structure (collapse-fall / rise-stand) sets falling against rising in a symmetrical reversal that enacts the theological claim: trust in the name produces standing where trust in technology produces falling.

Adele Berlin's analysis of phonological parallelism would note the dense paronomasia of vv. 6–9, where the root *yashua* (save) appears in multiple forms and weaves through *anokhi yada'ti* ("now I know"), the king as *meshicho* (his anointed), and the closing *hoshi'ah ha-melech* ("save the king"). James Kugel's "what's more" logic illumines the vow-and-fulfillment movement of vv. 4–5: from heart's desire to plans to salvation to banners — each colon adds a new register of communal participation in the king's deliverance.

Westermann's form-critical analysis recognizes the psalm as a royal intercession with internal liturgical complexity: blessing-prayer (vv. 1–5), oracle of salvation or priestly assurance (v. 6), proverbial-confessional contrast (vv. 7–8), closing petition (v. 9). The shift between voices — second-person address, first-person assurance, communal closing — fits a multi-voiced cultic performance.

The psalm contains one *selah* (after v. 3), marking the liturgical pause associated with the offering. The chiastic envelope binds the opening petition that the LORD *answer* the king in the day of trouble (20:1) to the closing prayer that *he answer us when we call* (20:9). The king's prayer and the people's prayer are bound together in the same divine answering.

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

In the Psalter's first book, Psalm 20 stands in a triptych with Psalms 18 and 21 — three royal psalms clustered around the figure of the Davidic king at war. Psalm 18 celebrates the deliverance accomplished; Psalm 20 prays the intercession before the battle; Psalm 21 thanks God for the victory granted. Together they form a sustained meditation on the king's relationship to Yahweh in the central political-military reality of Iron Age Israel: warfare. Psalm 19, between Psalms 18 and 20, makes the unexpected move of grounding the warring king's life in cosmic and Torah revelation — the king at war is the king under Torah and under the stars.

**Jewish Trajectory.** *Midrash Tehillim* on Psalm 20 reads the prayer as Israel's prayer for any righteous king, with eschatological weight: the ultimate "anointed" for whom this prayer is offered is the messianic king of Jewish expectation. Rashi reads the chariots-and-horses contrast as polemic against the Egyptian and Aramean military powers David faced. Ibn Ezra emphasizes the philological precision of *nazkir* — "we will remember" or "we will mention" or "we will invoke" — with the verb suggesting both memorial-act and proclamation. Radak treats the psalm Christologically in a Jewish key, reading the *mashiach* of v. 6 as the future messianic king.

In modern Jewish thought, Psalm 20 has been read in connection with the realities of Jewish powerlessness and, after 1948, Jewish military responsibility. The chariots-and-horses verse takes on a particular charge in the State of Israel — the question of how a Jewish state should hold its military strength under the theology of Psalm 20. Jewish chaplaincy traditions have wrestled with how to pray Psalm 20 in conditions where Israel itself possesses the chariots. In Jewish liturgy, Psalm 20 is recited daily in many traditions as part of the *tachanun* (penitential prayers) and is associated with prayers for those in distress.

**Christian Trajectory.** Augustine's *Enarrationes in Psalmos* on Psalm 20 (Latin Psalm 19) reads the psalm in the voice of the church praying for Christ in his passion — the day of trouble is the cross, the chariots and horses are the powers of the world, and the rising and standing upright are the resurrection. Athanasius and Cassiodorus preserve similar Christological readings. Theodore of Mopsuestia, characteristic of Antiochene exegesis, holds the historical-Davidic referent more firmly while allowing typological extension.

Aquinas in his *Lectura* reads the psalm as the prayer of the church for Christ-the-king and, by extension, for all Christian rulers under Christ's lordship. Luther's *Operationes* presses the chariots-and-horses contrast into the believer's spiritual life: the Christian's chariot is faith, the Christian's horse is the Word, and trust in any other instrument is idolatry. Calvin's 1557 commentary holds the psalm both as Davidic prayer and as the church's intercession for civil rulers — a reading that became central to Reformed political theology.

The psalm has had particular resonance in the Christian liturgical and political tradition. The Anglican *Book of Common Prayer* (1549, 1662) included prayers for the sovereign that draw on Psalm 20's language; the *Te Deum* tradition celebrates God's salvation of his anointed king; the Roman *Liturgia Horarum* includes Psalm 20 in the Office. The Catholic tradition has used the psalm in coronation rites and royal anniversaries.

But the psalm has also produced a major confessional fault-line on the question of Christian prayer for civic rulers. The Anabaptist tradition, beginning with the sixteenth-century radical reformation and continuing through the Mennonite, Hutterite, and Brethren traditions, has refused to pray Psalm 20 for civic rulers in the way Anglican or Catholic tradition does. The Anabaptist argument is that the church's only king is Christ; civic rulers are at best tolerated and never to be sacralized through royal prayer. The Reformed two-kingdoms theology (especially in its Lutheran and Kuyperian developments) takes a different path: Christians do pray for civic rulers, but with clear distinction between the kingdom of God and the kingdom of the sword. The Barmen Declaration of 1934, written largely by Karl Barth in response to the Nazification of the German Protestant church, made the Christological priority over civic obedience a confessional matter — Christians may pray Psalm 20 for civic rulers only insofar as they remember that Jesus Christ alone is Lord.

Modern critical scholarship has illuminated the psalm. Gunkel's classification; Mowinckel's cultic reconstruction; Westermann's form-critical structure; Hans-Joachim Kraus's theological commentary; Hossfeld and Zenger's Hermeneia; John Goldingay's Baker commentary; Gordon Wenham's *Psalms as Torah*; Walter Brueggemann's classification of the psalm as "orientation" in his schema; Patrick Miller's *Interpreting the Psalms*; William Brown's *Seeing the Psalms*; Beth Tanner's commentary work — all contribute to the conversation. The Aramaic Psalm 20 of Papyrus Amherst 63, studied especially by van der Toorn and Richard Steiner, has opened a new window into the diaspora afterlife of the psalm.

**New Testament Use.** Psalm 20 is not directly quoted in the New Testament, but its language saturates the Christology of the early church. The *Christos* of Christian confession is the *mashiach* of Psalm 20:6; the *yeshu'ah* of vv. 5, 6, and 9 anticipates the proper name *Iēsous*; the chariots-and-horses contrast informs Paul's "weapons of our warfare are not of the flesh" (2 Cor 10:4) and Ephesians 6:10–17's anti-imperial spiritual-warfare imagery. Revelation 19's vision of the rider on the white horse — the only horse in Scripture that bears the king of kings — recasts the chariots-and-horses theology in apocalyptic register: the horses of imperial might collapse; the horse of the Lamb stands.

## Lens 4 — The Theological Intent

Two tensions run through Psalm 20, and the second is acute for any community that prays it.

**Tension 1: Anti-militarism or anti-foreign-alliance?** "Some trust in chariots and some in horses, but we trust in the name of the LORD our God" (20:7). What exactly is the psalm against? Three positions.

The anti-militarism reading (some pacifist traditions, some contemporary Anabaptist scholarship, certain liberal Protestant readings) takes the verse as a categorical rejection of military technology — Israel is to fight, if at all, with the name alone, and the anti-chariot polemic is a rejection of war-making itself. The anti-foreign-alliance reading (Mowinckel, Goldingay, mainstream contemporary scholarship) takes the verse as polemic against trust in *Egyptian* or *Mesopotamian* military technology — the issue is not chariots-as-such (Israel had chariots) but chariots-as-substitute-for-trust. The psalm is anti-idolatry, not anti-military. The Christological-spiritualizing reading (much patristic and medieval exegesis) takes the contrast figurally: chariots and horses are the powers of the world, against which the church wields the name of Christ.

Steelmanning each: the anti-militarism reading honors the prophetic stream of Hosea 1:7, Isaiah 31:1, and Zechariah 9:9–10 ("his rule shall be from sea to sea... and the war horse from Jerusalem"); the anti-foreign-alliance reading honors the historical specificity of the psalm's setting and its likely function in actual Iron Age Judahite cult; the spiritualizing reading honors the psalm's afterlife in Christian devotion.

The historical-critical reading is probably correct in its first sense: Psalm 20 in its original setting is anti-foreign-alliance, polemic against substituting Egyptian military aid for trust in Yahweh. But the eschatological trajectory of the prophetic stream — Isaiah 2's vision of swords beaten into plowshares, Zechariah 9's war horse cut off from Jerusalem — pushes the psalm's logic toward the anti-militarism reading. The original specific polemic opens onto the larger eschatological vision.

**Tension 2: Praying for civic rulers.** Does the church pray Psalm 20 for the President, the Prime Minister, the king? Three positions, each with confessional weight.

The Catholic-Anglican-Reformed mainstream prays Psalm 20 for civic rulers with theological care — the prayer is not a sacralization of the ruler but an intercession that the ruler govern justly under God. The Anabaptist tradition refuses this prayer in its royal form, holding that Christ alone is the church's king and that civic rulers are not legitimate objects of the kind of intercession Psalm 20 enacts. The Barthian-Barmen position holds the Christological priority absolute: civic rulers may be prayed for only insofar as the prayer never confuses them with Christ.

Steelmanning each: the mainstream tradition honors the New Testament's call to pray for kings and all in authority (1 Tim 2:1–2); the Anabaptist tradition honors the cruciform shape of Christian discipleship and the dangers of Christian nationalism; the Barmen position honors both, holding civic-prayer and Christological-priority in tension.

The honest answer is probably contextual. In settings where the church is tempted to sacralize civic rulers — Constantinian Christendom, Christian nationalism, the Nazi-era German Christians — Barmen's caution is theologically necessary. In settings where the church is tempted to abandon any prayer for the public order — pure sectarianism, religious privatism — the mainstream tradition's prayer for civic rulers under God is theologically necessary. Psalm 20 itself does not resolve this tension; it is the tension's site.

Where this leaves us: the psalm is liturgical prayer for an embattled anointed one, prayed by a community that has bet its life on the *name* rather than on the *technology*. Whether the anointed one is David, the Davidic-messianic future, the Christ of the apostolic preaching, or the church-king of Christian liturgy — and whether the technology is Egyptian chariots, Roman legions, modern weapons systems, or any other accumulated power — the psalm's central question remains: what do you trust? The chariots collapse; the name stands.

The closing petition — "O LORD, save the king!" — is the only direct prayer to Yahweh in the entire psalm. Everything else has been prayer *about* the LORD's deliverance and *to* the king. The final cry breaks through to direct address. Save the king. The psalm enacts what it declares: trust in the name takes the form, finally, of crying that name.

Closing question: When the day of trouble actually comes, what is the chariot you reach for, and whose name comes out of your mouth?

## The Thread

- **Psalm 21** — The companion thanksgiving; victory granted, the king crowned, the prayer of Psalm 20 answered.
- **Hosea 1:7** — "I will save them by the LORD their God; I will not save them by bow or by sword or by war or by horses or by horsemen." The prophetic echo of Psalm 20:7.
- **Zechariah 9:9–10** — The peaceful king on a donkey, with the war horse cut off from Jerusalem; the eschatological completion of Psalm 20's logic.
- **2 Corinthians 10:3–5** — Paul's anti-imperial spiritual warfare draws on the chariots-and-horses theology in a new key.
