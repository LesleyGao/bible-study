# Leviticus 17: Life in the Blood

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

Leviticus, *Wayyiqrāʾ* (וַיִּקְרָא) in Hebrew, continues from Exodus 40 where the glory cloud descends on the tabernacle and Moses is unable to enter. The book's dating remains one of the central questions of Pentateuchal criticism. Wellhausen's 1878 *Prolegomena* located the Priestly source (P) in the post-exilic period as a retrojection of Second Temple realities onto the wilderness narrative. Menahem Haran (*Temples and Temple-Service in Ancient Israel*, 1978) and Jacob Milgrom's Anchor Bible commentary (1991–2001) argued forcefully for a pre-exilic core, citing terminological archaisms and the cultic specificity of the material. Israel Knohl's *The Sanctuary of Silence* (1995) produced the most consequential recent refinement: within the priestly material, Knohl distinguished P (cult-centered, terse, theologically silent about ethical imperatives) from H, the Holiness School — a later priestly stratum that is ethically inflected, socially expansive, and responsible for much of Leviticus 17–26 along with redactional additions throughout the Pentateuch.

This matters crucially for chapter 17, which Knohl identifies as the opening of the Holiness Code (H). The structural shift is real: chapters 11–15 are P's purity regulations of the body; chapter 16 is P's climactic cultic day; and chapter 17 inaugurates a new theological voice — one that speaks equally to priests and laity ("Aaron and his sons and all the people of Israel," v. 2), that integrates the *gēr* (sojourner/resident alien) into the statutes (vv. 8, 10, 12, 13, 15), and that articulates the blood prohibition as a creation-wide principle rather than a cultic technicality. The Leviticus structure thus reads: 1–7 sacrifices, 8–10 ordination, 11–15 purity, 16 Yom Kippur (theological pivot), 17–26 Holiness Code, 27 appendix. Chapter 17 is the H school's opening manifesto.

ANE and material-culture parallels. The prohibition on consuming blood is distinctively Israelite in its absoluteness. Mesopotamian, Ugaritic, and Hittite cuisines did not universally prohibit blood consumption; Homeric Greek cult frequently featured blood-drinking or blood-handling. Ritualized blood prohibition in the ancient world was unusual. Comparative scholarship (William Gilders, *Blood Ritual in the Hebrew Bible*, 2004; David Biale, *Blood and Belief*, 2007) documents the distinctiveness. What Leviticus 17 articulates — that blood equals life and therefore belongs on the altar rather than on the table — is theologically innovative.

The *śĕʿîrîm* (שְׂעִירִים, "goat demons," literally "hairy ones," v. 7; KJV "devils") reflect a direct ANE cultic backdrop. The term appears in Isaiah 13:21 and 34:14 as wilderness demons or satyrs in desolated places; in 2 Chronicles 11:15, Jeroboam's illegitimate priests serve the *śĕʿîrîm* at the northern high places. Mesopotamian *šēdu* and Akkadian demon-lists attest similar field-dwelling spirits, often goat-shaped. Egyptian Mendes cult featured sacred goats. The Israelite charge — "they shall no more sacrifice their sacrifices to goat demons, after whom they whore" — assumes that wilderness sacrifice was not a neutral category but entailed ongoing entanglement with non-Yahwistic powers. Milgrom notes the connection to the Azazel goat of chapter 16 — *śāʿîr* and *śĕʿîrîm* share the root — and argues chapter 17 consciously echoes chapter 16 as an H corrective: whatever Azazel represented in the ritual of 16 cannot be multiplied into open-field demon-cult in 17.

Archaeological evidence. Open-air sacrificial installations dating from the Iron Age (e.g., the Mount Ebal installation excavated by Adam Zertal, though its interpretation is contested) and high-place cultic remains at Tel Dan, Arad, and Beersheba attest the reality of distributed sacrifice in ancient Israel. Chapter 17's centralization demand is polemical, legislating against observed practice. Manuscript witnesses include 4QLev fragments from Qumran; the LXX preserves the text with minor expansions; the Samaritan Pentateuch aligns with MT.

Three to five Hebrew terms to register: *dām* (דָּם, "blood"); *nepeš* (נֶפֶשׁ, "life/soul/person" — central to v. 11); *śĕʿîrîm* (שְׂעִירִים, "goat demons"); *nikrat* (נִכְרַת, "cut off" — the *kārēt* penalty, probably divine excision from kinship rather than human execution); *kippēr* (כִּפֵּר, "atone/purge," now tied explicitly to blood in v. 11).

## Lens 2 — The Literature

Genre: cultic law with homiletic motivation clauses — the H signature. Where P tends to state ritual requirements without extensive justification, H supplies reasons. Chapter 17's every prohibition carries an embedded *kî* ("for") clause: "*for* the life of the flesh is in the blood" (v. 11); "*for* the life of every creature is its blood" (v. 14). This explanatory texture is a Knohl diagnostic: H tells you why.

The chapter has a clear architecture: (A) slaughter as sacrifice vv. 1–7; (B) extension to sojourners on sacrificial locus vv. 8–9; (C) blood prohibition with theological motivation vv. 10–12; (C') blood covering for hunted game vv. 13–14; (D) carrion and cleansing vv. 15–16. The repeated refrain "shall be cut off from among his people" (vv. 4, 9, 10, 14) structures the pericope into its four major prohibitions. The double inclusive clause — "any one of the house of Israel, or of the strangers who sojourn among them" (vv. 8, 10, 13) — is rhetorically striking and theologically important: the blood prohibition is not an ethnic peculiarity but a universal standard for anyone residing in Israel's land.

Verse 11 is the rhetorical and theological center: *kî nepeš habbāśār baddām hîʾ, wa-ʾănî nĕtattîw lākem ʿal-hammizbēaḥ lĕkappēr ʿal-napšōtêkem, kî-haddām hûʾ bannepeš yĕkappēr* — "For the life of the flesh is in the blood, and I have given it for you on the altar to make atonement for your souls, for it is the blood that makes atonement by the life." This verse is the single most cited Old Testament text in atonement theology. Its three-fold structure — life in blood, blood on altar, blood atones by life — supplies a compressed theological grammar that both Jewish and Christian traditions have unpacked endlessly.

Textual variants: the LXX reading of verse 11 has been much discussed — its subtle shifts have been used to support both substitutionary and purgation readings. The Samaritan Pentateuch aligns with MT on the essentials. 4QLev fragments confirm the verse structure.

Literary devices: inclusio, chiasm, and the H-characteristic motivation clauses. The alternation between ritual prohibition (vv. 1–9) and dietary/theological rationale (vv. 10–16) gives the chapter a balanced feel: it speaks to cult and kitchen together, insisting the two are one domain.

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

**Jewish interpretive trajectory.** Second Temple reception of the blood prohibition is vivid. The Book of Jubilees 7:28–33, 21:18 extends the prohibition with apocalyptic stringency, tracing it to the Noahide covenant (Genesis 9:4) and making it a universal human obligation. The Temple Scroll (11Q19) tightens the centralization requirement. Philo (*Special Laws* 1.201, 4.122–125) rationalizes the blood prohibition as pedagogy in respect for life and restraint from cruelty. Josephus (*Antiquities* 3.260) summarizes.

Rabbinic literature develops the chapter into a detailed legal tradition. *Shechita* — the Jewish ritual slaughter protocol (throat-cutting with a flawless blade, complete drainage of blood, salting and soaking of meat to remove residual blood) — is traced to Leviticus 17 and Deuteronomy 12 in tractate *Chullin*. Sifra on chapter 17 is extensive. Rashi follows the plain sense, explaining *śĕʿîrîm* as demons who appear to those who seek them, and linking the blood prohibition to the pedagogy of life-reverence. Ibn Ezra tends rationalist: blood is prohibited because it carries the life-essence, and consumption would incorporate that essence unnaturally. Ramban (Nachmanides) reads the blood prohibition as cosmologically grounded: blood is *nepeš*, and *nepeš* belongs to God as Creator; consuming blood is usurping what is not ours. Maimonides (*Guide* 3.46) rationalizes by reference to pagan blood-drinking rites: Israel's prohibition trains the people to reject those practices and the theologies undergirding them.

The tension between Leviticus 17 and Deuteronomy 12 is classical rabbinic territory. Leviticus 17's H legislation demands all slaughter be brought to the tabernacle; Deuteronomy 12:15–16, 20–25 permits profane slaughter (*ḥullîn*, non-cultic slaughter) once Israel settles in the land. The Talmud (*Chullin* 16b–17a) harmonizes: Leviticus 17 applied to the wilderness period when the tabernacle was accessible; Deuteronomy addresses the settled land where travel to a single sanctuary would be impractical. Milgrom's modern solution preserves the rabbinic intuition: H represents the wilderness ideal, D accommodates the settled reality. Knohl pushes further: H and D represent differing priestly-prophetic-cum-legal schools with overlapping but distinct theological commitments.

Modern Jewish engagement: Abraham Joshua Heschel's *The Prophets* (1962) engages blood prohibition as part of Israel's ethical distinctiveness. Jonathan Sacks's writings on *kashrut* return to Leviticus 17 for the theological grammar. Isaac Leibowitz (essays on the parashot) reads the blood prohibition as sanctifying the mundane: what we eat is where holiness begins. The state of contemporary kosher practice — from Chief Rabbinate supervision to the 20th-century controversies over stunning in ritual slaughter — is rooted in the legal tradition Leviticus 17 initiates.

**Christian interpretive trajectory.** The earliest Christian engagement is the Jerusalem Council (Acts 15:20, 29; 21:25), where James proposes that Gentile converts abstain from "what has been strangled and from blood" — a direct retention of Leviticus 17's prohibition even for non-Jewish believers in the new covenant community. The decision is theologically puzzling on standard Pauline accounts of ceremonial law abrogation; most scholars (Richard Bauckham, James Dunn, N. T. Wright) argue that Acts 15 is invoking Leviticus 17's extension to the *gēr* (stranger) — Gentile believers are, in the James-logic, analogous to resident aliens in Israel, on whom the blood prohibition is already binding per Leviticus 17:10. The practical concern is table fellowship: Jewish and Gentile believers eating together requires a minimum shared standard, and the Levitical *gēr* laws provide it.

Patristic reception: Tertullian (*Apology* 9) boasts that Christians abstain from blood; the Didascalia and Apostolic Constitutions retain the prohibition. Augustine (*Contra Faustum* 32.13) and Chrysostom debate whether the prohibition is ceremonial (abrogated) or moral (retained). The Eastern Orthodox tradition has largely maintained the prohibition on blood-eating as Apostolic, grounded in Acts 15 and Leviticus 17; Latin Christianity largely did not. Aquinas (*ST* I–II q. 103 a. 4) treats the prohibition as originally ceremonial and no longer binding, though its moral undertone (reverence for life) remains. Calvin (*Institutes* 4.10.14) reads the prohibition as ceremonial and abrogated. Reformation polemics used the chapter in debates over the Mass: if blood belongs on the altar and not on the table, what is the theology of the cup?

Contemporary Christian scholarship has returned to Leviticus 17 primarily for its atonement theology. Verse 11 is the single most quoted Old Testament verse in treatments of substitutionary atonement. John Owen's *The Death of Death in the Death of Christ* (1647) grounds his particular-atonement argument partly in the verse's grammar. Calvin on Hebrews draws extensively. Joseph Ratzinger's *Spirit of the Liturgy* (2000) reads the blood-on-altar theology as the Old Testament's preparation for Eucharistic theology: what Leviticus forbids on the table, Christ gives on the altar, because the Altar *is* the table in his body. Fleming Rutledge's *The Crucifixion* (2015) lays substantial weight on verse 11 for a non-transactional substitutionary atonement. Milgrom's purgation reading (*kippēr* = "purge," not "propitiate") has been extensively engaged by Jay Sklar (*Sin, Impurity, Sacrifice, Atonement*, 2005), Christian Eberhart, and others. Hans Boersma's *Violence, Hospitality, and the Cross* (2004) treats the blood-atonement complex. N. T. Wright's work on atonement (*The Day the Revolution Began*, 2016) reads verse 11 within a covenantal-eschatological frame. Ephraim Radner's Brazos *Leviticus* reads the chapter figurally.

**Where they converge and diverge.** Both traditions read Leviticus 17 as the canonical source of "the life is in the blood" theology. Jewish tradition has preserved the prohibition through two millennia as living practice (*shechita*, salting, rabbinic supervision), while also legally accommodating the shift from sanctuary-slaughter to non-cultic slaughter as codified in Deuteronomy. Christian tradition has largely abrogated the literal prohibition (though Eastern Orthodox and some Jewish-Christian communities retain it), while redeploying verse 11 as the central Old Testament articulation of the theology of atonement that finds its fulfillment in Christ's blood. The divergence is where the blood goes: for Judaism, on the altar (then: in proper ritual slaughter now); for Christianity, in the Eucharistic cup and supremely in Christ's cross. The convergence: blood is not food, blood is life, and life is God's.

## Lens 4 — The Theological Intent

**Tension 1: Is the blood prohibition fundamentally ritual/dietary, or ethical/theological — and what does "the life is in the blood" (v. 11) actually mean?** Milgrom argues the prohibition is ritual-theological: blood is *nepeš*, life-force, and belongs to God alone. Its only legitimate use is on the altar — which is why it must not enter human consumption. Mary Douglas extends this symbolically: blood is the boundary-fluid between life and death, and its proper ritual handling is the grammar of the covenant. Knohl's H reading moralizes: the blood prohibition is ethical pedagogy — reverence for life — and this is H's distinctive contribution. Maimonides (*Guide* 3.46) rationalizes further: the prohibition disciplines Israel against pagan blood-drinking cults. Modern Jewish ethical readings (Heschel, Sacks) emphasize that the prohibition teaches kindness to animals and consciousness of life-taking. Jonathan Klawans (*Purity and Sacrifice in Ancient Judaism*, 2006) distinguishes ritual from moral purity and insists chapter 17 straddles both.

Catholic theology (CCC 613–618 on Christ's sacrifice; Aquinas, *ST* III q. 48 a. 3) reads verse 11 as the Old Testament's articulation of substitutionary-and-sanctifying atonement, fulfilled in Christ. Reformed theology (Calvin; Owen, *The Death of Death*; Horton) builds penal substitution on verse 11's "atonement by the life." Lutheran theology (Luther on Hebrews; Bonhoeffer) similarly reads substitutionary weight. Wesleyan theology emphasizes the sanctifying and transformative effect of the blood. Orthodox theology (Athanasius; Aulén's *Christus Victor*) reads the blood as the price of rescue from the powers, not primarily as penal payment. Jewish critical scholarship (Levenson's *Death and Resurrection of the Beloved Son*) reads verse 11 within a broader vicarious-substitution pattern across the Hebrew Bible.

The least intuitive position is the ritual-theological reading (Milgrom, Douglas), because it refuses both the moralizing reduction (the prohibition is about being nice to animals) and the purely penal reading (the blood pays a debt). Steelman: if blood is literally *nepeš* (life) in the Levitical grammar, then the prohibition is not about ethics or transaction but about ontology. Blood cannot be eaten because life cannot be owned. Life belongs to God as Creator, and the altar is the site where life is returned to its source. On this reading, verse 11's "atonement" is not a transaction but a return: the life God has given is given back, and that giving-back is what makes atonement possible. This is theologically profound because it grounds atonement not in mechanism but in relationship. Christ's blood atones on this reading because it is the fullest instance of life returned to God on behalf of all life.

**Where this leaves us:** ritual and ethical are not rivals in Leviticus 17; they are integrated. The prohibition teaches that life is not ours. This teaching carries ethical weight (reverence for life, restraint in slaughter) but is grounded in a prior ontological claim (life belongs to its Giver). Any atonement theology that ignores either dimension flattens the chapter.

**Tension 2: What is the relationship between Leviticus 17's centralization demand and Deuteronomy 12's permission of profane slaughter — and what does the tension reveal about the composition of the Pentateuch?** The classical rabbinic solution (Bavli Chullin 16b–17a) is developmental: Leviticus 17 applied to the wilderness; Deuteronomy 12 addresses settled life. This is the position of Rashi, Ramban, and most medieval commentators. Modern critical scholarship (Wellhausen, Milgrom, Knohl) treats the two as distinct literary layers from different periods or schools — H in Leviticus 17, D in Deuteronomy 12. Milgrom's synthesis: H represents the wilderness ideal, D accommodates settled reality, and the canonical shape preserves both without dissolving their tension. Knohl reads the relationship as internal priestly-school dialectic. Bernard Levinson (*Deuteronomy and the Hermeneutics of Legal Innovation*, 1997) treats D as deliberately revising earlier legislation, including H. Calvin and Reformed readings collapse the tension by treating both as provisional and pointing forward to Christ, the one altar.

The Jewish rationalist view (Maimonides) and the contemporary scholarly-critical view (Milgrom, Knohl, Levinson) converge on a functional reading: the laws operate in sequence, and the canon preserves both because both articulate something true. The Orthodox and Catholic emphasis on tradition as canonical interpreter (*Dei Verbum* 10) reads the tension as productive — the Torah's own self-interpretation includes its revisions.

The least intuitive position is Levinson's: that Deuteronomy *deliberately revises* Leviticus 17, and the canonical shape preserves both precisely because the canon acknowledges that law evolves with circumstance. Steelman: if the Torah itself models legal revision within its pages, then the reader's task is not to harmonize but to listen to the conversation. What Leviticus 17 establishes as wilderness ideal (all slaughter is sacrifice), Deuteronomy 12 modifies as settled practice (local slaughter permitted, but blood still prohibited). Both laws agree on the non-negotiable: blood is never consumed. The revision concerns the slaughter locus, not the blood prohibition. This is important because it shows the Torah itself distinguishing between negotiable (cultic geography) and non-negotiable (ontological claim about blood and life).

**Where this leaves us:** the Pentateuch's legal material is not a monolith but a canonical conversation. Leviticus 17 opens the conversation with the wilderness-ideal voice; Deuteronomy 12 continues it with the settled-life voice. Contemporary readers who treat the two as flat and identical miss something the text itself is doing. Those who treat the tension as a source problem to be excised miss the theological function of preserving both.

**Unresolved question:** If "the life is in the blood" grounds atonement ontologically rather than transactionally, and if Christian Eucharistic theology fulfills this in the cup that is Christ's blood, what is the theological status of the Acts 15 blood prohibition for Gentile believers — and does its gradual abrogation in Western Christianity (though retained in Eastern Orthodoxy) represent theological development, drift, or the quiet loss of a teaching Leviticus 17 was unwilling to surrender?

## The Thread

Four non-obvious cross-references. First, Genesis 9:4–6: the Noahide covenant explicitly prohibits consuming blood ("you shall not eat flesh with its life, that is, its blood") and grounds human dignity in the shedding of human blood being an offense against the image of God. Leviticus 17 is not innovating; it is extending and intensifying a Noahide principle, which is why Jewish tradition (Jubilees, rabbinic halakhah) treats the blood prohibition as binding on all humanity, not just Israel. This grounds the Acts 15 decision: Gentile believers are under Noahide, not Mosaic, law, but Noahide already contains the blood prohibition. Second, Deuteronomy 12:15–25: the permissive complement to Leviticus 17 — profane slaughter permitted in the settled land, but blood must still be poured out on the ground like water. The canon preserves both laws and thereby preserves the dialectic between wilderness ideal and settled pragmatism. Third, John 6:53–56, Jesus' scandalous discourse: "unless you eat the flesh of the Son of Man and drink his blood, you have no life in you." In a Jewish context formed by Leviticus 17, this is either blasphemy or claim of divine prerogative — Jesus is offering what Leviticus explicitly forbids, which means he is offering himself as the altar and the sacrifice simultaneously. Augustine and Chrysostom both read the Johannine discourse through this Levitical lens. Fourth, Hebrews 9:22, "without the shedding of blood there is no forgiveness of sins" — a near-direct adaptation of Leviticus 17:11's claim that blood atones by life. The epistle's use of chapter 17 is even more fundamental than its explicit citations; the whole logic of Hebrews' atonement theology presupposes the Levitical claim that blood is life, life belongs to God, and therefore the blood of the perfect sacrifice alone makes the ultimate atonement. What Leviticus 17 opened, Hebrews closes — and yet the Acts 15 blood prohibition suggests the early church did not think the chapter was entirely closed even after the cross.
