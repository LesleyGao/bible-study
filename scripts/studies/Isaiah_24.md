# Isaiah 24 — Go Deeper

## Lens 1: Historical World

Isaiah 24 opens what scholars since Bernhard Duhm have called the **Isaiah Apocalypse** — chapters 24-27, a unified literary block whose imagery, scope, and theological vocabulary mark a sharp departure from the OAN cluster (chs 13-23) that precedes it and the Hezekiah narratives (chs 36-39) that conclude First Isaiah. The section is widely regarded as one of the latest sections within Isaiah 1-39, possibly one of the latest in the entire book, and most mainstream-critical scholars place it in the **late post-exilic period**, between the **5th and 3rd centuries BC** (Hans Wildberger's *Jesaja* commentary, Otto Plöger's *Theocracy and Eschatology*, Joseph Blenkinsopp's Anchor Bible commentary, John J. Collins's *The Apocalyptic Imagination*, Christopher Rowland's *The Open Heaven*, J. Todd Hibbard's *Intertextuality in Isaiah 24-27*).

The dating arguments are textual and conceptual: the section's vocabulary clusters with late post-exilic Hebrew (the use of *ʿōlām*, "everlasting/age," and *ʿēt qēṣ*, "time of the end"); its eschatological scope outruns the localized horizons of the 8th-century oracles; its dualistic moral framework (the righteous vs. the wicked of the whole earth) and its cosmic-judgment imagery anticipate Daniel 7-12 (mid-2nd c. BC) and the later apocalypses. The "everlasting covenant" of v. 5 (בְּרִית עוֹלָם, *bĕrît ʿôlām*) is most plausibly read as the Noachic covenant of Gen 9:16 — meaning the breach is humanity's, not Israel's alone, and the judgment is correspondingly universal.

Conservative scholarship (Edward J. Young, J. Alec Motyer, John Oswalt) defends an 8th-century Isaianic origin, reading the cosmic language as the prophet's anticipatory reach beyond the immediate Assyrian crisis to an eschatological horizon. Brevard Childs's canonical approach brackets the dating question and reads the Apocalypse as theologically integrated with the rest of the book. The Great Isaiah Scroll from Qumran (1QIsaᵃ, c. 125 BC) preserves chs 24-27 in continuous form with the rest of Isaiah, fixing whatever editorial development had occurred by the late Second Temple period — the section was already canonical and Isaianically attached well before Christ.

The "Isaiah Apocalypse" label, coined in the 19th century, is technically anachronistic — the section is not a full apocalypse in the Daniel/1 Enoch/Revelation genre sense (no angelic mediator, no symbolic-vision sequences, no pseudonymous attribution). It is better described as **proto-apocalyptic** or **early apocalyptic** — a transitional form between classical prophecy and the developed apocalyptic genre of the late Second Temple period. John Collins's typology in *The Apocalyptic Imagination* identifies Isa 24-27 as one of the key textual bridges. Its features — cosmic scope, dualistic moral structure, eschatological judgment, restoration on the other side — anticipate the genre without yet inhabiting it.

## Lens 2: Literary World

Chapter 24 is unrelenting poetry. Its opening line is one of the most percussive in the Hebrew Bible:

**הִנֵּה יְהוָה בּוֹקֵק הָאָרֶץ וּבוֹלְקָהּ**
*hinnēh YHWH bōqēq hāʾāreṣ ûbōlqāh*
"Behold, Yahweh empties the earth and makes it desolate"

The two verbs **בָּקַק** (*bāqaq*, "to empty out") and **בָּלַק** (*bālaq*, "to lay waste") are nearly homophonous and rare in the Hebrew Bible — *bāqaq* appears only nine times, *bālaq* only here. The collocation is deliberately sound-painting: the earth is unmade by twin verbs that sound like cracks in pottery. The line is followed by a six-fold leveling: priest like people, master like slave, mistress like maid, buyer like seller, lender like borrower, creditor like debtor (v. 2). The Hebrew uses the same word *kā-* ("like") six times, hammering the point: every social hierarchy collapses in the cosmic judgment.

The vocabulary of cosmic scope is dense. **הָאָרֶץ** (*hāʾāreṣ*, "the earth/land") appears more than fifteen times in this single chapter — far more than in any prior Isaiah chapter. Whether it means "the whole earth" or "the land [of Israel]" is debated, but the sweep of vv. 1-13 and 21-23 makes the cosmic reading dominant: this is judgment on the inhabited world, not on a single nation.

Verse 5 names the offense in cosmic-covenantal terms: the inhabitants of the earth have **transgressed the laws** (תּוֹרֹת, *tôrōt*), **violated the statute** (חֹק, *ḥōq*), and **broken the everlasting covenant** (הֵפֵרוּ בְּרִית עוֹלָם, *hēphērû bĕrît ʿôlām*). The "everlasting covenant" most plausibly refers to the Noachic covenant of Gen 9:16, the only OT covenant explicitly extended to all flesh. If so, the chapter is reading Genesis 6-9 forward into eschatology: humanity has again corrupted the earth with violence, and a second un-creation is imminent.

The chapter's central image (vv. 18-20) is one of the most apocalyptic moments in the Hebrew Bible. The Hebrew uses three near-rhyming verbs of cosmic violence:

**רֹעָה הִתְרֹעֲעָה הָאָרֶץ פּוֹר הִתְפּוֹרְרָה אָרֶץ מוֹט הִתְמוֹטְטָה אָרֶץ**
*rōʿāh hitrōʿăʿāh hāʾāreṣ, pôr hitpôrĕrāh ʾāreṣ, môṭ hitmôṭṭāh ʾāreṣ*
"The earth is utterly broken, the earth is split apart, the earth is violently shaken"

The infinitive-absolute-plus-finite construction triples the intensity, and the three verbs — *rāʿaʿ* (to shatter), *pārar* (to break), *môṭ* (to totter) — pile on like aftershocks. The image of the earth "staggering like a drunken man" (v. 20) reverses the Genesis 1 picture of an ordered cosmos: creation un-stabilizes itself under the weight of its own *peshaʿ* (transgression).

The "windows of heaven are opened, and the foundations of the earth tremble" (v. 18) is direct allusion to the flood narrative (Gen 7:11; 8:2), where the same windows opened to drown the world. The Apocalypse is reading Noah's flood as eschatological pattern, not as past event.

The chapter ends (vv. 21-23) with a striking image: Yahweh punishes "the host of heaven, in heaven" (צְבָא הַמָּרוֹם בַּמָּרוֹם, *ṣĕvāʾ hammārôm bammārôm*) — language that anticipates Dan 10's heavenly princes and the Watchers traditions of 1 Enoch. The sun and moon will be "ashamed" before Yahweh's reign on Mt. Zion. This is the inaugural vision of theophanic eschatology that Joel 2:31, Mark 13:24, and Rev 6:12 will all extend.

## Lens 3: Canonical and Theological World

In the prophetic canon, Isaiah 24 is the doorway between **classical prophecy** (judgment-and-restoration of named nations, addressed to historically situated audiences) and **apocalyptic eschatology** (cosmic judgment, dualistic moral structure, end-of-the-age horizon). The chapter does not yet inhabit the full apocalyptic genre — there is no vision of a heavenly throne room (cf. Dan 7), no symbolic beast-vision, no angelic interpreter, no pseudonymous attribution to an ancient seer. But its features are unmistakably proto-apocalyptic, and Christopher Rowland argues in *The Open Heaven* that Isa 24-27 is one of the two or three most important bridges between the prophets and the apocalypses.

The Jewish trajectory has read Isa 24 as a key text for the *yom YHWH* ("day of Yahweh") tradition that runs from Amos 5 through Joel 2 and Zeph 1 into the late Second Temple apocalypses. Rabbinic literature (b. Sanhedrin 97a-99a) treats the chapter as a vision of the *ʿiqveta diMshicha* — the heel-tracks of Messiah — and reads the cosmic upheaval as the labor pains preceding messianic redemption. The vocabulary of v. 23 — Yahweh reigning on Mt. Zion "and his glory will be before his elders" — became foundational for Jewish *malkhut shamayim* ("kingdom of heaven") theology.

The Christian trajectory takes the chapter into the synoptic apocalypses. Mark 13:24-25 ("the sun will be darkened, and the moon will not give its light, and the stars will be falling from heaven, and the powers in the heavens will be shaken") is one of Jesus' Olivet Discourse statements, drawing on Isa 13:10, Isa 24:23, and Joel 2:31 simultaneously. Matthew 24:29 and Luke 21:25-26 carry the same fusion. The line **"the windows of heaven are opened, and the foundations of the earth tremble"** (Isa 24:18) shapes the cataclysmic-judgment language across the NT.

**Revelation** is saturated with Isa 24. Rev 6:12-17 (the sixth seal — the sun goes black, the moon turns to blood, the stars fall, and the kings of the earth hide themselves) reads as a deliberate amplification of Isa 24:18-23. Rev 19:1-3 (the multitude crying "Hallelujah" before the throne) echoes Isa 24:14-16's "they lift up their voices, they sing for joy." The chapter is one of Revelation's structural quarries.

The theological substance — that human transgression makes the earth itself defiled and that the Creator un-makes the earth in judgment — is central to the prophetic theology of land. Hosea 4:1-3 ("there is no faithfulness or steadfast love or knowledge of God in the land. There is swearing, lying, murder, stealing, and committing adultery; they break all bounds, and bloodshed follows bloodshed. Therefore the land mourns") is the close parallel: human evil pollutes the land itself, and the land-as-creature responds. Isaiah 24 universalizes this from the land of Israel to the whole earth.

Augustine's *City of God* 20 reads Isa 24 alongside Mt 24 and Rev 19-20 as a single eschatological fabric. Aquinas's *Catena Aurea* on Mt 24 quotes Isa 24 repeatedly. Calvin's Isaiah commentary, while skeptical of millenarian over-readings, treats Isa 24 as authentic eschatological prophecy fulfilled at the *parousia*. The Catholic Catechism §675-677 on the church's "final ordeal" draws on Isa 24-27 alongside Daniel and Revelation. In Eastern Orthodox liturgy, Isa 24 readings appear in the Great Lent and Holy Week cycles as preparation for the *parousia*.

## Lens 4: Tensions and Honest Reckoning

**Tension 1: The dating of the Isaiah Apocalypse — late post-exilic addition or 8th-century anticipation?** Three positions: (a) The mainstream-critical position (Wildberger, Plöger, Blenkinsopp, Collins, Hibbard) dates Isa 24-27 to the **5th-3rd centuries BC**, treating the section as one of the latest insertions into the Isaiah scroll, with vocabulary, scope, and proto-apocalyptic features that fit the late Persian or early Hellenistic period. (b) The conservative position (Young, Motyer, Oswalt) defends 8th-century Isaianic authorship, reading the cosmic scope as the prophet's anticipatory reach beyond the immediate Assyrian horizon. (c) The canonical position (Childs) brackets the dating question and reads the section as integrated with the larger Isaianic theology, regardless of compositional history. Steelman of the critical position: the literary fingerprints really are different — the Hebrew vocabulary, the proto-apocalyptic features, the dualistic moral framework, the absence of named historical referents, all cluster much more tightly with late post-exilic literature than with 8th-century prophecy. Steelman of the conservative position: the Great Isaiah Scroll integrates the section with the rest of Isaiah without seam, and the proto-apocalyptic features may simply represent the prophet stretching beyond his immediate horizon as Jeremiah and Ezekiel also do. Where this leaves us: the late post-exilic dating of Isa 24-27 is the dominant scholarly view, well-grounded in textual and conceptual evidence; but the section's canonical integration with Isaiah is real and theologically significant. The honest reading: probably composed late, but functionally integrated with the larger book's vision of Yahweh's reign over the nations. The conservative defense of unity is theologically motivated more than textually grounded; the critical dating is textually grounded but should not be over-leveraged into denying the section's prophetic legitimacy.

**Tension 2: The proto-apocalyptic genre — bridge or break?** Three positions: (a) The continuity reading (most patristic and Reformed commentary) treats Isa 24-27 as classical prophecy at full stretch — eschatological in scope but generically continuous with Isa 1-39. (b) The break reading (some critical commentary) treats Isa 24-27 as a generic departure that introduces apocalyptic features alien to classical prophecy and points forward to Daniel and Revelation. (c) The bridge reading (Collins, Rowland, Hibbard, J. J. M. Roberts) treats the section as a transitional form — proto-apocalyptic — that genuinely participates in classical prophetic theology while inaugurating features that the apocalypses will develop. Steelman of the bridge reading: Isa 24-27 lacks the full apparatus of Daniel or 1 Enoch (no angelic interpreter, no symbolic vision sequences, no pseudonymity), but it has the cosmic scope, dualistic moral structure, and end-of-the-age horizon that the apocalypses will inherit. Where this leaves us: bridge is the most accurate descriptor, and reading Isa 24-27 as the proto-apocalyptic linchpin between prophets and apocalypses illuminates both directions of the canonical conversation.

**Tension 3: The "everlasting covenant" of v. 5 and the universal scope of judgment.** If the broken covenant is the Noachic covenant of Gen 9 (the most plausible reading), then the chapter's judgment falls on all humanity for violations any human can commit (bloodshed, transgression of basic creational order). If it is the Sinai covenant or some other Israel-specific covenant, the scope is narrower. Steelman of the Noachic reading: the Hebrew *bĕrît ʿôlām* in the singular and unmodified is most naturally Gen 9:16's covenant; the universalizing scope of the chapter (vv. 1-3, 21) requires a universal covenantal frame; and the violence-pollutes-the-land logic of v. 5 mirrors Gen 6:11-13 directly. Where this leaves us: the chapter is reading Noah's covenant as still binding on all humanity, with humanity's violation precipitating a second un-creation. This makes Isa 24 one of the most theologically integrated chapters in the prophetic canon — knit to Genesis at one end and to Revelation at the other.

**Closing question:** When the prophet sees the earth itself stagger like a drunken man under the weight of human transgression, what does it mean that the cosmos is not neutral background to human action but a creature that mourns, languishes, and falls?
