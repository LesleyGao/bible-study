# 1 Corinthians 6 — Go Deeper

## Lens 1: The Historical World

The Corinth that received this letter in roughly AD 54 was a Roman colony with Roman habits, and the Roman habit at issue in 6:1–8 was litigation. Bruce Winter, in *After Paul Left Corinth* (2001), assembled the legal-papyrological evidence and showed that the Greek-Roman elite of Achaia made enthusiastic use of the courts; lawsuits were a recognized arena of status display, the legal profession a recognized cursus, and forum litigation a piece of civic theater. Roman law required litigants to be of comparable social standing in many cases; the practical effect was that the wealthier Corinthian Christians were dragging the less wealthy before magistrates whose decisions tracked the social hierarchy of the colony rather than the equities of the case. The Erastus pavement inscription, found near the Corinthian theater—"Erastus aedile (or *oikonomos*) paid for this pavement at his own expense"—gives a face to that elite stratum within the assembly. Paul's question in 6:5—"is there really no one wise enough among you to arbitrate?"—is shaped by the Jewish institution of the *beit din*, where members of a community settled disputes internally. The Corinthians had Roman magistrates and Greek philosophical arbitration as alternatives; Paul wants them to recover something closer to the Jewish pattern.

Then the chapter pivots, in 6:9–11, to a vice list and a baptismal claim. Vice lists are a known Hellenistic-Jewish form (Wisdom of Solomon 14, Philo's *De Specialibus Legibus*, the Sibylline Oracles), but Paul's list is shaped by the polemical edge of Diaspora-Jewish critique of Greco-Roman sexual culture. The flashpoint terms are *malakoi* (μαλακοί) and *arsenokoitai* (ἀρσενοκοῖται), and the historical questions about what each refers to in first-century Roman Corinth are not academic. *Malakos* in ordinary Greek meant "soft," covering everything from luxurious clothing to cowardice to effeminate self-presentation, and in some contexts the passive partner in male-male intercourse. *Arsenokoitēs* is a Pauline-period coinage—it does not appear in Greek literature before Paul—and the construction (*arsen* + *koitēs*, "male" + "lying with") closely tracks the Septuagint of Leviticus 18:22 and 20:13 (*meta arsenos ou koimēthēsē koitēn gynaikos*), which is the strongest argument that Paul forged the term out of Levitical law.

Then 6:12–20 turns to *porneia* and the body. The slogans Paul quotes—"all things are lawful for me," "food for the stomach"—are most likely catchphrases from a wing of the Corinthian assembly that drew dualistic conclusions from Paul's own gospel of freedom. Dale Martin's *The Corinthian Body* (1995) reads the chapter against the background of Greco-Roman medical and philosophical views of the body as a porous, hierarchically ordered system in which sexual contact was a matter of pollution categories. Paul does not buy the dualism, but he uses categories his audience would recognize: the body is a temple, *naos pneumatos hagiou* (ναὸς πνεύματος ἁγίου), and porneia is a violation of sacred space.

The textual witnesses—P46 (c. AD 200), Sinaiticus, Vaticanus—are stable for chapter 6; the storms are interpretive, not text-critical.

## Lens 2: How the Literature Works

The chapter has three movements that look loosely connected but are bound by a single argument about the public visibility of the church as a body.

- **6:1–8**: Lawsuits among believers. Five rhetorical questions (*ouk oidate*, "do you not know?") press the Corinthians toward shame.
- **6:9–11**: Vice list, exclusion from the kingdom, and baptismal reversal ("such were some of you, but you were washed").
- **6:12–20**: The body, porneia, and the temple of the Spirit, structured around quoted slogans Paul corrects.

The repeated *ouk oidate* (used six times in the chapter, more concentrated than anywhere else in the letter) is rhetorically pointed. Margaret Mitchell's analysis of the deliberative architecture of 1 Corinthians shows Paul using the formula to remind the Corinthians of teaching they already possessed, framing his rebuke as a recall to known truth rather than a new revelation. The repeated diatribe-style questioning is a Hellenistic philosophical move that would have read as recognizably high-rhetorical to the Corinthians' own self-image.

The Greek of 6:9 is the famous battlefield. *Malakos* (μαλακός): the lexical range runs from "soft, luxurious" through "morally lax" to, in some contexts, "effeminate" or "passive partner." Robin Scroggs (*The New Testament and Homosexuality*, 1983) and John Boswell (*Christianity, Social Tolerance, and Homosexuality*, 1980) argued that in Paul's sexual context the term is technical for the passive partner in pederasty. Dale Martin (*Sex and the Single Savior*) argued for a broader social meaning—the man who fails the masculine ideal. Robert Gagnon (*The Bible and Homosexual Practice*) and Richard Hays (*The Moral Vision of the New Testament*) defend the traditional reading: *malakoi* paired with *arsenokoitai* identifies both partners in male-male sex. *Arsenokoitēs* (ἀρσενοκοίτης), the harder term, is a *hapax* in pre-Christian Greek; it appears here and at 1 Timothy 1:10 and then enters patristic vocabulary. Hays, Wright, Gagnon, and most traditional readers argue from the Levitical morphology that Paul coined it to designate the active partner in male-male intercourse generally. Boswell narrowed it to the pederastic exploiter; Scroggs read it as the man who hires male prostitutes; Dale Martin proposed an economic-sexual exploitation reading; James Brownson treats the term as too obscure to translate confidently. The translation history tracks the debate: RSV (1946) "homosexuals" → NRSV "male prostitutes, sodomites" → ESV "men who practice homosexuality."

The *porneia* section uses the slogan-and-correction pattern Paul had used in chapter 5 and earlier. *Panta moi exestin* (πάντα μοι ἔξεστιν), "all things are permitted to me," is almost certainly a Corinthian quotation; Paul agrees with the form and reframes the substance. *Sōma* (σῶμα) recurs eight times in 6:13–20—Paul is hammering. *Exousiazō* (ἐξουσιάζω) at 6:12 ("not be brought under the power of anything") prefigures the marriage discussion in chapter 7 where the same verb describes mutual conjugal authority; the resonance is intentional.

## Lens 3: Where This Sits in the Larger Story

The **Jewish trajectory** in this chapter is harder to ignore than in chapter 5. Leviticus 18 and 20 are the proof texts the Septuagintal vocabulary of *arsenokoitēs* is built from. Diaspora Judaism's critique of Greco-Roman sexual practice—Philo's lengthy treatments in *De Specialibus Legibus* 3, Pseudo-Phocylides' Sentences, the Sibylline Oracles 2, Wisdom of Solomon 14—was a recognized literary genre, and Paul stands inside it. Josephus (*Contra Apionem* 2.199) defends Jewish law against Hellenistic moral pluralism in the same idiom. The Jewish-Hellenistic critique was specifically aimed at the institutionalized forms of Greek same-sex practice (pederasty, master-slave sexual use), which is why some revisionist readers (Boswell, Scroggs) argue that Paul's target is also institutionally specific. The traditional answer is that Diaspora-Jewish ethics reasoned from the Levitical proscription as a categorical norm, not as a critique of one specific institutional form, and Paul wrote inside that reasoning.

The **Christian trajectory** has been, until very recently, remarkably uniform. Clement of Alexandria (*Paedagogus* 2–3), Chrysostom (*Hom.* 4 on Romans), Augustine, and the entire patristic tradition read 6:9 as a categorical proscription. Aquinas (*ST* II-II q. 154) systematized it. The Reformers continued the reading without question; the dispute was over how to discipline, not over what was proscribed. The Catholic Catechism (CCC 2357–2359), the *Persona Humana* declaration (1975), and *Dignitas Personae* (2008) preserve this reading. Eastern Orthodox teaching is uniform with it.

The contemporary fracture begins in the late twentieth century. John Boswell's 1980 *Christianity, Social Tolerance, and Homosexuality* argued that the medieval church had tolerated same-sex relationships and that the New Testament terms had been mistranslated. Robin Scroggs followed in 1983 with the pederasty thesis. Richard Hays's *The Moral Vision of the New Testament* (1996) and Robert Gagnon's *The Bible and Homosexual Practice* (2001) constitute the major scholarly defenses of the traditional reading. James Brownson's *Bible, Gender, Sexuality* (2013), Matthew Vines's *God and the Gay Christian* (2014), and Ken Wilson's *A Letter to My Congregation* assemble the affirming case. Mainline Protestant denominations have splintered along this fault line through the 2000s and 2010s; ECUSA, ELCA, PCUSA, and the UMC's recent formal split are all, in part, downstream of how 6:9 is read. Catholic teaching has not changed; Eastern Orthodox teaching has not changed; the Global South Anglican communion has explicitly rejected revisionist readings.

The **other** Christian trajectory in this chapter—the lawsuits passage—has its own history. Tertullian and the early apologists pointed to internal Christian arbitration as a mark of the church's superiority over pagan civic culture. The Constantinian church absorbed this into the bishop's *audientia episcopalis*, an in-house ecclesiastical court system the empire eventually recognized. The Anabaptist tradition went further: Schleitheim (1527) refused the sword altogether, and Mennonite, Hutterite, and Amish communities have generally avoided civil litigation among believers as a matter of confession. Mainline and Catholic readings have generally limited 6:1–8 to internal disputes among believers, allowing recourse to civil courts for genuinely civil matters.

## Lens 4: What Is Theologically at Stake

**Tension 1: What *malakoi* and *arsenokoitai* mean, and what Christian ethics requires today.**

The *traditional* reading (Hays, Gagnon, Wright, the Catholic *Catechism*, Eastern Orthodox teaching, confessional Protestantism) holds that the terms together name male same-sex activity categorically; that Paul's reasoning is rooted in Genesis 1–2 and Leviticus 18, 20; that the early Christian and Jewish reception of the texts was uniform on this; and that Christian sexual ethics today must continue to teach this even where it cuts against contemporary moral intuitions. Hays, in particular, argues that the moral logic does not depend on a thin word-study but on the canonical pattern of male-female complementarity that the New Testament assumes throughout.

The *revisionist* reading (Boswell, Scroggs, Dale Martin, Brownson, Vines) argues that the terms are either institutionally specific (pederasty, prostitution, exploitation) or too obscure to ground a categorical prohibition; that the cultural context of male-male sex in Paul's world was overwhelmingly exploitative; and that loving, mutual, covenanted same-sex partnerships are simply not what Paul had in view. Brownson works hardest on the canonical-theological side: he argues that the underlying moral concern is exploitation, not gender complementarity, and that contemporary same-sex covenants meet the moral concern.

**Steelman the revisionist case**, since most readers approach the passage already convinced of the traditional reading. The argument is not that Paul approved of male-male sex; it is that what Paul knew, and what he proscribed, was the world of pederastic and exploitative male-male sex documented in Greco-Roman literature—the world where adult elite men used boys, slaves, and prostitutes. The revisionist argues that contemporary Christian same-sex couples in covenanted partnerships are not in the population Paul addressed, and that to import a modern category called "homosexuality" into a first-century vice list is anachronistic. The strongest version of this argument concedes that Paul, if asked, would still have proscribed same-sex covenants—but argues that Paul's reasoning, on closer examination, depends on assumptions about gender hierarchy and procreative natural law that the church has otherwise abandoned, and so the proscription does not survive on its own foundations.

The traditional response, equally serious, observes that *arsenokoitēs* is built from Leviticus, that the Levitical proscription is categorical and not contextual, that early Jewish and Christian reception is uniform, that the Genesis 1–2 background of male-female pairing runs through the entire New Testament's sexual ethics, and that the hermeneutical move from "Paul did not know X" to "Paul would have approved X" requires a level of confidence in our reconstruction of Paul's reasoning that the texts do not warrant. Hays's specific point is that the *narrative pattern* of male-female complementarity in scripture—not the lexicography of two terms—does the moral work, and the lexicography only confirms what the larger pattern already establishes.

**Tension 2: Whether 6:1–8 forbids all civil litigation among believers, or only intramural disputes.**

The *Catholic and mainline Protestant* reading: the passage applies to disputes that ought to be settled within the church—property, debts, defamation among believers—not to genuinely civil matters with state-sanctioned outcomes (criminal cases, regulatory matters). Christian arbitration boards, ecclesiastical courts, and modern parachurch mediation services are the institutional inheritance of this reading.

The *Reformed* reading: similar to the Catholic, but with a heavier emphasis on the consistory or session as the proper first court for disputes among church members.

The *Anabaptist* reading: serious Christians do not go to court at all, against anyone, on the basis of the broader pacifist commitment that joins 6:1–8 with the Sermon on the Mount. The Mennonite and Hutterite traditions have institutionalized this.

Where this leaves us: the chapter assumes a community that takes its own internal life seriously enough to resolve its own disputes and to hold its own ethical standards, including in matters as intimate as sexual practice. The contemporary church's struggle is not simply with one issue or another; it is with whether such a community is still possible.

**One unresolved question:** the relationship between 6:11 ("such were some of you, but you were washed") and the trajectory of sanctification. Is Paul claiming that baptismal regeneration ended the patterns named in the vice list as ongoing realities? Or is he claiming a status before God that holds even while the patterns are still being mortified? The pastoral and ethical stakes are large, and serious Christians read the verse both ways. The Wesleyan tradition tilts toward the first reading (entire sanctification as a real possibility); the Reformed tradition holds the second more firmly (justification declares a verdict that sanctification works out over a lifetime); Catholic teaching sits between, with sacramental grace as ongoing efficacious cause. Paul's perfect-tense verbs—*apelousasthe*, *hēgiasthēte*, *edikaiōthēte*—describe completed actions, but the ethical exhortation that follows assumes the actions are not yet completely lived out.

## The Thread

- **Romans 1:26–27** — Paul's other major treatment of same-sex practice, and the only New Testament passage that names female same-sex activity. The theological logic (gender complementarity rooted in creation, the exchange of natural for unnatural use) parallels 6:9.
- **Leviticus 18:22; 20:13** — the source of the *arsenokoitēs* construction. Reading 6:9 without the Septuagint of these verses produces shallow exegesis in either direction.
- **Genesis 2:24** — Paul cites it directly in 6:16 ("the two shall become one flesh"), pulling porneia into the gravity of creation theology rather than treating it as a discrete sin. The body is not a tool; it is a covenanted location.
- **Matthew 18:15–17** — paired with 6:1–8 in most traditions as the basis for internal Christian dispute resolution.

The chapter ends with one of the most striking sentences in Paul: "you are not your own; you were bought with a price." Whatever else 6:9–20 is doing, it refuses to make the body a private possession. The lawsuit passage and the porneia passage join here. The body, like the bank account, is owned in common with Christ, and the church is the visible form of that common ownership. The practical question the chapter leaves open is what kind of community would actually be capable of acting that way—and whether the contemporary church is willing to be one.
