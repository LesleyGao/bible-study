# Isaiah 50 — Go Deeper

## Lens 1: The Historical World

Isaiah 50 sits squarely within what scholars since Bernhard Duhm's 1892 commentary have called **Second Isaiah** (*Deutero-Isaiah*, chapters 40-55), a discrete literary unit composed during the Babylonian exile, conventionally dated to roughly 545-540 BC, in the closing years of Babylonian dominance and the rising shadow of Cyrus the Persian. Where First Isaiah (chs. 1-39) addresses the Assyrian crisis of the eighth century, Second Isaiah speaks to a community that has already suffered the catastrophe Isaiah 1-39 anticipated: Jerusalem destroyed (587 BC), the temple burned, the Davidic monarchy ended, the elite deported to the rivers of Babylon. The voice of Second Isaiah is anonymous — there is no superscription, no autobiographical anchor, no king's reign by which to date the oracles. The prophet speaks from within exile, to exiles, of a homecoming that has not yet happened but that Yahweh is about to bring to pass.

The composite-authorship debate is not optional background; it shapes how the chapter is read. The mainstream-critical consensus — represented by Claus Westermann, Joseph Blenkinsopp, John Goldingay, Roy Melugin, Patricia Tull, R. N. Whybray, and earlier James Muilenburg — holds that Second Isaiah is the work of an anonymous exilic prophet (or a prophetic school), distinct from the eighth-century Isaiah ben Amoz. The unified-defense position — represented by Alec Motyer, John Oswalt, and others in the evangelical tradition — argues that the entire book is the work of the eighth-century Isaiah, prophetically projected forward. Brevard Childs's canonical approach largely sidesteps the historical question and reads the book as a unified theological whole that the believing community received as Scripture. The Great Isaiah Scroll from Qumran (1QIsaᵃ), dated paleographically to roughly 125 BC, preserves the entire book as a single continuous text with no break between chapters 39 and 40 — proof that by the late Second Temple period the book was already a unified scroll, but not proof of single authorship. For the present purpose, I follow the mainstream-critical reading: chapter 50 was composed by the exilic prophet, and its addressees are Judeans in Babylon who are wondering whether Yahweh has divorced them for good.

That divorce metaphor is not incidental. The chapter opens with a forensic challenge from Yahweh: *"Where is your mother's certificate of divorce, with which I sent her away? Or which of my creditors is it to whom I have sold you?"* (50:1). Hebrew **סֵפֶר כְּרִיתוּת** (*sefer keritut*, "scroll of cutting-off") is the legal term used in Deuteronomy 24:1-4 for the document a man gave his wife when he divorced her. Yahweh is invoking the legal vocabulary of marriage and creditor-slavery — both familiar from the social world of the exiles — to make a precise theological point. The mother (Zion / Judah) was sent away, the children (the exiles) were sold, but no divorce certificate was ever issued, no creditor ever held the bond. The exile is real; the rupture is not final. The exiles were sold *for their iniquities* (Hebrew **בַּעֲוֹנֹתֵיכֶם**, *ba-avonoteichem*), not because Yahweh's hand was shortened (50:2 — **הֲקָצוֹר קָצְרָה יָדִי**, *haqatsor qatsrah yadi*, "is my hand really too short?"). The same arm that dried up the Reed Sea at the Exodus, that clothes the heavens in blackness, is still the arm that will redeem.

Verses 4-9 then shift voice. A first-person speaker emerges — the **Servant of Yahweh** — who speaks in confessional tones of his own discipleship, his suffering, and his confidence in vindication. This is the **Third Servant Song** (50:4-9, with most scholars extending the unit through verse 11 as a hortatory coda). Duhm's 1892 commentary first isolated four "Servant Songs" (42:1-4; 49:1-6; 50:4-9; 52:13-53:12) as a distinct literary stratum within Second Isaiah, marked by their first-person or third-person focus on a single figure called *eved Yahweh* (**עֶבֶד יְהוָה**, "servant of the LORD"). The Songs share vocabulary, theology, and an arc: from commissioning, to mission to the nations, to suffering, to the climactic atoning death and exaltation in Song Four. Whether the Songs were originally independent compositions secondarily inserted, or whether they are integral to Second Isaiah's structure, remains disputed (Mettinger's *A Farewell to the Servant Songs* argued against treating them as a separate stratum; most contemporary scholars retain Duhm's category as a literary observation while reading the Songs in their canonical context).

## Lens 2: The Literature

The chapter divides cleanly into three movements: a divine disputation (1-3), the Servant's confession (4-9), and a closing exhortation about light and darkness (10-11). The form-critical genre of verses 1-3 is the **rib** (**רִיב**, "lawsuit"), a courtroom oracle in which Yahweh acts as both plaintiff and judge against his own people. The rhetorical questions — *"Where is the certificate? Why was no one there when I came? Is my hand shortened?"* — are not requests for information but legal challenges that expect the answer "nowhere," "no reason," "no." The disputation form is a Second Isaiah signature (cf. 40:12-31; 41:1-7; 43:8-13) and serves the prophet's pastoral aim: to dismantle the exiles' assumption that Yahweh's silence means Yahweh's absence.

The Servant's confession in 4-9 is a tightly constructed unit framed by the formula **אֲדֹנָי יְהוִה** (*Adonai Yahweh*, "the Lord GOD"), which appears four times (vv. 4, 5, 7, 9) — at the opening, the middle, and the closing — and acts as a structural skeleton. The opening line introduces the Hebrew that gives this Song its character: *"The Lord GOD has given me the tongue of those who are taught"* — Hebrew **לְשׁוֹן לִמּוּדִים** (*leshon limmudim*), literally "tongue of the disciples / taught ones." The noun *limmudim* derives from **לָמַד** (*lamad*, "to learn"), and it depicts the Servant as a disciple in an ongoing pedagogical relationship with Yahweh. Morning by morning Yahweh awakens his ear (**יָעִיר לִי אֹזֶן**, *ya'ir li ozen*) — the verb is repetitive, daily, pastoral. The Servant is not a self-generated visionary but a learner who has himself first been sustained, and only on that basis does he sustain *the weary* (**יָעֵף**, *ya'ef*).

The shift to suffering in verse 6 is abrupt and visceral: *"I gave my back to those who strike, and my cheeks to those who pull out the beard; I hid not my face from disgrace and spitting."* Hebrew **גֵּוִי נָתַתִּי לְמַכִּים** (*gevi natati le-makim*, "my back I gave to those who strike") uses the active voice — the Servant *gives* his back; he is not merely passive. *Cheeks to those who pull out the beard* (**לְחָיַי לְמֹרְטִים**, *lechayai le-mortim*) and *spitting* (**רֹק**, *roq*) are public-shaming acts in the ancient Near East. To pull out a man's beard was to strip him of honor; to spit on his face was to mark him as ritually and socially repulsive. Yet the Servant's response in verse 7 inverts the shame: *"I have set my face like a flint"* (**שַׂמְתִּי פָנַי כַּחַלָּמִישׁ**, *samti panai ka-challamish*). Flint is the hardest accessible stone in the Levantine landscape; the imagery is of the Servant's face as unyielding rock, refusing the disorienting power of disgrace.

The forensic section (vv. 8-9) returns to lawsuit vocabulary — **מַצְדִּיקִי** (*matsdiqi*, "my vindicator"), **בַּעַל מִשְׁפָּטִי** (*ba'al mishpati*, "my legal opponent"), **יַרְשִׁיעֵנִי** (*yarshi'eni*, "will declare me guilty"). The Servant presents himself in the courtroom of the cosmos and dares any accuser to step forward. The closing image — *"all of them will wear out like a garment; the moth will eat them up"* — is the same trope used of the chaos-powers in Isaiah 51:6, 8: human accusers are ephemeral; Yahweh's vindication is durable.

The closing exhortation (vv. 10-11) is structured by a deliberate contrast between two communities: those who **fear the LORD and obey the voice of his servant** (v. 10) and those who **kindle their own fire** (v. 11). The first walk in darkness yet trust; the second walk by self-made torches yet lie down in torment. The Hebrew **תּוֹרְכֵם בְּזֹאת** (*torkhem be-zot*, literally "from my hand will be this for you") is an oracular pronouncement of doom on those who substitute their own light for the Servant's word. The Song thus ends not with the Servant's vindication alone but with a community-shaping summons: align with the Servant or kindle your own fire and burn in it.

## Lens 3: The Larger Story

Within the prophetic canon, the Third Servant Song deepens a portrait being assembled across all four Songs. The First Song (42:1-4) presents the Servant as a chosen, Spirit-endowed figure who brings *mishpat* (justice) to the nations without raising his voice. The Second Song (49:1-6) extends his mission from Israel to the nations and introduces the paradox that the Servant's labor seems vain. The Third Song (50:4-9) portrays the Servant as **a disciple who suffers public humiliation yet trusts Yahweh's vindication**. The Fourth Song (52:13-53:12) will then resolve the paradox: the Servant's suffering is not merely endured — it is **vicarious**, atoning, salvific. Read sequentially, the Songs trace a narrative arc that culminates in death and exaltation. The Third Song is the hinge: it introduces concrete bodily suffering without yet revealing its purpose.

The Jewish trajectory of Servant interpretation is varied and important. The earliest extant Jewish reading is the **Targum Jonathan** to Isaiah (probably first-century AD or earlier), which renders the Servant Songs in a way that frequently identifies the Servant with the Messiah — most strikingly inserting "the Messiah" at 52:13, but then redistributing the suffering to Israel and the nations, an interpretive move that scholars like Samson H. Levey and Jostein Ådna have analyzed in detail. **Talmud Sanhedrin 98b** records a tradition that the Messiah's name might be "the leper of the house of Rabbi" because Isaiah 53:4 says he bore our diseases — an explicitly messianic-sufferer reading attested in rabbinic literature. After the Christian appropriation of Isaiah 53 became polemically charged, the dominant rabbinic reading, articulated most forcefully by **Rashi** (1040-1105) in his Isaiah commentary, shifted to reading the Servant as **corporate Israel**, suffering at the hands of the nations. This corporate reading has remained dominant in Jewish tradition and is robustly defended in modern Jewish scholarship — Jon Levenson's *The Death and Resurrection of the Beloved Son* and Daniel Boyarin's *The Jewish Gospels* and *Border Lines* show that suffering-and-vindication motifs were native to Second Temple Judaism long before Christian interpretation.

The Christian trajectory reads the Songs Christologically, and chapter 50 in particular as anticipating the Passion. The New Testament does not directly cite Isaiah 50:6, but the **Synoptic Passion narratives describe its imagery with striking specificity**: in Matthew 26:67, *"they spat in his face and struck him"*; in Mark 14:65, *"some began to spit on him and to cover his face and to strike him"*; in Luke 22:63-65, *"the men who were holding Jesus in custody were mocking him as they beat him."* The literary echo is unmistakable — *gevi natati le-makim, lechayai le-mortim* — back to the strikers, cheeks to those who pulled out the beard. Matthew, Mark, and Luke are not quoting Isaiah 50:6 as proof-text; they are describing the same scene. Whether one reads this as the Servant Song *predicting* the Passion or as the evangelists *patterning* their accounts on Isaiah 50:6 (or both), the textual relationship is dense.

The **face-like-flint** image of 50:7 is also picked up in Luke 9:51, where *"Jesus set his face to go to Jerusalem"* — Greek *to prosōpon estērisen* echoes the Septuagint's translation of *samti panai ka-challamish*. The exalted patristic tradition (Cyril of Alexandria, Eusebius, Augustine in his *Tractates on John*) read 50:6-7 as a prophetic depiction of Christ's resolute movement toward the cross. In modern Catholic teaching, the *Catechism of the Catholic Church* §601 places the Servant Songs (especially Song Four) at the heart of Christ's atoning mission. Protestant readings have similarly woven 50:4-9 into Christological devotion — the morning-by-morning awakening of the disciple's ear, the tongue trained to sustain the weary, the back given to strikers — as a pattern not only fulfilled in Christ but extended in the suffering of his disciples (a reading prominent in Bonhoeffer's *Discipleship*).

## Lens 4: Theological Intent

The deepest tension in Isaiah 50 is the question of who the Servant is and what his suffering accomplishes. The text itself does not name the Servant; the verses move from disputation (where Yahweh speaks) to first-person confession (where the Servant speaks) to exhortation (where, ambiguously, the Servant or the prophet addresses the audience). The interpretive options are well known: the Servant is corporate Israel; the Servant is a faithful remnant within Israel; the Servant is the prophet himself; the Servant is the Messiah; the Servant is Christ. Mainstream-critical scholarship (Westermann, Blenkinsopp, Goldingay) tends toward a *fluid* reading: the Servant figure oscillates among these referents, sometimes within the same Song, and the text resists a single fixed identification. Christian-canonical readings (Childs, Wright, Motyer) read the Songs together and find in them an emerging portrait that finally requires an individual representative figure.

What is striking about the Third Servant Song specifically is its **theology of suffering-without-shame**. The Servant *suffers* — his back is struck, his beard is plucked, his face is spat upon — but he refuses the social meaning of that suffering. *I have not been disgraced* (50:7); *I shall not be put to shame* (50:7); *who will declare me guilty?* (50:9). In an honor-shame culture, public spitting *is* disgrace; the Servant's claim that he is not disgraced is not a denial of the physical reality but a re-narration of its meaning. Yahweh's nearness as vindicator (**מַצְדִּיקִי קָרוֹב**, *matsdiqi qarov*, "my vindicator is near") relocates the Servant's identity outside the social judgment of his strikers. This is a major theological move: it severs honor from social reception and re-anchors it in Yahweh's verdict. Paul's later language of *"if God is for us, who can be against us?"* (Rom 8:31) and *"it is the Lord who judges me"* (1 Cor 4:4) operate within this Isaianic logic.

The steelman for the **corporate-Israel** reading: in Second Isaiah, "Israel" is repeatedly named as Yahweh's servant (41:8-9; 44:1-2, 21; 45:4; 48:20) — the term *eved Yahweh* applies to the nation before it applies to any individual. The exiles, scattered and shamed, are precisely the body that has had its back struck and its beard pulled. To read 50:4-9 as Israel's collective confession is textually grounded and theologically powerful: it locates the suffering of the exilic community within Yahweh's redemptive purpose rather than outside it. Levenson's work on the *barren-mother* and *beloved-son* traditions in Hebrew Scripture shows that Israel's vocation has always involved representative suffering for the nations.

The steelman for the **individual-Servant** (and ultimately Christological) reading: the Songs increasingly distinguish the Servant *from* Israel — Isaiah 49:5-6 has the Servant given the task of *bringing Jacob back* and being *a light for the nations*, which requires the Servant to be a figure within or beside Israel rather than identical to it. The first-person voice of 50:4-9 has a singular, individual quality — the Servant is a *learner*, taught morning by morning, with a tongue, an ear, a back, cheeks, a face. The progression toward Song Four, where the Servant dies and is then *seen* by his disciples, presses toward an individual figure. Acts 8:34, where the Ethiopian eunuch asks Philip *"about whom does the prophet say this, about himself or about someone else?"*, treats the question as genuinely open — and Philip's answer points to Jesus. The early Christian reading is not arbitrary imposition; it is one resolution of a question the text itself raises.

**Where this leaves us:** the Third Servant Song is best read as both a window into Israel's exilic vocation and a portrait of an emerging individual figure who embodies that vocation in concentrated form. The text resists collapse in either direction. Christian readers who recognize Christ in 50:6 should be honest that the corporate reading is not a Jewish evasion but a textually grounded interpretation with its own deep canonical roots. Jewish readers who read the Servant as Israel should be honest that pre-rabbinic Jewish tradition entertained messianic-sufferer readings, and that the move to a strictly corporate reading was, in part, polemical. The post-*Nostra Aetate* horizon of Christian-Jewish dialogue requires that both communities hold their readings without erasing the other.

**Closing question:** when the Servant says *"morning by morning he awakens my ear,"* what does daily discipleship — the slow, repetitive, unglamorous training of attention to Yahweh's voice — look like in a culture that rewards velocity and self-projection rather than patient listening? The Song does not answer; it hands the question forward.

## The Thread

Chapter 50 is the third panel in a four-part portrait. The Servant has been called, sent to the nations, and now is shown enduring public humiliation while trusting in vindication. The hortatory close — *fear the LORD and obey the voice of his servant; do not kindle your own fire* — sets up chapters 51-52, where Yahweh's arm awakens, Zion is summoned to rise, and the watchmen on the mountains announce *good news*. The face set like flint here will become, in chapter 52:13-53:12, the face that was *marred beyond human semblance* (52:14) — and through that marring, the many will be made righteous. The Third Song does not yet say why the Servant suffers. The Fourth Song will.
