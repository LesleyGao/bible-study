# Job 11: The Harshest Friend and the Limits of God

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

Zophar the Naamathite enters the dialogue in chapter 11 as the third and youngest of Job's visitors. His designation *ha-Na'amati* (הַנַּעֲמָתִי) identifies him with a place called Naamah — likely Naamah in Arabia, east of the Jordan, not the Naamah of Josh 15:41 in Judah. The three friends together — Eliphaz the Temanite (Edom), Bildad the Shuhite (a tribe near the middle Euphrates, known from the *Mari* texts), and Zophar the Naamathite — form a triad from the arc of the eastern ancient Near Eastern wisdom tradition. Their geography is the geography of sages, the very "people of the east" (*bene qedem*, 1 Kgs 4:30) whose wisdom 1 Kings says Solomon's exceeded. The Joban setting places the dialogue outside the covenant community deliberately, among the wisdom-saturated tribal leaderships of the Transjordanian and north-Arabian plateau.

Dating the book continues to divide scholarship. Pope (Anchor Bible) and Clines (Word Biblical Commentary) lean post-exilic on philological grounds — Aramaisms, hapax legomena (chapter 11 contains several including *ta'alumot* "secrets," v. 6, and *ta'im* "silence" by shaming, v. 3), and the character of the theological debate. Newsom situates the book in the post-exilic Persian period as part of a broader wisdom-crisis literature. Gordis, Andersen, and Hartley (NICOT) hold to a pre-exilic or at least pre-Deuteronomistic date, arguing that the archaic patriarchal setting is not mere coloring. Ezekiel 14:14, 20 (sixth c. BCE) treats Job as a legendary righteous figure alongside Noah and Daniel, which Kaufmann, Greenberg, and Block all take as evidence that a Job tradition already existed well before that date even if the dialogue's final form is later.

Chapter 11 presupposes a worldview shared across Mesopotamian wisdom. The *Ludlul Bēl Nēmeqi* ("I will praise the Lord of Wisdom") and the *Babylonian Theodicy* contain the same friend-structure: a sufferer complains, a wisdom-voice responds with a defense of the gods' inscrutability. Zophar's "Can you find out the deep things of God?" (v. 7) has a close cognate in the Babylonian Theodicy's Stanza XXIV: "The divine mind, like the center of the heavens, is remote; knowledge of it is difficult; the masses do not know it." The Sumerian "Man and His God" lament, older than both, shares the retribution framework Zophar assumes. The Ugaritic divine council texts from Ras Shamra (fourteenth-thirteenth c. BCE) describe an Ēl who convenes the *phr bn ilm* ("assembly of divine sons"), a structure present in Job's own prologue but not directly invoked in chapter 11.

Textual witnesses matter. The Old Greek (LXX) of Job runs about 400 lines shorter than MT, and the shortened passages often lie precisely in the friends' speeches — including chapter 11. The 11QTargum Job from Qumran (11Q10) is fragmentary at chapter 11 but where preserved tends to literal translation with mild softenings. The Peshitta's handling of *ta'alumot* (v. 6) shifts toward "hidden things" in a more mystical register than MT's "secrets."

Five Hebrew terms carry the chapter:

- *ta'alumot* תַּעֲלֻמוֹת — "hidden things," "secrets" (v. 6): the plural noun from the root *'alam* (to conceal); used elsewhere only Ps 44:21 and Job 28:11.
- *hoqer* / *heqer* חֵקֶר — "limit," "searching out" (v. 7): the unsearchable depth that defines divine otherness.
- *tiqvah* תִּקְוָה — "hope" (v. 18, 20): the same word Job curses in chapter 7 and that the wicked lose in v. 20.
- *tam* תָּם — "blameless" (implicit in v. 4's *bar*): Zophar implicitly denies Job the designation the prologue grants him.
- *hokmah* חָכְמָה — "wisdom" (v. 6): the "manifold understanding" Zophar claims belongs to God alone.

## Lens 2 — The Literature

Zophar's speech is the shortest and harshest of the friends' first-cycle speeches. It breaks structurally into four movements: rebuke of Job's speech (vv. 1–6), assertion of divine inscrutability (vv. 7–12), call to repentance (vv. 13–14), and promise of restoration (vv. 15–20). The symmetry is striking — a chiasm centered on the divine inscrutability passage. Habel (Old Testament Library) identifies the structure as A-B-C-B'-A', where the exterior frames are Job's speech (bad) vs. the wicked's fate (bad), and the interior frames are the sinner's condition (guilty) vs. the repentant's hope (restored), with the inscrutability passage as the theological center.

Hebrew parallelism does the heaviest lifting. Verses 7–9 deploy a tightly constructed fourfold cosmography — higher than heaven, deeper than Sheol, longer than earth, broader than sea — that Alter (*The Wisdom Books*) reads as a deliberate evocation of Mesopotamian cosmic geography. The four axes define the totality of created space, and God's *heqer* exceeds all four. Verse 8's *mah tif'al* ("what can you do?") and *mah teda* ("what can you know?") is a classic rhetorical doublet that subordinates human action and knowledge to divine vastness.

The register is forensic and pedagogical. Verse 10's "summons the court" uses the verb *yaqhil* from the root *qahal* (to assemble): if God passes through and arrests a human, who can recall the assembly he convenes? The legal language that has been Job's (see chs. 9–10) is flipped against him. Zophar deploys Job's own register against him.

The chapter's most notorious line is verse 12: "But a stupid man will get understanding when a wild donkey's colt is born a man!" The Hebrew is nearly impossible (*'ish navuv yillaveb, we'ayir pere 'adam yivvaled*), and translations vary widely. Pope reads it as a proverbial impossibility: the empty-headed man will become wise when a wild donkey's colt is born human. Clines notes the play between *'ish* (man) and *'ayir* (donkey) and the verb *yillaveb* (he gets a heart/mind), which Zophar deploys as a cutting insult aimed at Job. The insult is the sharpest in the friends' speeches and signals Zophar's character as the harshest friend.

Good (*In Turns of Tempest*) and Newsom (NIB, and *The Book of Job: A Contest of Moral Imaginations*) both emphasize the *irony* of Zophar's speech: the promises of verses 13–19 are ultimately what the Epilogue will grant Job, yet Zophar's path to them — repentance from a sin Job has not committed — is exactly the path the Epilogue rejects. The form is right; the content is wrong. Zophar's theology is orthodox on every detail and wrong about Job in the whole.

Intertextually, verse 7's "Can you find out the deep things of God?" has clear echoes with Deut 29:29, Isa 40:28, Ps 145:3, and most closely Job 28 (the wisdom poem), where the same question is answered differently. Verse 10's language of God "passing through" (*yahalof*) connects to Isa 21:1 and Job 9:11 (where Job has already used the same verb against God). Verse 20's "eyes of the wicked will fail" echoes Deut 28:32 and Lam 4:17 in covenant-curse language.

Textually, LXX shortens verses 6 and 12 significantly. The Targum fills in verse 6's *kipalayim* ("double") with a didactic gloss. MT verse 12's wild-donkey line in 4QpaleoJob appears to share MT consonantal text, with no apparent textual amelioration despite the line's difficulty.

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

Within the canonical wisdom tradition — Proverbs, Job, Ecclesiastes, Song of Songs, and the deuterocanonical Sirach and Wisdom of Solomon — chapter 11 represents the orthodoxy that Proverbs asserts: the righteous prosper, the wicked perish, wisdom is access to divine order (cf. Prov 3, 8). Job's protest stands against this orthodoxy; Zophar speaks for it. Von Rad in *Wisdom in Israel* locates the whole dialogue inside the collapse of the Proverbs-style confidence that the world's moral architecture is legible.

Jewish reception has been uneasy with Zophar throughout. The *Testament of Job* (first c. BCE–first c. CE) significantly reduces the friends' role. The Qumran 11QTargum Job preserves chapter 11 but shortens. Bava Batra 15a–16b's rabbinic discussion of Job does not single out Zophar for praise; the later midrashic tradition (*Midrash Iyov*) treats Zophar as the one whose rhetoric is hardest to love. Rashi on verse 6's *kipalayim* offers an arithmetic reading: God is taking only half of what Job's sin deserves. Ibn Ezra focuses on the syntax of verse 12, proposing a reading in which *'ish navuv* is the hollow-headed skeptic whose wisdom will come only by miracle. Maimonides, in Guide III.23, reads Zophar as representing one of the inadequate theological positions the book refutes: Zophar holds that God's justice is hidden because our ignorance is total, which Maimonides ultimately rejects as a form of theological abdication. Nachmanides reads Zophar more sympathetically as voicing the truth about divine inscrutability while applying it wrongly to Job.

Modern Jewish readings push further. Buber and Levenson both read Zophar as an example of orthodox theology unable to accommodate genuine suffering. Zornberg's midrashic-psychological reading finds in Zophar the voice that wants Job's complaint to stop; the problem is not that Zophar is wrong about God but that he is afraid of the question Job is asking. Kugel treats chapter 11 as the clearest example in the book of a theology with nothing left to say once the facts change. Sarna emphasizes that verse 6's "God exacts of you less than your guilt deserves" is the cruelest line any friend speaks.

Christian reception follows the Jewish pattern of unease. Origen (surviving fragments) treats Zophar as representing the *carnal* understanding that assumes sin must underlie suffering. Chrysostom uses Zophar as a foil for the patient Job tradition — Job's patience shines precisely against this kind of friend. Gregory the Great's *Moralia* (books IX–X) gives Zophar sustained treatment, allegorizing his speech as the voice of the half-converted soul — orthodox in doctrine but hard of heart. Aquinas's *Expositio super Iob ad litteram* reads Zophar more charitably, as speaking true things (God's inscrutability, the call to repentance) applied incorrectly. Calvin's sermons on Job (sermons 40–44 cover chapter 11) find in Zophar a preacher whose theology is right and whose application is wrong — a warning Calvin explicitly applies to his own homiletical tradition. Luther's occasional comments treat Zophar as the voice of the *theologia gloriae* — the theology of glory that cannot see the cross.

Wellhausen and Gunkel emphasized the formulaic character of Zophar's speech, reading it as crystallized wisdom convention. Von Rad placed the speech inside the "crisis of wisdom" where traditional answers are being tested. Clines's WBC treats verses 13–20 as an almost formally liturgical summons to repentance, complete with cultic overtones (*tiqon libbekha*, "prepare/direct your heart"). Newsom reads Zophar as a compressed example of the friend-discourse the book as a whole rejects. Gutiérrez's *On Job* treats chapter 11 as the paradigmatic failure of theology that has no room for the innocent sufferer. Davis and Ticciati both emphasize the irony: Zophar promises Job the very restoration the epilogue grants him — but only on condition of an admission of sin that would have made Job into a different man. Habel and Hartley (NICOT) offer the most sympathetic readings, treating Zophar as a genuine theologian whose framework has simply run out of explanatory power. Andersen notes that Zophar's verse 7 question ("Can you find out the deep things of God?") will be answered in the whirlwind — not as Zophar means it, but not as Job hopes either.

Christian and Jewish readings converge in treating Zophar as the voice of inadequate orthodoxy. They diverge on the implications: the Christian trajectory often reads this as pointing forward to a resurrection-grounded theodicy that exceeds both Zophar and Job; the dominant Jewish trajectory keeps the resolution terrestrial, grounded in Job's vindication in this life (42:7–17) without recourse to a posthumous judgment.

## Lens 4 — The Theological Intent

**Tension 1: Is God's inscrutability a consolation or a silencing?**

Zophar's central affirmation in verses 7–9 — that God's *heqer* (limit) is higher than heaven, deeper than Sheol, longer than earth, broader than sea — has been read across traditions as both a profound truth and a rhetorical weapon.

The Catholic tradition, via Aquinas (*Summa Theologiae* I.12 on the vision of God; *Expositio super Iob* on chapter 11), treats divine inscrutability as the ground of theology's analogical humility. We know God, but we do not comprehend God. Zophar states a true proposition — but applies it to silence Job rather than to humble himself. Aquinas sides with the proposition and against Zophar's use of it. The CCC (206, 230–231) takes similar care: God's incomprehensibility is the condition of prayer, not the refutation of protest.

Reformed Protestantism has been drawn to Zophar's inscrutability claim but has also been its sharpest critic. Calvin in sermons 42–43 reads the inscrutability correctly but excoriates Zophar for wielding it against Job. Calvin's own doctrine of *Deus revelatus / Deus absconditus* (though more associated with Luther) and his stress on the *theater of glory* keep inscrutability from becoming a silencer. Lutheran theology, especially in Luther's own *theologia crucis*, treats Zophar as the archetypal theologian of glory — the one whose God is too high to meet the sufferer at the cross. Wesleyan readings, with their stronger accent on divine accessibility through grace, find Zophar's *heqer* language dangerously distant. Orthodox theology, from Chrysostom through Gregory of Nyssa's *epektasis* to Maximus, treats divine inscrutability as inexhaustibly participatory — one is drawn further in, not kept out.

Jewish readings diverge. Maimonides (Guide III.23) sees inscrutability rightly understood as the philosophical truth the whirlwind discloses, but Zophar's version is merely piety's mask over ignorance. Rashi takes verse 7 as genuine wisdom misapplied. Levenson's *Creation and the Persistence of Evil* presses further: inscrutability language has often been used to paper over the real theological problem — that in the biblical worldview God's mastery over chaos is contested, not total. Zophar's vast cosmography assumes a divine control that the chaoskampf tradition Levenson traces does not fully support. Zornberg's reading treats inscrutability as the category that forecloses conversation; the function of the language in Zophar's mouth is silencing. The steelman: Zophar is not wrong that God exceeds human knowing, and a theology without inscrutability is a theology without mystery. But inscrutability deployed as argument against the sufferer becomes an instrument of cruelty.

Critical scholarship (Newsom, Clines) sides with the silencing reading: the chapter's structural irony — Zophar speaks truth that the book ultimately endorses while applying it in a way the book rejects — is the point. Inscrutability is a theological truth and a rhetorical weapon; distinguishing them is the book's labor.

Where this leaves us: God's inscrutability is both the ground of legitimate theological humility and a tool that can be wielded to silence questions. The book of Job rejects Zophar's deployment without rejecting the doctrine.

**Tension 2: Can suffering be read as evidence of sin?**

Zophar assumes it can. Verse 6's "God exacts of you less than your guilt deserves" is the sharpest formulation of retributive theology in the friends' speeches — Job is not only guilty but Job has gotten off lightly. The restoration promises of verses 13–19 are conditional on repentance from a guilt Zophar has inferred backward from Job's suffering.

The Catholic tradition has generally rejected the inference. Aquinas in *ST* II-II.67–71 on justice and II-II.121 on piety distinguishes the reality of sin from its detectability by outside observers. Suffering is not a reliable sign of sin (he cites Job). The CCC (1502–1505) reinforces this: suffering has meaning, but not as evidence of personal guilt. Aquinas's *Expositio* on chapter 11 makes the point directly against Zophar.

Reformed Protestantism is more divided. Calvin in his Job sermons rejects Zophar's inference about Job specifically while maintaining that suffering is pedagogically linked to the fallen human condition generally. Calvin's double-effect reading — suffering is *never* proof of personal guilt in a given case, but is always *evidence* of the human condition's brokenness — walks a line later Reformed theology (Piper, and parts of the Westminster tradition) has pushed in both directions. Lutheran theology, via the theology of the cross, treats suffering and righteousness as compatible (Christ's passion being the paradigm), and Zophar's inference is explicitly rejected. Wesleyan traditions, with the doctrine of prevenient grace and sanctification, rarely endorse Zophar's move.

Orthodox readings, especially those emphasizing *ascesis*, can occasionally sound Zopharian — suffering is medicinal, and one's response (repentance, endurance) matters. But the Orthodox emphasis on the innocent sufferer as icon of Christ precludes reading suffering as evidence of sin in the individual case.

Jewish readings vary. Rabbinic tradition in general rejects Zophar's inference; the rabbinic maxim *ein mita bil'o het* ("there is no death without sin") has historically been softened by counter-traditions emphasizing *yissurin shel ahavah* ("afflictions of love") for the righteous. Maimonides in Guide III.17 rejects Zophar's view as one of five inadequate theodicies. Levenson, steelmanned: the biblical tradition does generally expect righteousness and suffering to be inversely correlated, and the book of Job is *precisely* the book that registers this expectation's failure. Zophar is not an outlier; he is the tradition speaking, and the book is his undoing. The radical move is to preserve Zophar's voice at all rather than simply silencing it — the book refuses to erase the position it rejects.

Critical scholarship (Newsom, Clines, Habel) largely sides with this: Zophar is not a caricature but a faithful representative of a widespread theological position, and the book's verdict in 42:7 ("you have not spoken of me what is right") is a genuine rejection of that position, not a mere narrative flourish.

Where this leaves us: the inference from suffering to sin has been resisted across traditions in principle, but has returned in practice again and again. Zophar remains the honest face of a theology most believers still partly hold.

**Closing unresolved question:** If Zophar's promises in verses 15–19 are exactly what the epilogue delivers to Job — security, hope, rest — then is the book's verdict against him about his theology, or only about his timing?

## The Thread

- **Deuteronomy 29:29** — "The secret things belong to the LORD our God, but the things revealed belong to us and to our children." Zophar's *ta'alumot* and *heqer* language in vv. 6–7 share this register, but Deuteronomy leaves room for revealed knowledge while Zophar collapses everything into the hidden. The friend knows the doctrine; he has forgotten its second half.

- **Isaiah 55:8–9** — "My thoughts are not your thoughts... as the heavens are higher than the earth, so are my ways higher than your ways." Isaiah's cosmic-vertical language is Zophar's, but in Isaiah's mouth it is consolation (the invitation to the thirsty); in Zophar's mouth it is accusation. Same metaphor, opposite pastoral register.

- **1 Corinthians 2:10–11** — "The Spirit searches everything, even the depths of God" (*ta bathē tou theou*). Paul uses *bathē* where LXX Job 11:7 uses *eschaton*. Paul's answer to Zophar's question is Christological and pneumatological: the depths are not found by searching but disclosed by the Spirit. The Christian canonical trajectory runs counter to Zophar's foreclosure.

- **James 5:11** — "You have heard of the patience of Job" (*tēn hypomonēn Iōb*). James reads Job as the model of endurance, but does not mention the friends. The New Testament's one explicit Job reference preserves Job's voice and leaves Zophar unmentioned — a subtle canonical verdict.
