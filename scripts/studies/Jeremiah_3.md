# Jeremiah 3: The Torah Violated for the Sake of Return

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

Chapter 3 is composite. Verses 6–11, the prose interlude on the "two sisters" (Israel and Judah), open with an explicit dating: "the LORD said to me in the days of King Josiah" (3:6). That places at least this section in the period 627–609 BC, after the discovery of the law book in 622 and during the years of the Josianic reform. The poetic frame around it (3:1–5 and 3:12–25) is harder to date but probably belongs to the same general window — Josiah's later reign, when the reform was visible but incomplete and the northern population (the "house of Israel" deported by the Assyrians more than a century earlier in 722 BC) remained scattered across the former Northern Kingdom and the Assyrian Empire.

The geography of the chapter matters. The "north" of 3:12 ("proclaim these words toward the north") is not yet the Babylonian invasion threat of chapter 1's boiling pot but the territory of the former Northern Kingdom — Samaria, the hill country of Ephraim, and beyond. Josiah's reform was geographically expansionist: 2 Kgs 23:15–20 reports that Josiah extended his cultic purge into Bethel and "all the cities of Samaria," and 2 Chr 34:6–7 claims he reached "as far as Naphtali." The Assyrian Empire was crumbling — Asshur fell in 614, Nineveh in 612, and Haran in 610 — and Josiah was attempting, briefly and with limited success, to reunify the old Davidic territory. Jeremiah's call to "faithless Israel" to return from "the north" (3:12, 14, 18) sits inside this political moment: the prophet is urging the northern population to participate in a renewed covenant centered on Zion.

The sexual and ritual polemic of 3:2 ("by the waysides you have sat awaiting lovers like an Arab in the wilderness") and 3:6, 13 ("under every green tree") refers to the cult on the high places — *bamot* — that Josiah's reform attempted to abolish. Archaeologically, Israelite cultic platforms have been excavated at Tel Dan (the largest preserved bamah, in continuous use from Iron I through the Hellenistic period), Tel Arad (the Judean sanctuary in the desert fortress, with its standing stone and incense altars), and Hazor. The "high hill and green tree" formula echoes Deut 12:2 verbatim and is the Deuteronomistic shorthand for the syncretistic cult Jeremiah's audience knew firsthand.

The legal premise of 3:1 — "if a man divorces his wife and she goes from him and becomes another man's wife, will he return to her?" — directly invokes Deut 24:1–4, the law of remarriage. Deut 24:1–4 prohibits a divorced wife from returning to her first husband if she has married another man in the interim; the prohibition is framed as preventing the land from being "polluted" (*tahatī*). Jeremiah explicitly cites the legal language: "would not that land be greatly polluted?" (3:1). The startling rhetorical move is that, after invoking the law, the prophet has YHWH call Israel to return *anyway* — "return, faithless Israel" (3:12). This is a public-square legal-theological provocation that no contemporary listener would have missed.

Bullae from the late seventh century — the Baruch ben Neriah seal, the Gemariah ben Shaphan seal — confirm the bureaucratic environment in which Jeremiah was preaching. The Lachish Ostraca, written a generation later during the final Babylonian siege, attest to the chaos that the chapter's "weeping on the bare heights" (3:21) prefigures. The Babylonian Chronicle (BM 21946) anchors the dates of the Assyrian collapse and the Egyptian-Babylonian conflict that frames Josiah's window of expansionist policy.

The textual situation is mostly stable in chapter 3, with minor MT/LXX variants in the prose section but no meaning-altering divergence. The standard commentaries (Lundbom Anchor, Holladay Hermeneia, Brueggemann, Carroll OTL, McKane ICC, Bright Anchor, Stulman, Thompson NICOT, Kathleen O'Connor's *Jeremiah: Pain and Promise*) all give the chapter sustained attention, especially for its juxtaposition of the divorce-law citation with the call to return.

**Key terms.** *Shuv* (שׁוּב, "return, repent") — the chapter's organizing leitwort, appearing fourteen times in chapters 3–4, the densest concentration of *shuv* in the Old Testament. *Meshuvah* (מְשׁוּבָה, "faithlessness, apostasy") — a noun derived from the same root, used as a play on the verb: "return, *meshuvah Israel*" (3:12, 14, 22). *Bagadah* (בָּגֵדָה, "treacherous one") — paired with *meshuvah* as the adjective for Judah. *Aron berit YHWH* (אֲרוֹן בְּרִית־יְהוָה, "the ark of the covenant of the LORD," 3:16). *Kisse YHWH* (כִּסֵּא יְהוָה, "the throne of the LORD," 3:17). *Bamot* (בָּמוֹת, "high places," implicit throughout 3:2, 6, 23). *Boshet* (בֹּשֶׁת, "the shameful thing," 3:24) — a euphemism for Baal in some contexts.

## Lens 2 — The Literature

The chapter alternates poetry and prose in a pattern characteristic of the Mowinckel C-source debate. Verses 1–5 are poetry; 6–11 are prose narrative ("the LORD said to me in the days of King Josiah…"); 12–14a are poetry; 14b–18 are prose oracle; 19–20 are poetry; 21–25 are poetry with a turn to dialogue. Holladay treats the alternation as evidence of editorial composition combining authentic Jeremianic poetry with Deuteronomistic prose homilies. Carroll's redaction-critical reading is more aggressive: he treats verses 6–11 and 14–18 as substantially Deuteronomistic, with the genuine Jeremiah confined to the poetic sections. Lundbom's reading is more conservative, treating the prose sections as Jeremiah's own preaching transposed by Baruch.

The leitwort *shuv* is the chapter's organizing device. The verb appears fourteen times in chapters 3–4, and chapter 3 itself contains nine occurrences across the two senses — *return* (geographically, relationally) and *repent* (morally). The Hebrew refuses to disambiguate: *shuv* is both physical and theological at once, and the refrain "return, faithless Israel" (*shuvah meshuvah Yisrael*, 3:12) plays the noun against the verb in a compressed wordplay. The chapter is, in effect, a single sustained call to *shuv* delivered through multiple genres.

Several rhetorical moves stand out. The *legal-then-overruled* structure of 3:1: the law of Deut 24:1–4 is cited explicitly, and then the call to return is issued *as if the law did not apply*. The two readings most often given are (a) that YHWH transcends the legal categories — what is forbidden under Torah-law is permitted under prophetic-covenantal grace — and (b) that the law itself, properly read, makes provision for divine prerogative that human husbands lack. The text gives no explicit resolution.

The *two-sisters* allegory of 3:6–11 anticipates Ezekiel 23 (Oholah and Oholibah, the elder sister Samaria and the younger sister Jerusalem). The literary innovation is the comparative move: "faithless Israel has shown herself more righteous than treacherous Judah" (3:11). The northern kingdom, which had no Davidic dynasty and no Jerusalem temple — which from a southern perspective was the apostate kingdom from its founding under Jeroboam — is judged *less guilty* than the southern kingdom that has the temple, the law, and the prophetic warnings of Hosea, Amos, and Micah. Holladay reads this as Jeremiah's theological barb against southern complacency: privilege increases culpability.

The *new things* oracle of 3:15–18 is one of the chapter's most striking literary moments. The promise that "they shall no more say, 'The ark of the covenant of the LORD.' It shall not come to mind or be remembered or missed; it shall not be made again" (3:16) is theologically extraordinary. The ark, the most sacred cultic object in Israelite religion, the throne-pedestal of YHWH, will simply be forgotten — and not rebuilt. Jerusalem itself will become "the throne of the LORD" (3:17), absorbing the ark's function. This is one of the strongest pre-exilic indicators of theological imagination capable of envisioning the cult without its central object — a vision that becomes practical necessity after 587 BC, when the ark disappears from the historical record.

The closing lament of 3:21–25 introduces a dialogical voice: the people respond. "Behold, we come to you, for you are the LORD our God" (3:22). The chapter ends not with judgment but with the audience's confession, "we have sinned against the LORD our God, we and our fathers, from our youth even to this day." This is unusual for Jeremiah, whose oracles typically end with denunciation; the dialogic closure prefigures the lament theology of chapter 14 and the longer movements of chapters 30–33.

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

The chapter's theological position — covenant violation, divine grief, call to return, promise of restoration — encapsulates the canonical pattern of the prophetic literature. It draws on Hosea (Hosea 2:14–23 is the proximate ancestor of "return, faithless Israel"), incorporates Deuteronomistic legal reasoning (Deut 24:1–4), and projects forward into the new-covenant theology of Jeremiah 31. The *forgotten ark* of 3:16 anticipates the post-exilic period when the second temple lacked the ark (Tacitus's *Histories* 5.9 reports that Pompey, entering the temple in 63 BC, found the holy of holies empty), and the universalizing vision of "all nations shall gather to [Jerusalem]" (3:17) anticipates Isaiah 2:2–4 and Micah 4:1–4 and projects toward Revelation's New Jerusalem.

**Jewish trajectory.** The legal provocation of 3:1 generated extensive rabbinic discussion. Sifre Deuteronomy treats the apparent contradiction with Deut 24:1–4 by distinguishing the legal regime from the *ba'al teshuvah* regime — the law of remarriage applies in normal cases, but God's invitation to *teshuvah* (return) operates in a category that transcends the legal. Bavli Yoma 86b uses Jer 3:14 ("return, O faithless children") as a foundational text on the power of repentance: "great is repentance, for it brings the redemption nearer." Rashi on 3:1 takes the rabbinic move: the law applies to humans, not to God. Radak develops the comparative reading of 3:11 (Israel "more righteous than" Judah) as a barb against southern self-righteousness. The medieval Jewish liturgical tradition for the High Holy Days — *Aseret Yemei Teshuvah*, the Ten Days of Repentance — leans heavily on the *shuv* refrain of Jeremiah 3–4 and Hosea 14. Modern Jewish scholarship, particularly Abraham Joshua Heschel (*The Prophets*) and Jon Levenson, has continued to engage the chapter's legal-theological provocation.

**Christian trajectory.** Origen's *Homilies on Jeremiah* allegorizes the chapter through the typology of the soul's return to Christ, with the high places as the soul's distractions and the call to return as the gospel summons. Jerome's *Commentary on Jeremiah* gives the chapter careful textual attention and treats the two-sisters allegory as a forerunner of the church-and-synagogue typology — a reading that became standard in medieval Christian exegesis but which contemporary scholars (J. Andrew Dearman, R. Kendall Soulen) have generally retired. Calvin's *Lectures on Jeremiah* gives chapter 3 substantial attention, especially 3:1's legal provocation; Calvin reads it as exhibiting the asymmetry between divine grace and human law without violating the latter. The critical tradition — Duhm, Mowinckel, Skinner, Holladay, Carroll, McKane — has focused on the editorial layering and on the question of how much of 6–18 is Jeremianic versus Deuteronomistic.

**Where the traditions diverge.** Jewish tradition reads chapter 3 as the canonical text of *teshuvah* — the doctrine that repentance is always available, that God is always ready to receive the returning sinner, that the *shuv* refrain is the heart of the divine-human relationship. Christian tradition has often read it Christologically (the call to return is fulfilled in the gospel summons) and ecclesiologically (the new ark of 3:16 is the church or the Eucharist). Critical tradition reads it as evidence of Deuteronomistic editorial expansion of authentic Jeremianic preaching. Feminist criticism (Trible, Brenner, Weems, Mary Shields's *Circumscribing the Prostitute*, 2004) has continued to scrutinize the marriage metaphor and especially the "playing the whore" language as theologically loaded with patriarchal violence.

## Lens 4 — The Theological Intent

**Tension 1: The legal violation in 3:1 — does YHWH transgress his own Torah?** The chapter opens by citing Deut 24:1–4 — the law forbidding a divorced and remarried wife from returning to her first husband — and then has YHWH call Israel to return anyway. The theological scandal is real, and the major traditions handle it differently. Walter Brueggemann reads the move as "the gospel embedded in the Torah" — the prophetic word announcing a divine grace that transcends the legal categories Torah itself establishes, without thereby invalidating the law. The Reformed tradition, following Calvin, has tended to read it as evidence of the asymmetry between the dispensations: the law is for humans regulating relations with each other; God's prerogative as covenant partner is not bound by human marital law. The Catholic tradition, particularly in the natural-law reading of Aquinas, treats the move as showing that divine positive law (Torah) is subordinate to divine eternal law (the unchanging will of God), and that what appears to be a violation is in fact a higher application.

The Jewish tradition's response is the most subtle. Heschel's *The Prophets* argues that Jer 3:1's move is not a transgression but an apocalyptic transcendence — God is *not* a human husband, the metaphor breaks down at the point of the legal application, and the prophet exploits the breakdown to reveal divine grace. Levenson develops a related reading: the law of Deut 24:1–4 is the *human* law of remarriage, but Israel's relationship to YHWH is sui generis, and the divine call to return is not constrained by the legal precedent. The Bavli's reading, articulated through the *teshuvah* tradition, treats the verse as the foundational text for the doctrine that repentance can repair what the law cannot remit.

The steelman of the most uncomfortable reading — that YHWH simply violates Torah — is given by some redaction-critical scholars (Carroll, McKane): they argue that Jer 3:1 was written before the legal codification of Deut 24:1–4 was firmly in place, and that the contradiction is the result of Deuteronomistic editors retrojecting the legal language into Jeremiah's earlier oracle. On this reading the contradiction is editorial, not theological. The text's final form, however, holds the tension without resolution. Where this leaves us: Jeremiah 3:1 is one of the strongest Old Testament loci for the theological priority of grace over law, and the way one reads it tracks one's broader theology of how divine command and divine love relate.

**Tension 2: The marriage metaphor's gendered violence and theological indispensability.** The chapter's polemic deploys the full force of the marriage-metaphor's vehicle: the unfaithful wife, the public shaming ("by the waysides you have sat awaiting lovers," 3:2), the polluted land, the "forehead of a whore" (3:3). Feminist critique — Phyllis Trible, Athalya Brenner, Renita Weems (*Battered Love*), Mary Shields — has argued that this imagery normalizes patterns of male violence against women and that the metaphor's theological message cannot be cleanly separated from its gendered vehicle. The metaphor *gendered* idolatry as female disorder, and the gendering has had real consequences in Christian theological history.

The counter-position, articulated by Brevard Childs in his canonical readings and by Levenson in the Jewish tradition, is that the marriage metaphor is theologically indispensable: it captures the *relational* dimension of covenant violation that no abstract category can match. Idolatry as a category mistake — the Aristotelian or scholastic reading — fails to capture what is happening when a covenant partner turns to another god. The marriage metaphor captures it precisely because it is shocking. The Lutheran *theologia crucis* turns this into a hermeneutical principle: the metaphor must be allowed to wound the reader, including the male reader who would prefer to identify with the betrayed husband rather than the unfaithful wife.

The Catholic tradition's reading, particularly in John Paul II's theology of the body and in the broader sacramental marriage theology, has tried to recover the metaphor by emphasizing its *mutuality*: the marriage of YHWH and Israel is not the patriarchal dominance metaphor of the surrounding ancient Near East but a covenant of mutual self-gift. Whether this recovery succeeds, or whether it requires too much from the textual data, is the contested question. The Orthodox tradition, with its emphasis on Mary as Theotokos and its long tradition of bridal mysticism, has generally preserved the metaphor with less anxiety than Western Christianity. Where this leaves us: the metaphor cannot be retired without losing something theologically central, but it cannot be deployed naively without participating in the imaginative violence the feminist tradition has rightly named.

**One unresolved question.** If the ark of the covenant will be "no more remembered or missed" (3:16) — if the most sacred cultic object will simply be forgotten — what does this say about the durability of any other religious institution, image, or practice? Is the chapter's most radical claim that *every* mediating object eventually becomes redundant when the relationship it mediates becomes direct?

## The Thread

The Deuteronomic background is unusually explicit: 3:1 cites Deut 24:1–4, and 3:6 ("on every high hill and under every green tree") echoes Deut 12:2. The legal-theological provocation of 3:1 anticipates Pauline arguments in Rom 7 and Gal 3:19–25 about the relationship of law and grace, and Hebrews 8's reading of Jer 31 as the new covenant that supersedes the legal regime. The "no more ark" oracle of 3:16 finds its strangest historical fulfillment in the second temple period — Tacitus reports the holy of holies as empty — and its eschatological fulfillment in Rev 11:19 (where "the ark of his covenant was seen within his temple" only as part of the heavenly vision) and Rev 21:22 (the New Jerusalem has no temple, "for its temple is the Lord God the Almighty and the Lamb"). And the *shuv* refrain of 3:12, 14, 22 finds its narrative counterpart in Lk 15:11–32, the parable of the prodigal son — the son who *returns* and is received by the father whose grief over his absence has been the chapter's silent center.
