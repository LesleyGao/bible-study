# Psalm 31 — Go Deeper

## Lens 1: The Historical World

The superscription reads simply, "To the choirmaster. A Psalm of David" (*lamnatzeach mizmor le-David*, לַמְנַצֵּחַ מִזְמוֹר לְדָוִד). The psalm carries no narrative anchor like Psalm 51's Bathsheba episode or Psalm 34's Achish escape — the redactor leaves the situation generic, which is part of the reason the prayer travels so well across centuries. Its placement in Book I (Pss 1–41), which is overwhelmingly Davidic, points the early Israelite reader toward a king-figure under siege, but the language is sufficiently formal that it functions as a temple-liturgy template into which any sufferer can step.

Form-critically, Hermann Gunkel classified Psalm 31 as a *Klagelied des Einzelnen* — an **individual lament** — but with the unusual feature that the lament moves through several full cycles of complaint and confidence rather than the typical single arc. Gunkel and Joachim Begrich (*Einleitung in die Psalmen*, 1933) noted that Pss 31, 71, and parts of 35 share a "double lament" structure that may indicate either two originally separate poems stitched together (the older critical position) or a single liturgical performance in which the worshiper returns to lament after a moment of trust before finally breaking through to thanksgiving (Hossfeld and Zenger's preferred reading). The closing verses (vv. 23–24) shift to a hymnic exhortation directed at the congregation — the lament has become a teaching psalm.

The vocabulary cluster around refuge is dense and architectural. Within the first three verses we meet *chasah* (חָסָה, "take refuge," v. 1), *tsur* (צוּר, "rock," v. 2), *metzudah* (מְצוּדָה, "fortress," vv. 2, 3), and *sela* (סֶלַע, "crag/rock," v. 3). The Iron Age reader knew these as literal: David's refuge at *Metzudat Tzion* (the stronghold of Zion, 2 Sam 5:7) and at the strongholds of Engedi and Masada used precisely this vocabulary. The poet stacks the architecture of Judean topography on top of the abstract noun *machseh* (מַחֲסֶה, "refuge"), making God a fortified position one can flee into.

The most famous Hebrew phrase in the psalm sits at v. 5: *beyadkha afkid ruchi* (בְּיָדְךָ אַפְקִיד רוּחִי, "into your hand I commit my spirit"). The verb *paqad* in the *hiphil* means to **deposit for safekeeping** — it is a banker's word, a treasurer's word, a temple-treasury word. The speaker is not surrendering to death but entrusting his life-breath to the only safe deposit box in the universe. The Septuagint renders it *eis cheiras sou parathēsomai to pneuma mou*, which is the form Luke 23:46 quotes (with *paratithemai* in the present middle).

Psalm 31 is well attested in the Dead Sea Scrolls — fragments appear in 4QPs-a (4Q83) and 11QPs-d among others. The text is essentially what the Masoretes preserved a thousand years later. The LXX is largely faithful, though it numbers the psalm 30 (Greek/Latin numbering runs one behind the Hebrew from Ps 10 through Ps 147). One textual moment matters theologically: in v. 6, the LXX and a few Hebrew manuscripts read "you hate" rather than the MT's "I hate," which softens the speaker's animus toward idolators by transferring it to God.

ANE parallels are easy to find for the refuge imagery — Mesopotamian *šuilla* prayers ("lifted-hand" prayers) and Hittite personal lament texts use similar language of fleeing to a deity for protection. What is distinctive in Psalm 31 is the personalism: the speaker addresses YHWH directly, names him "faithful God" (*el emet*, אֵל אֱמֶת, v. 5), and treats the relationship as covenantal rather than merely transactional.

## Lens 2: The Literature

Lowth's parallelism is everywhere. Synonymous parallelism in v. 1 ("In you, O LORD, do I take refuge // let me never be put to shame"); synthetic in v. 3 ("For you are my rock and my fortress // and for your name's sake you lead me and guide me"); antithetic in v. 6 ("I hate those who pay regard to worthless idols // but I trust in the LORD"). The poem moves with the steady iambic rhythm typical of Hebrew lament verse — the *qinah* meter (3+2 stress) is loosened here in favor of the more balanced 3+3 of public liturgy.

The strophic structure is where commentators divide. A workable mapping:

- **Strophe 1 (vv. 1–5):** Opening petition and confession of trust, climaxing in *beyadkha afkid ruchi*.
- **Strophe 2 (vv. 6–8):** Confession of loyalty and recollection of past deliverance.
- **Strophe 3 (vv. 9–13):** The lament proper — body wasting, social isolation, plots on his life.
- **Strophe 4 (vv. 14–18):** Renewed petition with imprecation against enemies.
- **Strophe 5 (vv. 19–22):** Hymnic praise for goodness already received.
- **Strophe 6 (vv. 23–24):** Communal exhortation.

The double movement (lament–trust // lament–trust // praise // exhortation) is what Westermann called the "movement from plea to praise" in its more elaborate form — a worship pattern that refuses to resolve too quickly.

Several literary ornaments deserve notice. The phrase "be put to shame" (*bosh*, בּוֹשׁ) forms an inclusio of sorts — appearing in v. 1 (the petition) and v. 17 (now turned against the wicked). The image of the **broken vessel** (*keli oved*, כְּלִי אֹבֵד, v. 12) is haunting — a discarded clay pot, useless, swept off the table. The **net** in v. 4 is a hunter's snare imagery that recurs across the Psalter (Ps 9:15, 35:7–8). And the closing word, *chazak* ("be strong," v. 24), is the same imperative Joshua receives at his commissioning (Josh 1:6, 7, 9) — a battlefield word redeployed for the spiritual struggle.

There is no acrostic and no *selah* in Psalm 31, which is unusual for a lament of this length. The absence of *selah* may indicate that the psalm was meant to be read or sung straight through without liturgical pause — a single sustained breath of prayer.

## Lens 3: The Larger Story

**In the canonical Psalter.** Psalm 31 sits in the Davidic core of Book I, surrounded by laments and refuge-psalms (Pss 25–28, 31). The catchword *chasah* ("take refuge") links it backward to Ps 25 and forward to Ps 34. Hossfeld and Zenger argue that Pss 25–34 form a deliberate sub-collection of acrostic and quasi-acrostic refuge psalms, with Ps 31 standing as the dramatic peak before the alphabetic resolution of Ps 34.

**The Jewish trajectory.** The Dead Sea Scrolls preserve the psalm without significant variant. *Midrash Tehillim* (the medieval midrashic compilation on the Psalms) reads v. 5 as the prayer Israel offers each evening, entrusting its soul to God in sleep — a reading that became the **bedtime Shema** in later Jewish liturgy. Rashi (11th c.) takes the psalm as fully Davidic, locating it in the Saul persecution narrative; Ibn Ezra (12th c.) is more cautious about historical anchoring; Radak (David Kimhi, 12th–13th c.) reads it Christologically-adjacent (in his polemic against Christian readings, Radak insists v. 5 is about temporal deliverance, not death). Nachmanides connects the *broken vessel* image to Israel in exile.

In modern Jewish thought, Robert Alter's translation captures the architectural force of the refuge cluster ("be a fortress-rock for me / a stronghold-house to rescue me"). Jon Levenson on the Psalter's persistence reads Ps 31 as a paradigmatic prayer of *bittachon* (trust). Avivah Zornberg, in her broader work on *teshuvah*, treats the psalm's double-lament structure as honest about how trust falters and recovers. The psalm is recited in the Ashkenazi rite at *Tachanun* and in selected Sephardi *selichot* (penitential prayers).

**The Christian trajectory.** Augustine in his *Enarrationes in Psalmos* takes v. 5 as **the voice of Christ on the cross**, with the church speaking the rest of the psalm as Christ's body — a totus Christus reading. Athanasius's *Letter to Marcellinus* lists Ps 31 among the psalms to pray "when you are persecuted by your own people." Cassiodorus (6th c.) divides the psalm as a moral itinerary from fear to courage. Aquinas in his Psalms commentary follows Augustine but adds the Christological reading of v. 5 as proof of Christ's voluntary death (he commits, he is not merely killed). Bonaventure, characteristically, mystically extends the *into-your-hand* into a model of the soul's surrender at every moment.

Luther's *Operationes in Psalmos* (1519–1521) — his second great Psalms commentary, contemporary with the Reformation's outbreak — treats Ps 31 as a model of *fiducia* (trusting faith) rather than a Christological prophecy primarily. Calvin's 1557 Psalms commentary reads it as Davidic in the first instance, applicable to the church under persecution, with v. 5 quoted by Christ as the "highest example" of trust but not exhausted by him.

Among modern critical interpreters: Gunkel (lament; possibly composite), Mowinckel (cultic, royal lament), Westermann (lament-to-praise movement), Hans-Joachim Kraus (Davidic personal lament), Frank-Lothar Hossfeld and Erich Zenger (a unified composition with deliberate double-movement structure), John Goldingay (lament with strong refuge motif; warns against over-Christological readings), Marvin Tate, Gordon Wenham (canonical-ethical reading). Walter Brueggemann puts Ps 31 in his "psalms of disorientation" category, with vv. 19–24 modeling reorientation. Patrick D. Miller emphasizes the prayer's bodily realism (vv. 9–10). William P. Brown attends to the architectural metaphor cluster. Beth Tanner reads the psalm pastorally, for sufferers in modern congregations.

**NT use and Christian liturgy.** The psalm's afterlife in the New Testament is dominated by v. 5. Luke 23:46 puts *eis cheiras sou paratithemai to pneuma mou* on the lips of the dying Jesus — the Lukan Jesus dies not with the cry of dereliction (Mark/Matthew) but with this psalm's confession of trust. Acts 7:59 modifies it onto the lips of the dying Stephen: *Kyrie Iēsou, dexai to pneuma mou* ("Lord Jesus, receive my spirit"). The early church absorbed this and made Psalm 31 the **bedtime psalm** of the Western office: *Compline* in the Benedictine and Roman traditions has prayed v. 5 every night for 1500 years. The same verse appears in funeral liturgies — Anglican, Catholic, Orthodox — and in the deathbed *commendatio animae* of medieval Catholic practice.

## Lens 4: Theological Intent — Christ's Last Word and David's First

**Tension 1: The Christological reading vs. the original-Davidic refuge reading.** The Lukan Jesus's quotation of v. 5 has so dominated Christian reception that for many readers the psalm *is* a passion prophecy. But the original poem is plainly a Davidic prayer of an embattled sufferer who has not died — vv. 21–22 celebrate God's hearing his cry, and vv. 23–24 exhort the congregation. The speaker survives.

- **Position A (Christological-dominant):** Augustine, Aquinas, Bonaventure, much medieval Latin tradition, and a strand of Reformed preaching. Christ is the true speaker; David's voice is an anticipation. Steelman: the New Testament's appropriation is canonical, and Luke's framing of Jesus's death with this verse is a Spirit-given hermeneutical key. To read the psalm without that key is to read with one eye closed.
- **Position B (Davidic-original-dominant):** Goldingay, Brueggemann, Hossfeld-Zenger, much modern commentary. The psalm is an Israelite lament about life-in-distress, not death. Jesus's quotation honors the psalm but does not exhaust it. Steelman: forcing every verse through Calvary flattens the psalm's pastoral usefulness for non-dying sufferers — and it was the psalm's *trust-in-life* that gave Jesus the language to face death, not the other way around.
- **Position C (Both/and, canonical):** Athanasius, Calvin, Tate, Wenham. The psalm is Davidic and pastoral in its first sense, Christological in its fulfillment, and ecclesial in its ongoing use. Christ teaches the church to pray Israel's prayer, and Israel's prayer teaches the church what Christ was doing on the cross.

**Tension 2: The imprecation in vv. 17–18 vs. the mercy in vv. 21–24.** The same speaker who asks that the wicked "go silently to Sheol" closes by exhorting the saints to love and praise. Augustine reads the imprecation eschatologically (the wicked's silence is the silence of judgment, not the speaker's vengeance). Lewis in *Reflections on the Psalms* finds it "devilish" until he reads it as honest indignation against systemic evil. Zenger on vengeance psalms argues that the speaker hands rage to God *so that* he need not act on it himself — a transfer of violence out of the human hand and into the divine court. The psalm's structure performs this: imprecation at v. 17, then four verses later (v. 21), the speaker is blessing God for steadfast love.

**Where this leaves us:** The psalm's pastoral genius is that it does not demand that we choose between the Davidic and Christological readings. *Beyadkha afkid ruchi* is simultaneously what David said when his life was threatened, what the synagogue says before sleep, what Jesus said on the cross, what Stephen said as the stones flew, and what the dying Christian whispers into the dark. The verb *paqad* — to deposit for safekeeping — implies the deposit will be returned. Whether the return is tomorrow morning's waking, next week's deliverance from enemies, or resurrection on the last day, the grammar of trust is the same. The psalm teaches that all our entrustings of life — small and great, daily and final — rest on the same divine faithfulness.

**Closing question:** When you say "into your hand I commit my spirit" tonight before sleep, do you mean it as David did (rescue me from enemies), as Jesus did (receive me in death), or as Stephen did (give me the strength to forgive)? The psalm refuses to make you pick.

## The Thread

- Psalm 31:5 is quoted by the dying Jesus in Luke 23:46 and modified by the dying Stephen in Acts 7:59 — making this single Hebrew sentence one of the most consequential lines in the Christian tradition.
- The refuge architecture (rock, fortress, stronghold) recurs across Pss 18, 27, 46, 62, 91 — a sustained theology of God-as-fortified-position that culminates in Heb 6:18, "we who have fled for refuge."
- The "broken vessel" image (v. 12) is picked up in 2 Cor 4:7 — Paul's "treasure in jars of clay" — turning the psalm's lament about being discarded into Paul's argument for the surpassing power of God in human weakness.
- *Be strong, and let your heart take courage* (v. 24) is the same exhortation Joshua receives at his commissioning (Josh 1:6, 7, 9) and that Paul gives the Corinthian church (1 Cor 16:13) — turning lament into a battlefield call to faithful endurance.
