# Exodus 25: That I May Dwell Among Them

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

Exodus 25 opens the tabernacle instructions — seven chapters (Ex 25–31) of astonishing architectural detail, given to Moses on the mountain, describing how Israel is to build a portable sanctuary in which the God of Sinai will travel with them. The chapter introduces the project (vv. 1–9), the ark and its atonement cover (vv. 10–22), the table of showbread (vv. 23–30), and the menorah (vv. 31–40).

The historical world is that of ANE portable sanctuaries. Kenneth Kitchen's *On the Reliability of the Old Testament* catalogues the Late Bronze evidence: Egyptian New Kingdom tent-shrines, the battle-tents of Ramses II at Kadesh, military *tabernacles* in the Amarna correspondence. The specific combination — acacia frame, dyed textile hangings, gold overlay, portability — matches Ramesside Egypt, not any first-millennium shrine.

The Timna excavations in the southern Arabah (Beno Rothenberg, 1960s–80s) uncovered a thirteenth-century Egyptian tent-shrine to Hathor, with post-holes, textile fragments, and a metal-worker's installation; the later Midianite layer included a serpent figurine (cf. Numbers 21). Timna is the closest material analogue to the biblical tabernacle.

Bedouin *qubbah* tent-shrines — red-leather portable shrines carried into battle — are documented ethnographically. Julian Morgenstern's 1945 study *The Ark, the Ephod, and the Tent of Meeting* proposed the tabernacle as preserved *qubbah*; Cross and Friedman extended this line.

The materials catalog of vv. 3–7 describes the trade network of the Late Bronze southern Levant. Acacia (*šiṭṭâ*) grows in the Sinai; blue and purple dye came from the *murex* of the Phoenician coast; scarlet from the *coccus ilicis*; gold and onyx were carried by Israel from Egypt (Ex 12:35–36). Sarna calls the list "a compact map of Israel's economic world."

The ark (*ʾărôn hāʿēdût*, vv. 10–22) is the chapter's center. The atonement cover (*kappōret*) sits on top with two *kĕrubîm* facing each other. Cherubim are attested throughout ANE iconography as composite guardian beings: the Assyrian *lamassu*, the Egyptian sphinx, Syro-Hittite gate-guardians. The thirteenth-century Megiddo ivories show cherub-like figures flanking a throne; the tenth-century Taanach cult stand has cherub guardians. Propp (Anchor Bible *Exodus* vol. 2) and Othmar Keel (*The Symbolism of the Biblical World*) document the parallels.

The table of showbread (vv. 23–30) — *šulḥān lĕḥem pānîm* — echoes Mesopotamian cultic tables on which the gods' "bread" (*akal pānē* in Akkadian) was set. Menahem Haran's *Temples and Temple-Service in Ancient Israel* analyzes the parallel in detail. The showbread is *lĕḥem pānîm* — "bread of the presence" or "bread of the face" — twelve loaves representing the twelve tribes, set out weekly (Lev 24:5–9).

The menorah (vv. 31–40) is a seven-lamp lampstand of hammered gold, with almond-blossom detailing. Archaeological comparanda include eighth-century Iron Age lampstands from Megiddo and Tel Dan (though without the seven-branch form) and Phoenician seven-branched cult objects. After the Second Temple's destruction in 70 CE, the menorah became the Jewish iconographic center — depicted on the Arch of Titus in Rome, on Bar Kokhba coinage, on the Magdala stone, in synagogue mosaics from Hammath Tiberias to Bet Alpha. No Jewish religious object has had a longer or deeper symbolic life.

On authorship, Exodus 25 is P material. Wellhausen argued the tabernacle is Second Temple retrojection; Kaufmann, Haran, Milgrom, and Knohl argued the opposite — that P preserves pre-exilic, perhaps pre-monarchic, tradition. Haran's argument is that retrojection would have included Second Temple elements (specific priestly rotations) that the tabernacle text omits; the absences argue for antiquity. Cassuto defended substantial Mosaic authenticity on linguistic grounds.

Key Hebrew terms:

- *miškān* (מִשְׁכָּן) — tabernacle, dwelling-place; from root *šākan*
- *šākan* (שָׁכַן) — to dwell, tabernacle, pitch tent
- *tĕrûmâ* (תְּרוּמָה) — contribution, heave-offering (v. 2)
- *tabnît* (תַּבְנִית) — pattern, model, blueprint (vv. 9, 40)
- *ʾărôn hāʿēdût* (אֲרוֹן הָעֵדוּת) — ark of the testimony
- *kappōret* (כַּפֹּרֶת) — atonement cover, mercy seat
- *kĕrubîm* (כְּרֻבִים) — cherubim
- *lĕḥem pānîm* (לֶחֶם פָּנִים) — bread of the presence

## Lens 2 — The Literature

The genre is architectural-cultic instruction, a form whose closest ANE parallels are Mesopotamian temple-building texts — especially the Gudea cylinders (ca. 2100 BCE) describing the building of the Eninnu temple for Ningirsu, and the Baal palace-building cycle in the Ugaritic texts (KTU 1.1–1.6). Mark Smith and Jon Levenson have developed the Ugaritic parallel: Baal's palace is built according to a pattern, at the command of El, with heavenly specifications. Levenson's *Creation and the Persistence of Evil* (1988) argues that the tabernacle participates in an ANE genre in which the construction of a divine dwelling is a cosmogonic act — temple-building recapitulates creation.

The parallel is deliberately exploited by the biblical text. As Nachmanides first noticed, and Levenson developed in *Sinai and Zion* and *Creation*, the seven speeches of tabernacle instruction (marked by the formula "the LORD said to Moses" at Ex 25:1, 30:11, 30:17, 30:22, 30:34, 31:1, 31:12) parallel the seven days of creation in Genesis 1. The completion of the tabernacle in Ex 39–40 is described with verbal echoes of the completion of creation ("Moses saw all the work," "he finished the work," "Moses blessed them"). The tabernacle is a miniature cosmos; Israel's construction of it rehearses the divine act of creation itself.

The instruction is organized from inside out and from top down: the ark (the innermost, holiest object) first, then the table and menorah (in the outer sanctuary), then (in Ex 26) the structure itself, then the courtyard (Ex 27), then the priesthood (Ex 28–29), the altar and basin (Ex 30), the craftsmen (Ex 31). The construction narrative in Ex 35–40 then reverses the order: the structure first, then the furniture. Chiastic inversion reinforces the careful, Priestly shaping of the material.

The *tabnît* (pattern) of verses 9 and 40 is the chapter's philosophical linchpin. Moses is shown a pattern on the mountain and must reproduce it below. The language has invited Platonic and Neoplatonic reading throughout interpretive history — Philo in *Life of Moses* 2.74–76 is already reading *tabnît* as the Platonic Form, and Hebrews 8:5 explicitly cites Ex 25:40 to make the earthly tabernacle a copy of the heavenly. Critical scholarship (Victor Hurowitz, *I Have Built You an Exalted House*, 1992) has argued the underlying concept is not Platonic but Mesopotamian — the divinely revealed temple blueprint, attested in the Gudea cylinders' vision of Ningirsu's temple and in Esarhaddon's visionary temple plan.

Verse 8 — "let them make me a sanctuary, that I may dwell in their midst" (*wĕšākantî bĕtôkām*) — is the theological hinge of the chapter. The verb *šākan* gives both the noun *miškān* (tabernacle) and, in later Jewish theology, the feminine-form *šĕkînâ* (divine presence). John 1:14's *eskēnōsen en hēmin* ("dwelt/tabernacled among us") deliberately Hellenizes the Hebrew verb. Brevard Childs, Terence Fretheim, and N.T. Wright all identify this clause as the summit statement of Israelite theology — the transcendent God chooses to dwell among his people.

The "bread of the presence" (*lĕḥem pānîm*, v. 30) and the injunction that God will "meet with you… from between the cherubim" (v. 22) frame the sanctuary as a meeting-place, a *miqdāš* where God is encountered face-to-face (*pānîm ʾel pānîm*, cf. Ex 33:11). The text's spatial imagination is rigorously anthropomorphic — face, hand, voice, dwelling — even as its theology resists anthropomorphism elsewhere.

Textual variants: the LXX in Ex 25 has a somewhat different ordering of cultic furniture (the table is described before the ark in some manuscripts), and the details of the menorah differ in several particulars. The Samaritan Pentateuch preserves a tradition that moves verses from Ex 26 into Ex 25. 4QExodb and 4QpaleoExodm preserve parts of the chapter, with minor orthographic variants.

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

Exodus 25 opens a block (chapters 25–40) that comprises roughly one-third of the book of Exodus. The sheer scale of attention the final editor devotes to the tabernacle — compared with the terse handling of the exodus itself — makes the theological claim unmistakable: the point of the exodus is this. God redeemed Israel from Egypt in order to dwell among them. Everything in Genesis, every prior movement in Exodus, is ordered toward the tabernacle.

Canonically, the tabernacle is the paradigm for every subsequent Israelite sanctuary: the Shiloh sanctuary (1 Sam 1–4), Solomon's Temple (1 Kgs 6–8, where the cherubim, ark, and showbread table are scaled up), the Second Temple, and — in different ways — the synagogue tradition after 70 CE. The ark disappears at the destruction of the First Temple (the Holy of Holies of the Second Temple was empty, per Josephus, *War* 5.219); the cherubim tradition moves forward into Ezekiel's throne-visions and into Jewish mystical literature.

**Jewish Interpretive Trajectory**:
- Second Temple: Philo's *Questions on Exodus* 2.51–106 is an extended allegorical exposition — tabernacle as cosmos, ark as intelligible world, cherubim as divine powers. Josephus (*Antiquities* 3.102–187) gives detailed description with allegorical overlay. The Temple Scroll 11Q19 gives an idealized plan; Jubilees develops tabernacle-temple symbolism eschatologically.
- Rabbinic: The Mekhilta, Sifra, and tractates Menahot, Middot, Tamid develop the cultic regulations. Yoma 54a debates whether the cherubim faced each other or the wall, depending on Israel's obedience. On v. 8 the rabbis famously noted "that I may dwell in *them* (*bĕtôkām*)" — not "in it" but "in them" — the divine presence dwells in the people, not merely the building. The reading has weight throughout rabbinic literature.
- Medieval: Rashi works through the architectural details; Ibn Ezra attends to philology. Ramban's commentary is among his most theologically rich: the tabernacle is a continuation of Sinai — the glory that descended on Sinai takes up residence; the tent is a portable Sinai. Maimonides in *Guide* 3.32 and 3.45 offers the startling reading that the tabernacle and sacrifices were a *concession* to Israel's Egyptian-formed idolatrous instincts. Ramban explicitly rejects this. The Maimonides-Ramban debate is among the most important in medieval Jewish theology.
- Modern: Sarna's JPS Exodus, Milgrom's *Leviticus*, Haran's *Temples and Temple-Service*, Knohl's *Sanctuary of Silence*, and Levine's priestly work constitute the modern Jewish-scholarly core. Levenson's *Sinai and Zion* and *Creation and the Persistence of Evil* reshaped the conversation by reading the tabernacle as miniature cosmos. Zornberg's *Particulars of Rapture* gives a literary-psychological reading.

**Christian Interpretive Trajectory**:
- Patristic: Origen's *Homilies on Exodus* 9–13 inaugurated the Christian allegorical reading — ark as Christ, mercy seat as the cross, cherubim as the two Testaments, menorah as the sevenfold Spirit, table as the Eucharist. Gregory of Nyssa in *Life of Moses* 2.170ff treats the tabernacle as "the philosophy according to the mysteries," a portable discipline of ascent. Augustine in *City of God* 10 and Chrysostom on Hebrews develop the Christological typology.
- Medieval: Aquinas *ST* I-II q. 102 systematizes the cultus as ceremonial precepts prefiguring Christ. Bonaventure's *Itinerarium* structures the soul's ascent through six stages modeled on tabernacle furniture.
- Reformation: Luther's Hebrews 8–10 makes the Christological reading of the tabernacle foundational to Reformation atonement theology; Ex 25:40 becomes the warrant for the heavenly sanctuary in which Christ ministers. Calvin's *Commentary on the Last Four Books* treats the tabernacle as OT sacramental anticipation of Christ.
- Critical: Wellhausen's Second Temple retrojection thesis dominated the field for a century. Milgrom, Haran, and Knohl's *Sanctuary of Silence* (1995) pushed back; contemporary consensus is that P preserves pre-exilic tradition even if the final form is post-exilic. Dozeman's *Exodus* (ECC) treats the tabernacle as structural climax of the Priestly narrative.
- Contemporary: Brueggemann treats the tabernacle as the OT's supreme image of divine presence. Fretheim (*Exodus*, Interpretation) emphasizes divine *vulnerability* — God chooses to dwell in a tent. N.T. Wright's *Paul and the Faithfulness of God* makes temple theology foundational to Pauline Christology — Jesus *is* the place where God dwells. Morales's *Who Shall Ascend* and Beale's *The Temple and the Church's Mission* give the most extensive biblical-theological treatments.

**Where they converge and diverge**: Jewish and Christian traditions converge on the centrality of divine dwelling and on the tabernacle as paradigm sanctuary. They diverge on the forward reference. Jewish tradition reads the tabernacle forward into the Jerusalem Temple, then (after 70 CE) into the synagogue, the home (the Shabbat table as *lĕḥem pānîm*), and the study of Torah itself — *šĕkînâ* dwells wherever Torah is studied (Pirkei Avot 3:6). Christian tradition reads the tabernacle forward into Christ (John 1:14's *eskēnōsen*, "tabernacled"), into the church as the body of Christ (1 Cor 3:16, Eph 2:21–22), and into the eschatological new Jerusalem (Rev 21:3, "the tabernacle of God is with men"). Gregory of Nyssa's mystical reading and Philo's cosmic reading represent rare convergence points — both traditions have produced allegorical readings that transpose the tabernacle's structure into the soul's ascent or the universe's structure. The deepest divergence is on the relation between the tabernacle's physical particularity and its spiritual meaning: Jewish tradition has insisted on preserving the physical particularity (hence the continuing liturgical centrality of Temple-restoration in Jewish prayer), while Christian tradition has more readily absorbed the physical into the spiritual (though Orthodoxy, with its theology of sacred space, preserves particularity most strongly).

## Lens 4 — The Theological Intent

**Tension 1: Is the tabernacle a concession to human weakness (Maimonides) or a genuine divine desire for embodied presence (Ramban, most Christian tradition)?**

- Catholic: The *Catechism* (CCC 1165 on the liturgy, 2581 on God's dwelling) reads the tabernacle as the foreshadowing of the Incarnation — God's desire to dwell with humanity is eternal and is fulfilled in Christ and the Eucharist. Aquinas *ST* I-II q. 102 a. 4 answers the Maimonidean objection directly: the ceremonial precepts are not concessions but deliberate pedagogies, ordered to Christ.
- Reformed (Calvin): Calvin argues the tabernacle's physicality is pedagogical accommodation — God condescended to human capacity by giving visible forms — but the accommodation is gracious, not merely corrective. The tabernacle is not a concession to Israel's idolatry; it is God's gracious self-giving in forms Israel could receive.
- Lutheran: Luther reads the tabernacle Christologically: God has always desired to dwell among his people, and the tabernacle is the OT form of the same desire fulfilled in the Incarnation ("the Word became flesh and tabernacled among us," John 1:14 — Luther cites this constantly).
- Orthodox: Alexander Schmemann's *For the Life of the World* and *Introduction to Liturgical Theology* treat the tabernacle as the paradigm of sanctified space. Orthodoxy has never accepted the Maimonidean concessionist reading; the tabernacle is the fundamental architecture of divine-human encounter. Georges Florovsky in *Creation and Redemption* makes this theologically explicit.
- Jewish (Maimonides): *Guide* 3.32 argues God instituted sacrifices and the tabernacle because Israel, shaped by Egypt, could not immediately receive pure worship; the tabernacle redirected idolatrous habits toward YHWH. The argument is pedagogical-developmental — as humanity matures, cultic apparatus is gradually set aside.
- Jewish (Ramban): Ramban explicitly rejects Maimonides on this point. The tabernacle is the continuation of Sinai — what Sinai was in one place, the tabernacle makes portable. God genuinely desires to dwell among Israel; the dwelling is not a concession but the point.
- Critical: The tabernacle passages preserve a priestly theology in which the cultus is the divinely instituted means of ordered divine-human relation; it is neither concession nor mere pedagogy but the cosmic-moral structure of Israelite life.

Steelmanning the least intuitive — Maimonides' concessionist reading: Maimonides was not dismissing the tabernacle as unimportant; he was arguing that God's highest will is pure intellectual worship (in line with his Aristotelian philosophical theology), and that the tabernacle is a means by which Israel could grow toward that end. The reading is internally coherent with Maimonides' broader theology in *Guide* and with his account of prophetic accommodation (*Guide* 1.46). It has been resisted throughout Jewish tradition, but the argument is not trivial: it asks whether all religious particularity is ultimately instrumental to a more abstract divine purpose. Christian theologians who read the OT cultus as "types and shadows" displaced by the NT substance (Heb 8–10) are, functionally, making a Maimonidean argument from the Christian side — though without acknowledging it.

**Where this leaves us:** The dominant verdict across Christian traditions and most Jewish tradition is Ramban's: God genuinely desires to dwell among the people, and the tabernacle is the means by which he does so — not a concession to weakness but an expression of love. Yet Maimonides' reading has never been fully refuted; it continues to surface whenever religious communities debate the relative status of embodied practice versus interior devotion.

**Tension 2: Is the "pattern shown on the mountain" (*tabnît*, vv. 9, 40) a heavenly blueprint (Hebrews 8:5, Levenson) or a divinely revealed plan without ontological heavenly referent (critical minimalism, some rabbinic tradition)?**

- Catholic: *ST* I q. 1 on sacred doctrine and *ST* I-II q. 102 on ceremonial precepts maintain that the heavenly pattern is metaphysically real. The *Catechism* (CCC 1138) treats the heavenly liturgy (Rev 4–5) as the archetype of the earthly.
- Reformed (Calvin): Calvin's reading (following Hebrews) is that the earthly tabernacle corresponds to a genuine heavenly reality but the language is accommodated; the ontological status of the "heavenly tabernacle" should not be pressed into Platonic reification. Hodge and Vos develop this into a redemptive-historical typology.
- Lutheran: Luther cites Ex 25:40 and Heb 8:5 together; the heavenly pattern is Christ himself, the eternal Son in whom the Father's dwelling takes its archetypal form.
- Orthodox: The heavenly liturgy (celebrated eternally around the throne of God, per Rev 4–5 and the liturgical tradition) is the archetype; the earthly liturgy (in tabernacle, Temple, church) participates in it. Schmemann's liturgical theology and Florovsky's writings develop this.
- Jewish: The rabbinic tradition is strikingly reserved. The Bavli at Menahot 29a contains accounts of Moses' difficulty understanding the pattern (the menorah in particular — God showed him a form of fire on the mountain; Moses still could not grasp it), but the tradition generally does not develop a full heavenly-temple ontology (though some mystical texts do). Maimonides treats *tabnît* as the actual specification shown to Moses, not an ontological heavenly reality.
- Critical: Hurowitz's *I Have Built You an Exalted House* documents the ANE genre of divinely revealed building plans; the *tabnît* belongs to this genre, whose underlying theology is not Platonic heavenly-forms but the more specific notion of a divinely-authored blueprint communicated in vision.

Steelmanning the least intuitive — the critical minimalist position: the "pattern" language need not imply a Platonic heavenly sanctuary. The Gudea cylinders describe a divinely revealed blueprint without developing a theology of heavenly archetype; the pattern is simply what the god specifies. Philo's Platonic reading and Hebrews' appropriation of it reflect post-biblical development, not the original text. The reading preserves the text's specificity — Moses received detailed instructions — without the ontological weight that Platonism and later Christian theology imposed.

**Where this leaves us:** Every tradition that affirms a heavenly sanctuary (Catholic, Reformed, Lutheran, Orthodox) does so on the basis of NT texts (Heb 8–10, Rev 4–5) and theological development, not on the exclusive testimony of Ex 25. The text itself is more restrained: *tabnît* is what God shows Moses. Whether that pattern has ontological heavenly reality is a question Ex 25 does not itself answer.

**An unresolved question**: Ex 25:22 promises that God will "meet with you" and speak "from between the two cherubim that are on the ark of the testimony." But the Holy of Holies is entered only once a year, by the high priest alone (Lev 16). The tabernacle is designed as God's dwelling and as the place where God *cannot* normally be approached. How do we hold together the claim that God dwells among the people (v. 8) and the claim that the dwelling is almost entirely inaccessible? The tension — divine presence and divine hiddenness, sanctuary as both nearness and distance — runs through the entire biblical theology of the tabernacle and into its Christian and Jewish afterlives without final resolution.

## The Thread

Three non-obvious cross-references:

- The cherubim over the mercy seat (vv. 18–22) reappear at 1 Kings 6:23–28 (Solomon's Temple, with wings touching), Ezekiel 10 (the cherubim of the throne-chariot), and Revelation 4:6–8 (the four living creatures around the throne). The image of winged guardian beings flanking the divine throne is the single most sustained biblical iconographic tradition.
- The "pattern shown on the mountain" (vv. 9, 40) is cited twice in the NT: Acts 7:44 (Stephen's speech, where it authorizes the tabernacle against Solomon's Temple), and Hebrews 8:5 (where it authorizes the heavenly sanctuary as archetype). The same verse grounds two different NT uses, one suspicious of physical temples, one affirming the heavenly.
- The "bread of the Presence" (v. 30) is the OT anchor for Jesus' feeding miracles (Mark 6:30–44, the bread-in-the-wilderness typology), for his "bread of life" discourse (John 6, which culminates in eucharistic language), and for the early church's *agapē* meals. The twelve loaves for twelve tribes are echoed in the twelve baskets collected after the feeding of the five thousand — a deliberate narrative signal.
