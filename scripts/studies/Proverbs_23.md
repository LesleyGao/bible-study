# Proverbs 23 — Go Deeper

## Lens 1: The Historical World — Inside the Sayings of the Wise, Inside Amenemope's Shadow

Proverbs 23 sits in the heart of the **Sayings of the Wise** (*divrê chakamim*, דִּבְרֵי חֲכָמִים), the instruction-form collection that began at 22:17 and runs through 24:22. The chapter contains roughly the seventh through the twentieth of the **thirty sayings** signaled by 22:20, and it is the densest single chapter in the Hebrew Bible for direct verbal parallels with Egyptian instruction literature, especially **The Instruction of Amenemope** (12th–11th c. BC; published from the British Museum papyrus in 1923 by Wallis Budge).

Michael V. Fox's Anchor Bible commentary (*Proverbs 10–31*, 2009) is the most exhaustive treatment of the parallels. The chapter's specific borrowings include:

- **Pr 23:1–3** (the dinner with a ruler) ≈ **Amenemope ch. 23**: *"Do not eat bread before a noble… look at the cup which is before you, and let it satisfy your need… the great man with his food has it as his demon."* Both texts warn that a ruler's food is a *snare*; the Hebrew *lechem kezavim* (לֶחֶם כְּזָבִים, "deceptive bread," 23:3) and Amenemope's "demon-food" make the same point: hospitality at power's table comes with hidden costs.
- **Pr 23:4–5** (do not toil to acquire wealth — *"suddenly it sprouts wings, flying like an eagle toward heaven"*) ≈ **Amenemope ch. 7**: *"They have made themselves wings like geese, and have flown to the sky."* The bird-flight image for vanishing wealth is one of the most striking verbal parallels in the OT-ANE corpus.
- **Pr 23:6–8** (the stingy host's deceptive hospitality) ≈ **Amenemope ch. 11 (sometimes ch. 14 in older numberings)**: *"Do not covet the property of the dependent, nor hunger for his bread… it is a vomit-block to the throat."*
- **Pr 23:10–11** (do not move the ancient landmark, do not enter fatherless fields) ≈ **Amenemope ch. 6**: *"Do not carry off the landmark at the boundaries of the arable land… do not encroach upon the boundaries of a widow."* (This pairs with Pr 22:28, which had the same source.)

**Mainstream-critical position** (Fox, Roland Murphy, William P. Brown, Christine Roy Yoder, Will Kynes): the parallels are direct enough that literary dependence is the simplest explanation — Israel's sages had access to Egyptian instruction and reframed it under Yahwistic theology. **Conservative-evangelical accommodations** (Bruce Waltke, Tremper Longman III, Derek Kidner): the parallels show Israel's wisdom tradition engaging the broader ANE wisdom-stock; the inspired reframing is what matters. The reframing in Pr 23 is visible at 23:11 — where Amenemope speaks of cosmic order and the gods of Egypt, Proverbs names *go'alam chazak* (גֹּאֲלָם חָזָק, "their Redeemer is strong"). The Hebrew *go'el* is the kinsman-redeemer of Israel's covenant law (Lev 25; Ruth 4) — a thoroughly Israelite category imported into a borrowed saying. Whatever the borrowing, the theological re-centering is decisive.

The chapter also contains material with **no obvious Amenemope parallel**, including the great **wine-warning** of 23:29–35 — which appears to be distinctively Israelite, drawing on the same prophetic-wisdom sensibility as Isa 5:11–12, 22; 28:7–8.

## Lens 2: The Literature — Instruction Form, Vivid Imagery, and the Climactic Wine Poem

Pr 23 is **instruction** (*musar*), not sentence-proverb. The form is:
- Direct address to "my son" (*beni*, בְּנִי) — 23:15, 23:19, 23:26.
- Imperative or prohibition + motivation clause ("do not X… for Y").
- Multi-line units rather than two-line *meshalim*; some sayings span four to six lines.
- Vivid concrete imagery — wings on money, snake-bite from wine, a man asleep on the top of a mast.

The chapter's most extraordinary literary set-piece is **23:29–35**, the wine-temptation poem:

*Who has woe? Who has sorrow? Who has strife? Who has complaining? Who has wounds without cause? Who has redness of eyes?*

The Hebrew opens with six interrogative *mi* (מִי, "who") clauses — a riddle. The answer comes in v. 30: *those who tarry long over wine.* The poem proceeds through visual seduction (v. 31, *"do not look at wine when it is red, when it sparkles in the cup and goes down smoothly"* — Hebrew *yit'addam* יִתְאַדָּם "becomes red," *yiten 'eyno* יִתֵּן עֵינוֹ "gives its eye [sparkle] in the cup"), to physical consequence (v. 32, snake-bite imagery — *yishshok ke-nachash* יִשֹּׁךְ כְּנָחָשׁ, *u-ke-tsif'oni yafrish* וּכְצִפְעֹנִי יַפְרִישׁ "stings like an adder"), to perceptual disorder (v. 33, *zarot* זָרוֹת "strange things"; *tahpukot* תַּהְפֻּכוֹת "perverse things"), to vertigo (v. 34, lying on the top of a ship's mast in a storm), to the fatal final voice (v. 35) — the drunk who feels nothing, remembers nothing, and asks only *"when shall I awake? I must have another drink."*

Fox calls this poem the most psychologically sophisticated description of intoxication in any ANE text. It does not preach abstinence; it traces the experiential arc of addiction with cold precision.

**Key Hebrew terms:**
- **לֶחֶם כְּזָבִים** (*lechem kezavim*, "deceptive bread") — 23:3. Food that betrays the eater; not poisoned, but *politically* costly.
- **גֹּאֵל** (*go'el*, "redeemer") — 23:11. Kinsman-redeemer; the same word used of YHWH in Job 19:25, Isa 41:14, 43:14, etc. Here YHWH himself is the *go'el* of the orphan.
- **יַיִן** (*yayin*, "wine") — 23:30, 23:31. The most common Hebrew word for wine; not the same as *shekar* (שֵׁכָר, "strong drink") of Lev 10:9.
- **מִמְסָךְ** (*mimsakh*, "mixed wine") — 23:30. Wine mixed with spices or honey; potent. The English idiom "to try mixed wine" captures the deliberate seeking-out of stronger drink.
- **יַיִן זָר** — though this exact phrase does not appear in the Hebrew of 23:31, the broader "stranger-wine" motif (the wine that becomes a *zar*, a stranger, to the drinker) infuses the poem.
- **שְׁאוֹל** (*she'ol*, Sheol) — 23:14. The shadowy realm of the dead; rod-discipline (in the contested 23:13–14) is said to "save his *nefesh*" (soul) from Sheol.

The chapter's parallelism shifts between synthetic (line B advances line A — 23:1–3) and emblematic (a vivid image yields a maxim — the wings-on-wealth of 23:5).

## Lens 3: The Larger Story — Wisdom Canon, Jewish Trajectory, Christian Trajectory

**Within the wisdom canon**, Pr 23 develops three threads that ripple through the OT and beyond:
1. **Wealth's instability** (23:4–5) — taken up in Eccl 5:13–17 and distilled in Job 1:21.
2. **Care for the orphan and widow** (23:10–11) — central to Torah (Ex 22:22, Deut 10:18, Deut 24:17) and prophets (Isa 1:17, 1:23; Jer 7:6; Mal 3:5). The *go'el* language ties the wisdom tradition to Israel's covenant-redemption story.
3. **The dangers of wine** (23:29–35) — joined by Isa 5:11–12 (*"Woe to those who rise early in the morning, that they may run after strong drink"*) and the Nazirite vow tradition (Num 6).

**Jewish trajectory.** The wine-warning of 23:29–35 became a foundational text in rabbinic discussion of *yayin* and the limits of permissible drinking. Jewish *halakha* on wine is **moderation, not abstinence**: wine is essential to *kiddush* (the sanctification of Sabbath and festivals), to the Passover Seder's four cups, to the wedding canopy, to *havdalah*. The Talmud (*Eruvin* 65a, *Yoma* 76b) cites Pr 23 in its discussions of when wine is a blessing and when a curse — the same wine that gladdens the heart (Ps 104:15) destroys the man who tarries too long over the cup. *Megillat Esther* commentary uses Pr 23:31 to interpret the king's drunkenness in Esther 1.

The 23:13–14 **rod-discipline** verses ("if you strike him with a rod, he will not die… you will save his soul from Sheol") are treated by rabbinic tradition with significant nuance — Maimonides (*Hilkhot Talmud Torah* 2:2) urges restraint, recommending that a teacher strike *only with a small strap* and never out of anger; later authorities (R. Moshe Feinstein) effectively limited corporal punishment in education to vanishing thresholds.

**Christian trajectory.** The wine-warning became a battleground text in modern Christianity:

- **Catholic and Orthodox traditions.** Wine is sacramental — the cup of the Eucharist. Pr 23:29–35 is read as warning against intemperance, consonant with the Catholic virtue of temperance (one of the four cardinal virtues; cf. *CCC* §1809). Moderation, not abstinence, is the historic Catholic position.
- **Reformed traditions.** Calvin and the Reformers held to moderation — wine as a creational gift to be received with thanksgiving, abuse to be condemned. Pr 23 supports the moderation reading.
- **American evangelical temperance** (19th–20th c.). Pr 23:31 (*"do not look at wine when it is red"*) was central to the Prohibition argument: even *looking* at wine was forbidden. The Methodist, Baptist, and broader holiness movements built abstinence theology partly on this verse, often substituting grape juice for wine in communion (Welch's, 1869, was developed for this purpose).
- **Modern recovery and 12-step traditions.** AA's Big Book and recovery literature read Pr 23:29–35 as the most accurate ancient description of the addictive cycle — denial ("they struck me, but I did not feel it"), tolerance ("when shall I awake?"), and craving ("I must have another drink"). The poem's diagnostic precision is acknowledged by secular addiction medicine; AA founders Bill W. and Dr. Bob were both familiar with it.

**NT use.** Pr 23 is not directly quoted in the NT, but its themes saturate it:
- Pr 23:4–5 (do not toil for wealth) anticipates Mt 6:19–21 (*"do not lay up for yourselves treasures on earth, where moth and rust destroy"*).
- Pr 23:10–11 (the *go'el* of the orphan) joins the larger biblical tradition culminating in Christ as the great kinsman-redeemer (Heb 2:14–17, Tit 2:14).
- Pr 23:20–21, 23:29–35 (wine-warnings) feed Eph 5:18 (*"do not get drunk with wine, for that is debauchery, but be filled with the Spirit"*) and 1 Tim 3:3, 8 (overseers and deacons "not addicted to much wine").
- Pr 23:13–14 (discipline) — Heb 12:5–11 quotes Pr 3:11–12 on parental discipline, building the same ethic.

## Lens 4: The Theological Intent — Tensions, Positions, Steelman

Two tensions in Pr 23 deserve honest reckoning.

**Tension 1: The Amenemope-direct-parallel chapters.** Pr 23 contains the highest density of verbal parallels with Egyptian instruction in the entire OT. How should we hold it?

- *Mainstream-critical reading.* Pr 23:1–11 is materially dependent on Amenemope. The Hebrew sages received Egyptian wisdom, translated it, and reframed it under YHWH. Steelman: this is the historically honest position — the parallels are too dense and ordered to be coincidence; the doctrine of inspiration is not damaged by acknowledging it.
- *Conservative-evangelical accommodation.* Whether by direct borrowing or shared ANE wisdom-stock, Israel's sages exercised discernment in what they took up. The reframing — the *go'el* of 23:11 replacing Amenemope's cosmic order, the trust-in-YHWH frame of 22:19 — shows inspiration at work in selection and redirection. Steelman: this position has held for Waltke, Longman, Kidner, and many serious evangelical scholars; it does not require pretending the parallels are not there.
- *Older defensive reading* — Amenemope borrowed from Israel. Steelman: this preserved Solomonic priority on Mosaic-law assumptions; but it is now unsustainable on textual grounds.

**Where this leaves us:** Pr 23 is a test case for how we think about Scripture's authority. If we require that every word be cut whole-cloth from heaven without human-cultural mediation, the chapter becomes a problem. If we receive inspiration as the doctrine the Church has actually held — God speaking through human authors using their full cultural-linguistic toolkit, redirecting and reordering to reveal himself — the chapter becomes a gift. The Egyptian sage's wisdom about dinner with rulers, vanishing wealth, stingy hosts, and ancient landmarks is *true* wisdom, and YHWH claims it as his own through Israel's sages. Inspiration absorbs and redirects; it does not start from zero.

**Tension 2: The wine-warning (23:29–35) and Christian / Jewish reception.** The poem is unforgettable; what it teaches is contested.

- *Jewish moderation tradition.* Wine is a divine gift — *yayin yesammach levav-enosh* ("wine that gladdens the heart of man," Ps 104:15) — required for *kiddush*, Seder, weddings. Pr 23 warns against *tarrying long*, not against drinking. Steelman: the canonical witness is unambiguous; wine is sacramental and celebratory in Israel's life, and abstinence as moral norm is not biblical.
- *Catholic / Orthodox / Reformed moderation.* The Eucharistic cup is wine. Christian virtue is temperance, not abstinence. Pr 23:29–35 warns against the addiction-cycle, not against moderate use. Steelman: this is the historic Christian position across the great traditions for nineteen centuries.
- *Evangelical / holiness abstinence.* Pr 23:31's "do not look at wine when it is red" plus modern alcohol's potency, social cost, and addictive draw together justify total abstinence. Steelman: in cultures where alcohol-related harm is severe — addiction, domestic violence, traffic deaths, generational trauma — abstinence is not merely a personal preference but a love-of-neighbor witness; a recovering alcoholic must not drink at all, and a Christian community whose members include such persons may rightly choose collective abstinence.
- *Recovery-literature realism.* Pr 23:29–35 is not a moral lecture but a clinical description of addiction. For the addict, moderation is impossible; abstinence is survival. Steelman: this reading respects both the canonical norm of wine as gift and the experiential reality that for some persons it is a deep pit (cf. 23:27).

**Where this leaves us:** Pr 23 does not legislate one drinking-norm for every Christian; it gives diagnostic wisdom. The poem describes the experiential arc of addiction with precision sufficient to warn anyone whose hand has begun to reach too often for the cup. The canonical witness affirms wine as gift; the chapter warns that gifts can become snares; the wisdom is in knowing which side of the line you are on, and in honoring the conscience and the recovery of the brother or sister beside you.

**Closing question:** What is the cup you tarry over — wine, screen, scroll, food, status, work, news? The poem of 23:29–35 is not only about alcohol. It is about the slow movement from *gift* through *seduction* through *consequence* through *numbness* to the final voice — *"when shall I awake? I must have another."* Where in your life is that voice already speaking?
