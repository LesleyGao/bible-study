# Nehemiah 13: The Book Ends with a Plea Not to Be Forgotten

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

Nehemiah 13 is the most precisely datable chapter in the book and also the one whose dating creates the deepest theological problems. The frame is 13:6: "in the thirty-second year of Artaxerxes king of Babylon I went to the king. And after some time I asked leave of the king and came to Jerusalem." The thirty-second year of Artaxerxes I Longimanus (465–424 BC) is 433 BC. Nehemiah's first term as governor of Yehud began in the king's twentieth year (Neh 2:1, 445 BC), so his first term ran approximately twelve years. Then he returned to Susa for an unspecified interval — *yamim*, "days" or "some time" (13:6) — before requesting leave again. Most scholars (Williamson, Blenkinsopp, Lester Grabbe) read the second term as falling in the late 430s or early 420s BC. The chapter therefore narrates events between roughly 432 and 425 BC, the closing years of Artaxerxes I.

The Persian context is unchanged but stretched: the satrapy of *Avar-nahara* still administered from Damascus and ultimately from Susa; the sub-province of Yehud still under a *peḥah*. What has changed in Nehemiah's absence is that the surrounding regional powers — Sanballat the Horonite (governor of Samaria), Tobiah the Ammonite official, the Ashdodite merchants — have penetrated Yehud's institutions to a degree that genuinely shocks the returning governor. The marriage that Nehemiah confronts at 13:28 — the son of Joiada, who is the son of Eliashib the high priest, married to the daughter of Sanballat — is the most consequential. Through this single union, the high-priestly line of Jerusalem and the governing line of Samaria became kin. From a Persian-administrative standpoint, this is the standard mechanism of regional alliance; from a Yehud-cultic standpoint, this is the contamination of the temple itself.

The Tobiah problem (13:4–9) is structurally devastating. Eliashib the priest — almost certainly the high priest Eliashib of Neh 3:1 and 12:10, 22, though the text at 13:4 calls him simply "Eliashib the priest who was appointed over the chambers" — has cleared one of the temple's largest store-chambers (a *lishkah gedolah*, 13:5) of grain offerings, frankincense, vessels, and tithes, and given it to Tobiah as a private apartment. The chamber's *prior* contents are exactly what Neh 10:32–39 had committed the people to provide. The very chambers commissioned by the sealed covenant of ch. 10 to receive tithes have been emptied to house an Ammonite official. Williamson rightly calls this "the most devastating sentence in the book": the high priest himself has been compromised. The *lishkot* are not just rooms; they are the architectural manifestation of the covenant of ch. 10. Their occupation by Tobiah is the covenant's spatial undoing.

The Sabbath crisis (13:15–22) shows the same pattern: the commitment of 10:31 ("if the peoples of the land bring in goods or any grain on the Sabbath day to sell, we will not buy from them on the Sabbath or on a holy day") has collapsed. Tyrian fish merchants — Phoenician traders from Tyre, who maintained a colony in Jerusalem itself, "Tyrians also, who lived in the city" (13:16) — are running the Sabbath market. The historicity of a Phoenician merchant presence in fifth-century Jerusalem is supported by archaeological evidence of Phoenician material culture in late Iron-IIc and Persian-period strata across the Levant; Sara Japhet and Charles Carter both treat the detail as plausible. The tithe-and-Levite crisis (13:10–14) is the third covenantal failure: the Levites, unpaid, have abandoned their temple posts and fled to their fields. The temple is functionally hollowed out.

The marriage crisis (13:23–28) is told at the highest emotional register in the entire book. Nehemiah finds Jewish men married to women of Ashdod, Ammon, and Moab; the *children* "spoke the language of Ashdod, and they could not speak the language of Judah" (13:24). The narrator then describes Nehemiah's response with verbs of escalating violence: *va-arīv* (וָאָרִיב), "I confronted/contended"; *va-aqalelem* (וָאֲקַלְלֵם), "I cursed them"; *va-akkeh me-hem ana shim* (וָאַכֶּה מֵהֶם אֲנָשִׁים), "I beat some of them"; *va-emrṭem* (וָאֶמְרְטֵם), "I pulled out their hair." The verb *m-r-ṭ* (מָרַט) is a strong term — it appears in Isaiah 50:6 ("I gave my back to those who strike, and my cheeks to those who pull out the beard") and in Ezra 9:3 (Ezra pulling out his *own* hair in lament). Here Nehemiah pulls out the hair of others. The escalation is the point. The chapter then ends with the Sanballat-Joiada-grandson expulsion at 13:28 — which Josephus (*Antiquities* 11.302–311) treats as occurring much later, with the priest in question becoming the founder of the Samaritan temple at Mt. Gerizim. Cross's papponymous solution explains the chronological discrepancy: the Sanballat of Neh 13:28 is *the* Sanballat of Neh 2 (or his son), and Josephus's Sanballat is two generations later, sharing the dynastic name.

The chapter is the heart of the Nehemiah Memoir (NM). The first-person voice is unmistakable from 13:6 onward: "I went to the king," "I came to Jerusalem," "I discovered," "I was very angry," "I threw," "I gave orders." Williamson, Blenkinsopp, Throntveit, and Wright (*Rebuilding Identity*) all treat 13:4–31 as primary NM. The first-person "Remember me, O my God" formula appears three times in this chapter alone (13:14, 22, 31), and twice more in the rest of the NM (5:19, 6:14) — five times across the book, three concentrated in this final chapter. Wright's important argument is that the NM is, in genre, a *court memoir* or *self-defense document* — Nehemiah composing his own apology to God and to posterity, perhaps in his old age after his second term, perhaps in retirement. The chapter is therefore both a historical narrative and an extraordinary first-person *Apologia pro vita sua*.

Five terms repay attention. *Lishkah gedolah* (לִשְׁכָּה גְדוֹלָה) at 13:5, "a great chamber," the temple storeroom converted to housing — the architectural lynchpin tying ch. 13 to ch. 10. *ʾAleksandr* and *yikbol*… no, wait — *ḥātan* (חָתָן), "son-in-law" at 13:28, marking the kinship tie between Joiada's son and Sanballat. *Ashdōdīt* (אַשְׁדּוֹדִית), "the language of Ashdod" at 13:24 — the Philistine-coastal vernacular, attested in Persian-period ostraca from Ashdod and Idumaea. *Mašqi* / *Yafqedem*… Let me restart. The five operative Hebrew terms for this chapter: *zakhrah-li ʾelohay* (זָכְרָה־לִּי אֱלֹהַי), "remember me, O my God" (13:14, 22, 31) — the NM's signature phrase; *lishkah* (לִשְׁכָּה), "chamber," repeating from ch. 10; *ḥilel* (חִלֵּל), "profane," at 13:17–18 — Sabbath-profanation language drawn from Ezekiel; *ʾAshdodīt* (אַשְׁדּוֹדִית), "the language of Ashdod" at 13:24, the linguistic marker of cultural-religious dissolution; and *m-r-ṭ* (מָרַט), "pull out hair" at 13:25, the verb of physical violence Nehemiah turns on the offenders.

## Lens 2 — The Literature

The genre is **first-person memoir** at its most exposed. Nehemiah 13:4–31 is the longest sustained block of NM material outside chs. 1–6, and it functions as the book's *coda* — a chapter-length first-person reflection on what was supposed to be the consummation but turned out to be only a respite. The NM elsewhere alternates between administrative report (the wall-building chapters) and self-defense (the conflicts with Sanballat); ch. 13 is mostly self-defense, framed by the "Remember me" refrain.

The four reform-episodes are structurally parallel and deliberately escalating. (1) Tobiah expelled from the temple chamber (13:4–9), the narrator's first action upon return; (2) Levite tithes restored (13:10–14), addressing the personnel-and-economy crisis; (3) Sabbath enforcement (13:15–22), addressing the public-religious crisis; (4) the marriage crisis (13:23–28), the most violent and emotionally exposed. Each episode follows the same pattern: discovery, confrontation, action, restoration, "remember me." The pattern itself is literary — it imposes order on a sequence of events that, in lived experience, must have felt chaotic.

The single most important literary feature of the chapter is its **deliberate inversion of chapter 10**. Williamson and Eskenazi both treat this as the most carefully structured irony in post-exilic literature. Every commitment in ch. 10 is broken in ch. 13:

- 10:30 ("we will not give our daughters to the peoples of the land or take their daughters for our sons") → 13:23–28 ("the Jews who had married women of Ashdod, Ammon, and Moab")
- 10:31 ("if the peoples of the land bring in goods or any grain on the Sabbath day to sell, we will not buy from them") → 13:15–22 (Tyrian fish merchants in Jerusalem on Sabbath)
- 10:32–39 (the temple-support stipulations: third-shekel tax, wood offering, firstfruits, tithes, the *lishkot*) → 13:4–9 (the *lishkah* given to Tobiah), 13:10–14 (the tithes not delivered, the Levites fled)

The seal of ch. 10 is broken in ch. 13, item by item. Eskenazi (*In an Age of Prose*) reads this not as an accident of redaction but as the redactor's deliberate theological strategy: post-exilic covenant under colonial conditions cannot be self-sustaining. The literary form *says* what the explicit theology cannot.

The "Remember me, O my God" refrain (13:14, 22, 31) — *zakhrah-li ʾelohay* — is the NM's signature, occurring also at 5:19 and 6:14 (a slight variation, "remember Tobiah and Sanballat against me"). Three of the five occurrences are concentrated in this final chapter, framing each major reform-episode. The refrain is **doxological and forensic at once**: it asks God to remember favorably, against the implicit forgetting that human history makes inevitable. Williamson reads the formula as a literary signature; Wright (*Rebuilding Identity*) reads it as the rhetorical core of the Memoir — the Memoir is, in essence, a sustained *zakhrah-li*, a written demand that God remember.

The book ends with the formula. 13:31's final words, "Remember me, O my God, for good," are the last verse of Nehemiah and — in the canonical ordering of the Hebrew Bible (where Ezra-Nehemiah is part of the Writings) — also one of the last verses of Old Testament historical narrative. The Christian canon places the prophets after Nehemiah, but in narrative-historical terms, the Old Testament's story-arc *ends here*. With a man asking God not to forget him.

A second literary feature: the abrupt prose violence of 13:25. The narrator does not soften, dignify, or theologize what he did. "I confronted them and cursed them and beat some of them and pulled out their hair." The verbs come in clipped succession; the syntax is pure narration without commentary. This is not the prose style of someone trying to defend a moral position; it is the prose style of someone *reporting what happened*. Wright's reading: the NM is a defense, but it is not a *softening* defense — Nehemiah is willing to record his own violence because he believes that what he did was necessary to preserve the community.

Textual variants: the LXX (Esdras β) preserves the chapter substantially as in the Hebrew. Notable: 13:5's *lishkah gedolah* is rendered with Greek terms that emphasize the storage function (*pastophorion*, "treasury chamber") in some manuscripts. The Vulgate normalizes the violent verbs of 13:25 slightly but does not soften them materially. 1 Esdras famously *omits the entire chapter* — the Greek alternative tradition ends with Ezra's Torah-reading and never narrates Nehemiah's reforms or the broken covenant.

The chapter's place in the book's argument is the deliberate undoing of everything chs. 1–12 had constructed. This is not a tonal accident — it is the redactor's theological argument.

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

In the canonical arc, Neh 13 is the historical narrative's last chapter. Cyrus 538, Zerubbabel 520, Ezra 458, Nehemiah 445/433. After this, prophetic critique (Malachi) and apocalyptic hope (Daniel, in its final form) take over. The historical-narrative line itself ends here. The reader is left with a man asking God not to forget him.

**Jewish Interpretive Trajectory**

Second Temple readings of Neh 13 are reticent. 1 Esdras *omits* the chapter entirely — the Greek tradition declined to include Nehemiah's reforms in its restoration narrative. 2 Maccabees 1:18–36, by contrast, transforms Nehemiah into a hero of altar-fire restoration, projecting onto him a Hasmonean rededication theology. Sirach 49:13 names him: "the memorial of Nehemiah is also great, who raised up for us the walls that had fallen and set up the gates and bars and rebuilt our ruined houses." Sirach focuses on construction; the reforms are not mentioned.

The rabbinic tradition treats Nehemiah ambivalently. *Bava Batra* 14b–15a famously says, "Ezra wrote his book and the genealogies of Chronicles up to himself." A parallel tradition in *Sanhedrin* 93b asks why Nehemiah's name is not on the title of his own book and answers: because he claimed credit for himself ("Remember me, O my God, for good") — the book is therefore named *Ezra*, not *Nehemiah*, in some early Hebrew traditions. *Yoma* 9b and other tractates treat the chamber-cleansing of 13:4–9 as paradigmatic for the post-temple discussion of *kelei ha-mikdash*, the temple vessels. The marriage prohibition of 13:23–28 became the locus classicus for the rabbinic development of the *yikhus* concept and the matrilineal-descent rule (*Kiddushin* 68b). Maimonides codifies the latter in *Mishneh Torah, Hilkhot Issurei Biah*; the link to Neh 13 (and Ezra 9–10) is direct.

Medieval Jewish commentary on Neh 13 is dense. Rashi reads the chamber-cleansing as protecting *kedushah*; Ibn Ezra emphasizes the political reality of fifth-century Yehud; Radak harmonizes with the Ezra parallels. Modern Jewish scholarship — Sara Japhet's commentary, Tamara Eskenazi's *In an Age of Prose*, Jacob Wright's *Rebuilding Identity* (which reads the NM as defensive memoir), Jon Levenson's work on covenant and chosenness, Daniel Smith-Christopher's *A Biblical Theology of Exile* (which reads boundary-marking as colonial-survival ethics) — generally rehabilitates the chapter from the Wellhausen-era charge of legalism. The modern Jewish reading: Nehemiah 13 is the documentation of *what it cost* to remain a distinct people under Persian colonial rule.

**Christian Interpretive Trajectory**

Patristic engagement with Neh 13 is sparser than with the wall-building chapters. Bede's *In Ezram et Neemiam* (c. 725) reads the four reforms as four spiritual cleansings of the soul-temple — Tobiah's expulsion as the casting-out of carnal affections, Sabbath enforcement as the recovery of contemplation, the marriage purification as separation from worldly entanglements. Origen's allegorical method does similar work. The Glossa Ordinaria carries Bede forward.

Calvin's *Lectures on Nehemiah* (1559) treats ch. 13 as a sustained meditation on the failure of human resolution and the necessity of magisterial reform. Calvin reads Nehemiah's actions — even the violent ones — as legitimate exercise of the *cura religionis*, the magistrate's care for religion. The Geneva consistory drew on this reading in its disciplinary program. Luther's preface to Nehemiah is brief but seizes on the theological pattern: what is sealed in ch. 10 is broken in ch. 13, proving that the law cannot save and that human resolve is insufficient. Luther reads ch. 13 as a sustained witness to the inadequacy of *Gesetz* (law) without *Evangelium* (gospel). The radical reformers (Anabaptist, especially Menno Simons) cited Neh 13 in justifying strict church discipline and the practice of *Bann* (excommunication).

The critical tradition has been the most consequential and the most damaging. Wellhausen's *Prolegomena zur Geschichte Israels* (1878) read Neh 13:23–28 — the foreign-wives expulsion — as the dark turn into "Judaism" as legalism, the constriction of pre-exilic prophetic religion into post-exilic ethnic enclosure. This reading dominated late-nineteenth- and early-twentieth-century Christian biblical scholarship and provided ideological cover for both academic anti-Judaism and, eventually, much worse. C.C. Torrey's hyper-skeptical historical reading dismissed the chapter as fiction. The post-Holocaust generation has worked hard to repair the damage. Brevard Childs's canonical reading treats the chapter as theologically generative within the canon as a whole. Walter Brueggemann's commentaries develop the concept of "fragile faithfulness" to characterize ch. 13: the faithfulness is real but not durable. Daniel Smith-Christopher's *A Biblical Theology of Exile* reads the marriage prohibition as colonial-survival ethics and explicitly rejects the Wellhausen frame. Christine Hayes's *Gentile Impurities and Jewish Identities* (2002) is the decisive technical study — Hayes shows that Neh 13 introduces a *new* category, "genealogical impurity," that did not exist in earlier ritual systems, and that this innovation is theologically and politically motivated rather than pathological. Mark Throntveit, David Janzen, H.G.M. Williamson, Joseph Blenkinsopp, and Lester Grabbe make up the contemporary critical mainstream.

**Where the Traditions Converge and Diverge**

Both traditions converge on the chapter's narrative seriousness — neither tradition treats it as window-dressing. Both notice the irony with ch. 10. Both read the high priest's compromise (13:4–9) as devastating. The decisive divergence remains the marriage prohibition. The Jewish tradition, especially in its rabbinic and matrilineal-descent form, reads 13:23–28 as the constitutive moment for a load-bearing identity practice. The Christian tradition, after Paul (Gal 3:28) and the Acts 15 council, reads it as a culturally conditioned boundary that the gospel transcends — though the *manner* of transcending varies sharply (Lutheran *Gesetz/Evangelium*, Reformed third-use-of-the-law, Catholic sacramental boundary, Anabaptist church-discipline, post-Holocaust contextual). The Wellhausen reading — that the prohibition is the dawn of legalism — has been largely abandoned by careful scholars across the spectrum, but its cultural afterlife in popular Christian readings persists.

## Lens 4 — The Theological Intent

**Tension One: Ethnic-Genealogical Boundary-Marking — Bigotry, Survival, or Both?** This is the chapter's hardest tension. Nehemiah confronts Jewish men married to foreign women, beats some of them, pulls out their hair, and forces them to swear an oath against further intermarriage (13:23–28). He expels the high priest's grandson because he is Sanballat's son-in-law (13:28). What is the moral status of this? The Wellhausen tradition — dominant from 1878 to roughly 1945, persisting in popular Christian reading even today — read this as the dark constriction of prophetic religion into ethnic legalism. *Late* prophetic religion (Ruth, Jonah, parts of Isaiah 56) opens to the nations; *post-exilic* legalism closes against them; the gospel, for Wellhausen, recovers the prophetic openness against the priestly enclosure. This reading provided ideological cover for both academic anti-Judaism and worse, and it has been substantially deconstructed by post-Holocaust scholarship.

The Jewish tradition reads the prohibition as *boundary-marking necessary for survival* in a colonized province. Christine Hayes's *Gentile Impurities and Jewish Identities* shows that Neh 13 introduces a *new* category — genealogical impurity — that did not exist in earlier biblical ritual. This is not the continuation of pre-exilic purity logic; it is a post-exilic theological innovation, motivated by the actual political situation of fifth-century Yehud. Daniel Smith-Christopher's *A Biblical Theology of Exile* reframes the prohibition as the standard practice of colonized peoples maintaining identity against imperial pressure. Jon Levenson and Jacob Wright extend this. The post-Holocaust Christian reading has largely accepted these correctives.

The Catholic and Orthodox traditions hold a sacramental view of marriage that preserves the prohibition's intent (marriage within the faith) while denying its ethnic content (the gospel includes all nations). Vatican II's *Nostra Aetate* makes the latter explicit. The Reformed tradition tends to historicize the prohibition strictly: *for then, in that situation*; Galatians 3:28 is the relativizing word. The Anabaptist tradition cited the prohibition in support of strict community-internal marriage and church discipline. The Pauline tradition (Gal 3:28, 2 Cor 6:14) treats the underlying principle of *yoking* with covenant-faithfulness as preserved while the ethnic content is dissolved.

The steelman of the position one finds least native: if you read the chapter as bigotry, the steelman is that the prohibition is the *only* reason Judaism survived to produce the literature you are reading; without 13:23–28, the Jewish people are absorbed into the Tobiad-Sanballat coalitions within a generation, and there is no Second Temple Judaism, no New Testament, no gospel. If you read the chapter as righteous boundary-marking, the steelman is that the *manner* of the enforcement — beating, hair-pulling, forced oath — is genuinely violent, and that across history this passage has been used to justify ethnic exclusion of a kind the chapter itself does not authorize. **Where this leaves us:** there is no clean resolution. The prohibition does the political work it does, and that work is morally compromised by any standard that values human worth across ethnic lines, but it is also the precondition under which the community survived to produce the literature that eventually said "neither Jew nor Greek." Sitting with both is the most honest available posture.

**Tension Two: The Unresolved Ending — Restoration or Disillusionment?** The book ends with "Remember me, O my God, for good." This is its final line. What does it mean that the Old Testament's historical-narrative arc ends with a man asking God not to forget him? The Childs canonical reading frames the ending as part of the larger canon's witness: Nehemiah 13's failure points forward to the prophetic literature (Malachi, Isaiah 56–66) and ultimately to the New Testament's claim that what Israel could not sustain, Christ accomplishes. In the canonical reading, the disillusionment of ch. 13 is *purposive* — it creates the readerly hunger for what comes next. Walter Brueggemann's reading is darker. In *Theology of the Old Testament* and elsewhere, Brueggemann insists that the testimony of the Old Testament includes "fragile faithfulness" without resolution — that ch. 13 names a real and unresolved tension between God's promises and Israel's capacity, and that this tension is *not* dissolved by canonical hindsight. The Jewish tradition, especially in its modern form (Levenson, Wright, Eskenazi), reads the ending as *humanly truthful*: Nehemiah's "remember me" is the prayer of every faithful person who has done what he could and is left dependent on God's memory, not his own legacy. The Christian liberal tradition tends to treat the ending as confirmation that pre-Christian religion was incomplete — a reading that flirts with supersessionism. The Catholic and Orthodox traditions treat the ending eucharistically: the prayer "remember me" anticipates the *anamnesis* of the liturgy, the ongoing remembering that God does in the Mass. Lutheran tradition reads it as the law's despair waiting for the gospel. The radical-reformation tradition reads it as the proper humility of every believer. **Where this leaves us:** the chapter's ending is genuinely ambiguous. It can be read as setup-for-fulfillment (Childs), as honest tragic limit (Brueggemann), as eternal liturgical anamnesis (Catholic/Orthodox), as law-needing-gospel (Lutheran), or as the prayer of every saint (Reformed/Jewish modern). All five readings are textually defensible. The text does not adjudicate.

**The Unresolved Question:** If the Old Testament canon's historical narrative ends with a man begging God not to forget him, what does it mean that the next book in the Christian Bible (Esther in some orderings, Job in others, the prophets in others) is *not* the answer to his prayer — and what does it mean that the Hebrew Bible's *next* canonical section (in the rabbinic ordering, Chronicles) loops the entire story back to Adam, as if Nehemiah's ending forced the canon to start over?

## The Thread

**Deuteronomy 23:3–6.** "No Ammonite or Moabite may enter the assembly of the LORD. Even to the tenth generation, none of them may enter the assembly of the LORD forever, because they did not meet you with bread and water on the way." This is the proof-text Nehemiah 13:1–3 cites at the chapter's opening — the Mosaic warrant for the foreign-exclusion policy. The exegetical irony is that Ruth, the great-grandmother of David (Ruth 4:18–22), is a Moabite. The post-exilic community is operating with a Deuteronomic categorical that the Davidic genealogy itself violates. Some scholars (Yair Zakovitch, Tikva Frymer-Kensky) read the book of Ruth as composed in the Persian period as a deliberate counter-text to Ezra-Nehemiah's intermarriage prohibition; if that dating is right, Ruth and Neh 13 are arguing.

**Ezra 9:1–10:44.** Ezra's earlier handling of the same problem, dated to roughly 458 BC. Ezra responds to the foreign-wives crisis by tearing his own clothes and pulling out his own hair (Ezra 9:3) and ultimately compelling a mass divorce (Ezra 10:18–44). Nehemiah, fifteen to twenty-five years later, finds the problem recurring, and his response is more violent: he pulls out *others'* hair. The Ezra-Nehemiah pattern shows that the problem was structural, not solved by a single intervention. David Janzen's *Witch-Hunts, Purity, and Social Boundaries* (2002) is the key technical reading; Janzen reads both events sociologically, as boundary-rituals enacted by a stressed community.

**Malachi 2:10–16, 3:6–12.** Malachi's prophecy, dated by most scholars (Andrew Hill, David Petersen) to within a generation of Nehemiah, addresses the *exact* failures of Neh 13: "Judah has been faithless… Judah has profaned the sanctuary of the LORD, which he loves, and has married the daughter of a foreign god" (2:11), and "you are robbing me… in your tithes and contributions" (3:8). Malachi is the prophet of Nehemiah 13's broken covenant. The two texts are best read together.

**Acts 15:13–21 / Galatians 3:28.** James's Jerusalem-council ruling and Paul's universalist formula resolve the boundary-marking question that Neh 13 left open — but they do so in radically different ways. James preserves *some* of the boundary (the four prohibitions of Acts 15:20) while opening table-fellowship across the Jew–Gentile line. Paul declares the underlying ethnic distinction soteriologically dissolved ("there is neither Jew nor Greek… in Christ Jesus"). The forward arc from Neh 13 to these texts is not a smooth typology but a wrestling — the New Testament does not simply *cancel* Neh 13's concerns; it relocates them. The question of *who is in the assembly* runs from Deut 23 through Neh 13 through Acts 15 through Gal 3 and remains, in some forms, a live question for Christian and Jewish communities still.
