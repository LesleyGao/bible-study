# Job 39: The Wild Things That Will Not Serve

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

Chapter 39 continues, without break, the first of YHWH's speeches from the whirlwind begun in 38:1. Where chapter 38 toured cosmology — foundations, sea, dawn, storehouses, constellations — chapter 39 turns to zoology. God presents a gallery of wild animals: the mountain goat (*yāʿēl*), the hind (*ʾayyālāh*), the wild donkey (*pere*), the onager (*ʿārôd*), the wild ox (*rěʾēm*), the ostrich, the warhorse, the hawk, and the eagle. The selection is deliberate. These are not the domesticated animals of Israelite daily life — ox, donkey, sheep — whose ownership is regulated in Exodus 21–23 and whose ritual status is fixed in Leviticus 11. They are their untamed counterparts.

The chapter is unthinkable outside the ancient Near Eastern world of royal hunting iconography and zoological wisdom. The Assyrian palace reliefs at Nineveh (seventh century BCE, now in the British Museum) depict Ashurbanipal spearing lions and riding down onagers — the *pere* and *rěʾēm* of Job 39 were the signature quarries of royal virility in Mesopotamian imperial self-presentation. The Egyptian onomastica (Amenemope's and related lists) preserve extensive catalogues of natural phenomena including animals, organized for scribal instruction. Von Rad's famous 1955 essay "Hiob 38 und die altägyptische Weisheit" argued that the YHWH speeches drew formally from this onomastic tradition — though, as Habel and Clines note, the argument is structural rather than evidentiary, and the tone of Job 39 diverges sharply from dry Egyptian lists by being *praise* rather than *catalogue*.

The zoology of chapter 39 is mostly accurate to the fauna of the Levantine and Arabian landscape. The wild ass (*pere*, *Equus hemionus*) ranged from the Syrian desert through the Zagros; the onager is its swifter cousin. The wild ox (*rěʾēm*, often rendered "unicorn" in older translations via Septuagint *monokerōs* and Vulgate *rhinoceros*) is almost certainly the aurochs (*Bos primigenius*), a massive wild cattle species that survived in the Near East into the first millennium BCE before going extinct in Europe only in 1627. The ostrich (*raʿanannîm* or, with emendation, *reněnîm* in 39:13) — now extinct in the region since the mid-twentieth century — was then a native species, ranging throughout the Arabian peninsula into southern Palestine. The hawk (*nēṣ*) and eagle (*nešer*, probably griffon vulture) were, and remain, widespread.

Textually, chapter 39 shows some of the book's characteristic difficulties. The ostrich passage (39:13–18) is not in the shorter Old Greek text of Job but was restored by Origen from Theodotion; Marvin Pope and others have suggested the passage may be an interpolation or a later addition. 11QTargum Job preserves fragments of the chapter in Aramaic translation, including the wild ass section. The Hebrew text of 39:13 is notoriously difficult — the word usually translated "ostrich" (*raʿanannîm*) is unique and uncertain, and most modern commentators emend.

Five Hebrew terms anchor the chapter. First, פֶּרֶא (*pere*), the wild donkey, onager — a byword for ungovernability (Genesis 16:12 likens Ishmael to a *pere*). Second, רְאֵם (*rěʾēm*), the aurochs or wild ox, emblem of untamable strength (Numbers 23:22, Deuteronomy 33:17, Psalm 22:21). Third, רננים (*rěnānîm*, 39:13), the disputed ostrich term — possibly from the root *rnn* ("to shout, sing"), naming the bird by its strange cry. Fourth, the onomatopoeic הֶאָח (*heʾāḥ*, 39:25), the warhorse's "Aha!" at the sound of battle — one of the most arresting sonic moments in biblical poetry. Fifth, the repeated negative interrogative with the second person singular pronoun, הֲתֵדַע (*hătēdaʿ*, "do you know"), continuing from chapter 38 and pressing home human non-mastery over a non-human world.

## Lens 2 — The Literature

Chapter 39 forms the closing movement of the first divine speech. If chapter 38 is the macro-tour of cosmology, chapter 39 is the micro-tour of untamed biology. The structural pairing is deliberate: chapter 38 asks "who shut in the sea," chapter 39 asks "who has let the wild donkey go free." Both questions assume that the ordering of the world — including the freedom of some of it — is God's work, not human management.

Habel, in his commentary on Job (OTL), reads the animal gallery as a "wisdom bestiary" aesthetically close to Proverbs 30 (the things "too wonderful for me," including the way of an eagle in the sky). Newsom (NIB) argues that the selection is driven by a single thematic concern: *wildness*. Every animal in the chapter is either useless to humans (ostrich, hawk, eagle), ungovernable by humans (wild donkey, wild ox), or paradoxically useful precisely because of its untamability (warhorse). The warhorse is the telling middle case: it is domesticated, yet its value lies in behaviors that mirror wildness — exulting in battle, laughing at fear, smelling the fray from afar. The chapter refuses the category of the "merely domestic."

Alter, in *The Art of Biblical Poetry*, identifies the governing rhetorical strategy as what he calls "the poetry of creaturely verve." The descriptions do not read like scientific classifications; they read like portraits energized with perspective-taking. The ostrich "laughs at the horse and his rider." The warhorse says "Aha!" The eagle's young "suck up blood." The animals are given interiority, agency, even a kind of speech. Alter notes that the Hebrew verbs in these passages are unusually varied and vivid: the warhorse *paws* (*yāḥpōr*), *exults* (*śîś*), *laughs* (*śāḥaq*), *swallows* (*yegammēʾ*) the ground.

The most notorious crux is the ostrich passage (39:13–18). The ostrich is described as cruel to her young (a common ancient misreading of her nesting behavior — ostriches do nest communally in ground depressions and do sometimes abandon late eggs as food for hatched chicks), and yet, remarkably, the text attributes this not to a moral defect but to divine design: "God has made her forget wisdom and given her no share in understanding" (39:17). As Gordis noted, this is theologically startling. Newsom draws out the implication: the chapter presents foolishness and apparent cruelty as part of the cosmos God has made — not as fallen marring but as legitimate variation. The ostrich, despite her "foolishness," can outrun the horse; her creation has its own integrity. The passage deliberately undoes the wisdom-tradition assumption that everything in creation exemplifies order and sense. Some creatures are what they are because God made them that way, and "wisdom" as humans define it does not exhaust the range of divine craft.

The chapter ends on the eagle (39:27–30), with an image that the Christian tradition often found uncomfortable: "Where the slain are, there is he." The eagle (or vulture) feeding on corpses is the final image of the first divine speech. The effect is not reassurance but astonishment — the cosmos is not sanitized, and the scavenger's rapacity is presented without apology.

Textually, the LXX's shorter form (before Theodotionic restoration) omits 39:13–18 (the ostrich) and shortens several other sections. This has fueled the hypothesis that the ostrich passage is a secondary insertion, though Clines considers the argument inconclusive. 11QTargum Job's fragments show the ostrich and warhorse passages with fairly literal Aramaic rendering.

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

Canonically, Job 39 is part of the most extended divine speech in the Bible. In Jewish tradition, its animal gallery became a locus for reflection on divine purposes beyond human utility. The Talmud (Bava Batra 16a) discusses the wild ox (*rěʾēm*) as an eschatological delicacy to be served at the messianic banquet — a tradition that builds, perhaps playfully, on the untamability motif of Job 39 and on Psalm 92:10. Rashi reads the animal passages as demonstrating *hashgachah peratit* (individual providence): that even the ostrich, the wild ass, the eagle's chick have God's attention. Ibn Ezra pays close attention to the naturalist detail and corrects older interpretations where he thinks the Hebrew has been misread (he is particularly thoughtful on *rěʾēm*). Maimonides, consistent with his reading of chapter 38, uses chapter 39 as evidence for his thesis in *Guide* III.22–23 that divine knowledge and human knowledge are incommensurable: the ostrich's "foolishness" is not a divine oversight but a mode of being that does not map onto human categories of wisdom. Nachmanides reads the chapter mystically, as a hint that the diverse orders of creaturely life reflect hidden letters of Torah.

Levenson's work on *Creation and the Persistence of Evil* is less directly engaged with chapter 39 than with chapters 38 and 41, but his framework illuminates the animal gallery. If creation is not a finished, completely ordered, morally transparent product but a partially ordered world, then the wild animals of chapter 39 are not a problem to be solved but a feature to be confessed. Kugel and Zornberg both note how the chapter resists the temptation to domesticate the cosmos. Zornberg in particular has written on how the Joban theophany opens up a post-traumatic mode of speech in which the wild is allowed to be wild without needing to be explained away.

In Christian tradition, Gregory the Great's *Moralia in Job* gives each animal an allegorical reading. The wild ass is the unbelieving Gentile who ranges outside the city of God; the wild ox is the proud philosopher resistant to yoke; the ostrich is the negligent preacher; the warhorse is the zealous martyr; the eagle is Christ himself, whose young "suck up blood" by receiving the Eucharist. Gregory's readings are startling to modern eyes but internally consistent: for him the text's realism is a raw material that must be spiritualized to yield its true sense. Thomas Aquinas in his Job commentary takes a more literal and philosophical approach, reading the animals as demonstrations that divine providence operates through secondary causes — the ostrich is "foolish" by nature as part of the ordered variety of creation. Calvin's sermons on chapter 39 emphasize the humbling of human pretension to universal understanding.

Modern Christian readers have used chapter 39 to inaugurate what Ellen Davis calls an "ecological hermeneutic" of Scripture. Davis (*Getting Involved with God*), Bill McKibben, Norman Habel in his Earth Bible Project, and John Walton have all emphasized that the chapter asserts the intrinsic value of non-human creatures. The wild ass is not for anyone's use; the ostrich is not a good mother by human standards; the eagle feeds on the slain — and yet each is celebrated in the divine voice. This undercuts any triumphalist reading of Genesis 1:28's dominion mandate that would make humans the measure of all value. Clines (WBC) reads the chapter as subverting the anthropocentric premises of the preceding wisdom dialogues. Gutiérrez, in *On Job*, reads the chapter's refusal to cater to human utility as a theological resource for solidarity with those who do not fit regnant social categories.

A decisive convergence across Jewish and Christian traditions is the recognition that chapter 39 decenters the human: the cosmos does not exist for human utility. A decisive divergence is what follows. Traditional Christian allegory (Gregory, medieval commentary) re-centers the chapter on redemptive history by reading each animal as a figure within the Christian moral-spiritual economy. Modern ecological readings (Davis, McKibben) and much Jewish commentary (Maimonides, Levenson) resist this re-centering: the animals mean what they are, not what they represent. The ostrich is not a negligent preacher; she is an ostrich, and her foolishness is a mode of being God has given.

The specific chaoskampf motif of Leviathan is absent from chapter 39, but the thematic continuity is striking: the wild animals of chapter 39 are the naturalistic counterpart to the mythological monsters of chapters 40–41. In both cases God is enumerating creatures that exceed human control. Fyall (*Now My Eyes Have Seen You: Images of Creation and Evil in the Book of Job*, 2002) argues for thematic continuity between the naturalistic bestiary of chapter 39 and the mythological bestiary of chapters 40–41, with the warhorse as a transitional figure — a creature whose mastery of fear foreshadows Leviathan's own fearlessness (41:33).

## Lens 4 — The Theological Intent

**Tension 1: Do the wild animals of chapter 39 argue for providence or for the limits of providence?**

- *Providential reading* (Rashi, Aquinas, Andersen): Every creature is under God's care. The ostrich's foolishness is part of a wise divine design. The wild ass roams because God has loosed his bonds. Providence is universal, even when its logic eludes us.
- *Limiting-providence reading* (Levenson, Fyall, Clines): The cosmos contains significant wildness that God does not eliminate. The chapter is not showing that everything is orderly; it is showing that a substantial portion of creation is legitimately outside human, and in some ways outside providential, management. God lets the wild ass go free rather than tames it.
- *Epistemic reading* (Maimonides, Newsom): The question is not whether providence operates but whether we can read it. Chapter 39 disabuses Job of the assumption that creation's patterns are transparent to human judgment. Some of what God makes is *opaque* to us, including morally opaque.
- *Steelmanned least-intuitive reading* (Davis, ecological theology): The chapter argues not for the limits of providence but for the inadequacy of the category of providence itself. The chapter is not primarily about God's relation to creatures but about creatures' existence as *goods in themselves*. The ostrich is not a problem to be providentially solved; she is a being worth celebrating. This reading is hardest because it withdraws the theological question that has governed centuries of commentary and substitutes an aesthetic-ontological claim: the cosmos is good in its variety, including its uselessness.

**Tension 2: Is the warhorse celebrated or ironized?**

- *Celebratory reading* (most traditional commentary): The warhorse is a magnificent creature whose majesty in battle reveals divine craftsmanship. His "Aha!" is the joy of a being fulfilling its nature.
- *Ironic reading* (Good, Clines in some moods): Given the rest of Job's lament about violence and injustice, the celebration of the warhorse is unsettling. Is God praising a creature whose sole use is to bear humans into killing? The image is not obviously consoling to a sufferer who has lost his children to raiders.
- *Aesthetic-realist reading* (Alter, Newsom): The warhorse is presented not as morally exemplary but as biologically vivid. The chapter is not defending warfare; it is acknowledging that God has made creatures whose beauty includes their ferocity. Moral categories are not the operative categories here.
- *Steelmanned least-intuitive reading* (Ticciati, the reading that finds the warhorse theologically troubling): The warhorse is the shadow in the gallery. Read carefully, its celebration sits uneasily with the book's broader protest against violence. The text may be deliberately putting pressure on the reader: if God celebrates the warhorse, what does that say about the God Job has been addressing? This reading takes seriously the possibility that the chapter contains a productive discomfort that the text does not resolve.

*Where this leaves us:* Chapter 39 does not simply console. Its gallery of wild creatures disrupts the expectation that creation is ordered to human moral categories. The wildness, foolishness, ferocity, and rapacity of God's creatures are presented as wholly legitimate, even beautiful. A cosmos built this way is not one in which Job's question "Why do the righteous suffer?" admits of a neat answer, because the cosmos is not built for neatness.

The unresolved question: If God celebrates the ostrich's foolishness and the eagle's scavenging, is moral reasoning about creation a human imposition rather than a divine disclosure? And if so, what becomes of the entire enterprise of theodicy?

## The Thread

**Proverbs 30:18–19, 29–31**, Agur's two lists of the "things too wonderful for me" — the way of an eagle in the sky, the way of a serpent on a rock, the lion that retreats before none — runs parallel to Job 39 in content and tone. Both passages register the natural world with awe and refuse to reduce its creatures to utility. Reading them together suggests that Job 39 belongs to a broader Hebrew wisdom tradition that contemplated creaturely wildness as a category of divine disclosure.

**Psalm 104:10–23**, the great creation hymn, celebrates wild goats, coneys, young lions, sea monsters, and the restless cycles of creation with a tone of settled praise. Psalm 104 and Job 39 describe a similar cosmos, but in different keys — the psalm affirms confidence that God has ordered it all well; Job 39 presses the same data to produce awe at what exceeds human understanding. The same cosmos can ground both praise and interrogation.

**Isaiah 43:20** ("The wild beasts will honor me, the jackals and the ostriches") picks up the Joban ostrich and places it within an eschatological vision: even the foolish, useless, cruel ostrich will eventually render honor. The verse suggests an inner-biblical trajectory in which the wild creatures of Job 39 are gathered up into the final restoration rather than excluded from it.

**Mark 1:13** ("He was with the wild beasts"): Jesus in the wilderness is described as being "with the wild beasts" — the phrase has puzzled commentators, but a canonical reading connects it to Job 39. The Messiah's temptation is not in a domesticated garden but in the wildness God Himself celebrates. Richard Bauckham's essay "Jesus and the Wild Animals (Mark 1:13): A Christological Image for an Ecological Age" develops this connection: Jesus reconciles humanity not with a pacified creation but with the wild creation God made and loves.
