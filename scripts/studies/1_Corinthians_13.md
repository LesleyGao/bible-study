# 1 Corinthians 13 — Going Deeper

## Historical World

Roman Corinth produced bronze. Pausanias (*Description of Greece* 2.3) writes of "Corinthian bronze," an alloy famous across the Mediterranean for its acoustic and visual qualities; Pliny the Elder (*Natural History* 34.6–8) describes the metallurgy. The city's Asklepieion produced votive bronzes and the temples were equipped with bronze cymbals and gongs used in cultic processions, particularly those of Cybele (the Magna Mater) and Dionysus. William Harris ("'Sounding Brass' and Hellenistic Technology," 1982) argued that Paul's image of "noisy gong or clanging cymbal" (*chalkos ēchōn ē kymbalon alalazon*, 13:1) is a specific reference to the percussion of pagan ecstatic cult — the *tympana* and *kymbala* that accompanied frenzied worship of Cybele in particular, attested at Corinth's Eleusinion-Demeter sanctuary on the Acrocorinth (Bookidis and Stroud excavation reports). Plutarch's *De Iside et Osiride* and his treatment of Cybele's cult describe the cymbal as the signature instrument of inspired but unintelligible religious noise. Paul's first sentence is a barbed comparison: charismatic speech without love is indistinguishable from pagan cult percussion.

The mirror image in 13:12 has equally specific local resonance. Corinth was a center of bronze mirror production; its mirrors were known across the empire (Sider, "1 Corinthians 13:12a: A Bronze Mirror at Corinth?" *Archaeology Today*). Bronze mirrors gave a real but imperfect reflection — better than nothing, distinctly inferior to what direct sight would later make possible. The Greek phrase *di' esoptrou en ainigmati* (literally "through a mirror in a riddle") is a deliberate juxtaposition of two senses of partial knowing: the visual partiality of bronze mirror reflection and the cognitive partiality of riddling speech.

The chapter's literary form is encomium — a praise speech in classical rhetoric, with attributes catalogued (*aretai*) and superlative claims advanced. Pseudo-Demetrius's letter forms and the rhetorical handbooks of Hermogenes and Theon (later, but reflecting earlier practice) describe encomium as a discrete genre, one Paul's hearers would have recognized. Margaret Mitchell (*Paul and the Rhetoric of Reconciliation*, 1991) and Joachim Conzelmann (Hermeneia, 1969, ET 1975) note that 13:4–7's piling up of negated and affirmed predicates of *agapē* matches the deliberate *amplificatio* of Hellenistic encomium. Paul is not improvising a poetic outburst; he is using a recognized literary form for argumentative effect within a deliberative letter.

The chapter is the rhetorical heart of the letter. Mitchell argues that the entire letter is deliberative rhetoric urging concord (*homonoia*); 13 is the moral fulcrum on which the appeal turns. The Corinthian factionalism (1:10–17), the Corinthian eating practices (8–10, 11), the Corinthian sexual problems (5–7), the Corinthian charismatic competition (12, 14) — all are diagnosed in 13 by their absence of *agapē*.

## Literature

Chapter 13 is a tightly crafted three-stanza composition embedded between two chapters about *charismata*. The first stanza (13:1–3) lists three hypothetical possessions — speech (tongues of men and angels), cognitive gifts (prophecy, mysteries, knowledge, faith), and self-sacrificial action (almsgiving, surrendering the body) — and three corresponding losses (becoming noise, becoming nothing, gaining nothing). The pattern uses *ean* ("if") with subjunctive, framing extreme cases. The second stanza (13:4–7) personifies *agapē* as the subject of fifteen verbs: seven affirmative ("is patient... is kind... rejoices with the truth... bears all things, believes all things, hopes all things, endures all things"), eight negative ("does not envy... boast... is not arrogant... rude... does not insist on its own way... is not irritable, resentful... does not rejoice at wrongdoing"). The third stanza (13:8–13) introduces eschatological time: love does not end, but the gifts that mediate present knowing will. It moves through three images of partial-to-perfect: child to adult, mirror to face, partial knowledge to being fully known.

The closing triad — faith, hope, love (*pistis, elpis, agapē*) — is one of Paul's signature formulations (1 Thessalonians 1:3; 5:8; Galatians 5:5–6; Romans 5:1–5; Colossians 1:4–5). The triad predates Paul's writing of this chapter and is a stable element of his theology; the unique claim of 13:13 is the ranking ("the greatest of these is love").

A textual note: 13:3 has a major variant. The traditional reading "if I deliver my body to be burned" (*kauthēsōmai*) appears in later manuscripts. P46 (c. 200), Sinaiticus, Vaticanus, Ephraemi, and the early Alexandrian witnesses read *kauchēsōmai* — "that I may boast." A single letter difference (eta vs chi) changes the verse from a reference to martyrdom to a reference to vainglory. NA28, UBS5, NRSV, and most modern critical texts adopt "boast"; ESV, KJV, and NIV preserve "burned" as the harder traditional reading. Bruce Metzger's *Textual Commentary* (1994) gives "boast" a B rating and notes that "burned" likely arose under the influence of later martyr ideology and may also reflect a scribal slip from a similar-sounding form. The exegetical implication is significant: if Paul wrote "boast," the chapter's first stanza is even more directly aimed at Corinthian self-display — including ostentatious almsgiving — than at hypothetical extreme acts.

## The Larger Story

**Jewish trajectory.** *Agapē* has no exact Hebrew equivalent. The Septuagint uses *agapaō* and the noun *agapēsis/agapē* to translate Hebrew *ahavah* (love) and, more importantly, frames divine *hesed* (covenant loyalty, steadfast love) within the same vocabulary range. The Shema (Deuteronomy 6:4–5, "you shall love the LORD your God with all your heart") and Leviticus 19:18 ("you shall love your neighbor as yourself") are the foundational texts. Jesus's identification of these as the great commandments (Mark 12:28–34) places his teaching squarely within first-century Jewish moral discussion; the closest parallel is Hillel's golden-rule formulation reported in *Babylonian Talmud Shabbat* 31a. Boyarin (*A Radical Jew*, 1994) reads Paul's love-ethic as standing within Jewish covenantal-loyalty tradition rather than departing from it: *agapē* in 13 is the Greek translation of the *hesed* that holds covenant communities together when zeal, knowledge, and even sacrifice are present but solidarity has failed.

The Wisdom literature furnishes important background. Wisdom's personification in Proverbs 8 and Sirach 24 — wisdom as a feminine figure with attributes — is the formal precursor to Paul's personification of love in 13:4–7. The Qumran literature uses *ahavah* / *agapē* as a community virtue; 1QS 1:9–10 and 5:25–6:1 prescribe brothers loving one another. Jewish liturgical texts from the late Second Temple period (the Amidah, the *Ahavah Rabbah* / "with great love" benediction) make divine love the foundation of Israel's covenant standing.

**Christian trajectory.** Patristic theology of *agapē* developed along three intersecting lines.

The first is Augustinian. Augustine's *De Doctrina Christiana* (book 1) frames all of Scripture as oriented toward love of God and neighbor. *De Trinitate* book 8 uses the love-relation (lover, beloved, the love between them) as a Trinitarian analogy for the inner life of God. *Confessions* 13 famously declares "my weight is my love" (*pondus meum amor meus*) — love as the gravitational force of the soul. Augustine takes 13 as a guide to the moral life and an account of the soul's eschatological destiny.

The second is Thomas Aquinas. *Summa Theologiae* II-II qq. 23–46 treats *caritas* (charity) as the form of all virtue. Charity, on Thomas's account, is friendship with God (*amicitia*) infused supernaturally by the Holy Spirit. The chapter's claim that love is "the greatest" (13:13) becomes the structural claim of Thomistic moral theology: charity orders all the other virtues toward their final end.

The third, and most contested in modern theology, is Anders Nygren's *Agape and Eros* (1930–36, ET 1953). Nygren argued that *agapē* (Christian love, descending, unmotivated, creative of value in its object) and *eros* (Greek love, ascending, desiring, valuing what attracts it) are theologically opposed. Christian love is a one-way movement from God toward the unworthy creature; *eros* is a self-interested ascent toward what is desirable. Nygren's thesis dominated mid-twentieth-century Protestant theology but produced a sustained Catholic reaction. Henri de Lubac (*The Discovery of God*, 1956), Joseph Ratzinger / Benedict XVI (*Deus Caritas Est*, 2005), and a long Catholic tradition argued that *agapē* and *eros* are not opposed but integrated — divine love includes the ascending desire of the creature drawn toward its source. Eastern Orthodoxy follows Maximus the Confessor (*Ambigua* and *Centuries on Charity*) in seeing *eros* as ascending desire properly directed becoming the medium of *agapē*'s descending movement; Vladimir Lossky and John Zizioulas develop this in modern Orthodox theology.

C. S. Lewis's *The Four Loves* (1960) — *storgē* (affection), *philia* (friendship), *eros* (romantic love), *agapē* (charity) — popularized a quadripartite analysis that mediates between Nygren and the Catholic synthesis: human loves are good but need the gift of *agapē* to be rightly ordered. Lewis's account has been more pastorally influential than any twentieth-century academic treatment.

A fourth, distinctly modern line is feminist and liberation theology's critique. Beverly Harrison ("The Power of Anger in the Work of Love," 1981) and Margaret Farley (*Just Love*, 2006) argue that the long Christian tradition of valorizing self-sacrificial *agapē* has functioned to keep women, the poor, and the oppressed in postures of accommodation. Harrison's question — whose body, whose self, gets sacrificed in the name of love? — is a serious critique of any reading of 13:5 ("does not insist on its own way") that presses self-effacement on those who already have no way to insist. Stanley Hauerwas (*The Peaceable Kingdom*, 1983) reads love as a virtue formed by community practices, resisting both Nygren's dichotomy and the abstraction that detaches love from concrete ecclesial life.

## Theological Intent

**Tension 1 — The cessationist exegesis of 13:8–12.** When Paul says "as for prophecies, they will pass away; as for tongues, they will cease; as for knowledge, it will pass away. For we know in part... but when the perfect comes (*to teleion*), the partial will pass away" (13:8–10), what is *to teleion*?

*Position A — Canon completion (classical cessationist).* Warfield (*Counterfeit Miracles*, 1918), R. L. Thomas (*Understanding Spiritual Gifts*, 1999), and John MacArthur (*Strange Fire*, 2013) read *to teleion* as the completion of the New Testament canon. Once the canonical revelation is closed, the partial revelatory gifts (prophecy, tongues, the word of knowledge) cease. The exegetical pivots are: *to teleion* is neuter and could plausibly refer to "the complete thing" (the canon), not a person; the chapter contrasts partial knowledge with mature knowledge in 13:11 (the child-to-adult image suggests historical maturation, not eschatological transformation); and the cessationist case for 1 Corinthians 12 lights up if 13:8–10 means canon-completion.

*Position B — The parousia (continuationist majority and most modern exegesis).* Calvin (commentary on 1 Corinthians 13), F. F. Bruce, Anthony Thiselton, Gordon Fee, and the dominant modern exegetical tradition read *to teleion* as the parousia — Christ's return and the consummation of all things. The decisive evidence is 13:12: "now we see through a mirror in a riddle, but then face to face; now I know in part, then I shall know fully, even as I have been fully known." "Face to face" knowledge that mirrors how we are known by God is not provided by canon-completion. It is provided only in the eschaton. The child-to-adult image is therefore not historical maturation of the church but the transition between the present age and the age to come.

*Position C — Christ himself.* Some exegetes (Calvin, in passing; some patristic readings) take *to teleion* as the perfection of Christ, the perfection of love itself, or the perfection of the saints in glory — variations on Position B that locate the perfection in the person of Christ rather than in the parousia event. The practical effect overlaps with B.

The least intuitive position to most contemporary readers is the cessationist reading. Steelmanned: Warfield's case is not exegetical alone but also historical and theological. The New Testament treats apostles and prophets as foundational (Ephesians 2:20). Foundations are laid once. The post-apostolic church does not, in fact, possess apostles in the strict sense. If apostleship in that strict sense ceased, the analogy to other foundational gifts is at least plausible. The cessationist reading of 13:10 may strain the immediate context of 13:12, but it is supported by a broader ecclesiological argument the continuationist must answer separately. The strongest cessationist case concedes that the parousia reading is the more natural exegesis of 13:12 alone, but argues that the broader theological frame requires reading 13:8–10 in a way 13:12 does not exclusively dictate.

**Tension 2 — Agape and eros.** Is Christian love categorically opposed to desire, or does it include and transform desire?

*Position A — Nygren's opposition.* *Agapē* descends; *eros* ascends. Christian love is wholly other-regarding, creative of value, unmotivated by the worth of its object. *Eros* is acquisitive and self-interested. The two cannot be synthesized without distorting *agapē*.

*Position B — Catholic synthesis.* Benedict XVI in *Deus Caritas Est* (2005) explicitly rejects Nygren's opposition. Divine love, in the biblical witness, includes the passionate desire of God for his people (Hosea, the Song of Songs) and the ascending love of the creature drawn back toward God. *Agapē* and *eros* are two dimensions of one love.

*Position C — Eastern Orthodox integration via Maximus.* Maximus the Confessor in the *Centuries on Charity* treats *eros* as the natural movement of the soul toward what it desires; properly directed (toward God), this *eros* becomes the medium of *agapē*; misdirected, it becomes idolatrous. The integration is structural to the spiritual life.

*Position D — Feminist critique.* Harrison and Farley accept neither opposition nor synthesis as sufficient. The tradition of valorizing *agapē* as self-sacrifice has historically functioned to demand sacrifice from those already disempowered. Just love requires that the self of the lover be respected as well as the self of the beloved; "love does not insist on its own way" cannot be a counsel to those who have never had a way to insist on.

*Position E — Hauerwasian virtue ethics.* Stanley Hauerwas treats love as a virtue formed by ecclesial practices over time. The categorical disputes between *agapē* and *eros* abstract from the lived church communities in which Christians actually learn to love. The chapter's catalogue of virtues in 13:4–7 is a description of formed character, not metaphysical analysis.

Steelman the feminist critique. Paul's "love does not insist on its own way" is an absolutely correct correction of Corinthian elite men using their freedom to dominate. But the same sentence preached to the abused, the enslaved, the systematically silenced is dangerous in a way the original context was not. The interpretive question is whether 13's account of love is a description of mutuality between full agents or a counsel of submission to existing arrangements. Paul's broader letter (the inverted body image of 12:22–24, the Eucharistic equality of 11:33) suggests the former, but the textual cherry-picking of an authoritarian church can convert it to the latter. Reading 13 well requires reading it with the rest of the letter and with attention to who is using it on whom.

## Where this leaves us

Chapter 13 is not a wedding-poem detour. It is the moral logic that makes chapter 12's body-image and chapter 14's prophecy-priority intelligible. The Corinthian charismatic competition is finally addressed not by ranking gifts but by relativizing all of them against love. Without love, a tongue is a Corinthian bronze gong; with love, the same tongue can build up the assembly. The eschatological frame in 13:8–13 says something further: gifts are temporary instruments because they belong to a present in which we know in part. Love is not temporary because it belongs to the future as well as the present — to the face-to-face knowing that mirrors how we are already known by God. Faith and hope abide now; in the eschaton, faith becomes sight and hope becomes possession; love alone passes through the transition unaltered.

## One unresolved question

If "love bears all things, believes all things, hopes all things, endures all things" (13:7), where is the line between costly love and complicity in injustice? The verse cannot mean "love tolerates everything" without contradicting 13:6 ("does not rejoice at wrongdoing"), and yet the chapter does not specify when bearing becomes enabling. The boundary between Christian patience and the abuse of patience is not drawn in the text and has not been drawn convincingly by the tradition.

## The Thread

The Corinthians had tongues, prophecy, knowledge, almsgiving — every spiritual asset a wealthy Greco-Roman city could marshal — and they were tearing the body apart. Paul does not list more gifts to acquire. He gives them an encomium for the one virtue that holds everything else together, and he tells them that all their pneumatic display, set in the bronze workshop of their own city, sounds like Cybele's cymbals if love is missing. Faith, hope, love. The greatest is love, because the other two will end when sight begins. Love is what we will be doing forever.
