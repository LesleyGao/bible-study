# 2 Chronicles 6 — Going Deeper

## Lens 1: The Historical World

Chapter 6 contains Solomon's temple-dedication prayer — the longest and theologically densest royal prayer in the Hebrew Bible, and the rhetorical-theological centerpiece of the entire Chronicler's work. The parallel is 1 Kings 8:14–61, but the Chronicler makes significant additions, omissions, and adjustments. The most consequential adjustment comes at the prayer's close (verses 41–42), where the Chronicler replaces Kings's exodus-based conclusion with verses drawn from Psalm 132 — invoking the Davidic covenant. This single change reframes the entire prayer.

The chapter opens with Solomon's brief blessing-formula in response to the cloud-glory of chapter 5. Verses 1–2: *The LORD has said that he would dwell in thick darkness. But I have built you an exalted house, a place for you to dwell in forever* (Hebrew *vahtikhon le-shivtekha olamim*, וְהָכוֹן לְשִׁבְתְּךָ עוֹלָמִים). The opening references Yahweh's *araphel* (עֲרָפֶל, "thick darkness, deep cloud") — the same word used at Sinai (Exodus 20:21, Deuteronomy 4:11). The cloud that has just filled the temple is the *Sinai-presence* now resident in Jerusalem.

Verses 3–11 contain Solomon's blessing of the assembly, which is also a theological summary. *Blessed be the LORD, the God of Israel, who with his hand has fulfilled what he promised with his mouth to David my father*. The pattern *peh* (mouth) and *yad* (hand) — Yahweh promises with mouth and fulfills with hand — runs through the prayer. Verses 5–6 articulate the Chronicler's distinctive double-election theology: *I have chosen Jerusalem... and I have chosen David*. The temple is anchored in two divine choices, geographic and dynastic. Sara Japhet, in her *Old Testament Library* commentary, treats this as the Chronicler's foundational political theology: Jerusalem and the Davidic line are inseparably elected together. This double-election will become the theological framework for everything that follows in 2 Chronicles.

Verses 7–9 narrate Yahweh's earlier word to David: *it was in the heart of David... but the LORD said... it is not you who shall build the house, but your son*. The Chronicler is summarizing 1 Chronicles 17 (the Davidic covenant). Solomon presents himself as the fulfillment of the promise: *I have risen in the place of David my father and sit on the throne of Israel... and I have built the house for the name of the LORD* (verse 10).

Verses 12–13 contain a Chronicler-only addition that has had outsized theological-liturgical influence. Solomon stands before the altar, *in the presence of all the assembly of Israel, and spread out his hands* (verse 12). Then verse 13 — entirely missing from 1 Kings 8 — describes the *bronze platform* (Hebrew *kiyor nechoshet*, כִּיּוֹר נְחֹשֶׁת — though *kiyor* normally means "basin," here clearly meaning "platform" or "scaffold"): five cubits long, five cubits wide, three cubits high, set in the court. *He stood on it. Then he knelt on his knees in the presence of all the assembly of Israel, and spread out his hands toward heaven*. Hugh Williamson and Gary Knoppers both note the theological motivation. The Chronicler is harmonizing Solomon's *standing prayer-posture* in 1 Kings 8:22 with his later *kneeling* in 1 Kings 8:54 — by introducing the platform, the Chronicler shows Solomon publicly visible (standing on a raised platform) yet humbly kneeling before the LORD. The platform is an architectural detail that resolves a textual tension. It also creates a striking image: the king visibly elevated above the assembly but kneeling within his own elevation. Authority and humility in the same posture.

Verses 14–17 contain the prayer's first major section: praise of Yahweh's incomparability and request that the Davidic promise be confirmed. Hebrew *ein-kamokha Elohim ba-shamayim u-va-aretz* (אֵין־כָּמוֹךָ אֱלֹהִים בַּשָּׁמַיִם וּבָאָרֶץ, "there is no God like you, in heaven or on earth"). The prayer's conditional Davidic-promise — *if only your sons pay close attention to their way, to walk in my law as you have walked before me* (verse 16) — is the Chronicler's framing of the dynasty as conditioned on faithfulness. (1 Kings 8:25 has nearly identical wording.)

Verse 18 is the prayer's theological pivot: *But will God indeed dwell with man on the earth? Behold, heaven and the highest heaven cannot contain you, how much less this house that I have built!* Hebrew *ki ha-umnam yeshev Elohim et-ha-adam al-ha-aretz* (כִּי הַאֻמְנָם יֵשֵׁב אֱלֹהִים אֶת־הָאָדָם עַל־הָאָרֶץ). The interrogative *ha-umnam* — "indeed? truly?" — frames an astonished question. Solomon, having just built a temple of unparalleled magnificence, asks whether God can really dwell with humanity at all. The answer the prayer assumes is: *not in any literal containment, but in the meeting of prayer*. The temple is a *place of meeting*, not a residence. This is the deepest theological insight of the prayer.

Verses 19–21 establish the prayer's recurring formula: *that your eyes may be open day and night toward this house... listen to the prayer that your servant offers toward this place*. The Hebrew verb *shama* (שָׁמַע, "to hear") and the noun *tefillah* (תְּפִלָּה, "prayer") with its companion *techinnah* (תְּחִנָּה, "plea, supplication") run through the prayer in a refrain-pattern. Solomon is asking Yahweh to *hear from heaven* — to receive prayers offered *toward this place*. The temple is the directional focus of prayer, not the divine residence.

Verses 22–39 contain Solomon's seven petitions — seven scenarios in which Israel's prayer might be needed and Yahweh's hearing is requested. Each petition follows the same form: *if* X happens *and* Israel prays *toward this place*, *then* Yahweh *hear from heaven and forgive/act*.

Petition 1 (verses 22–23): oath-taking before the altar in cases of dispute. The temple as a place of judicial oath.

Petition 2 (verses 24–25): defeat by enemies because of sin. *Hear from heaven and forgive the sin of your people Israel and bring them again to the land*.

Petition 3 (verses 26–27): drought. *When heaven is shut up and there is no rain... if they pray toward this place... grant rain upon your land*.

Petition 4 (verses 28–31): famine, pestilence, blight, mildew, locust, caterpillar, enemy siege. *Whatever plague, whatever sickness... each knowing his own affliction*. The phrase *each knowing his own affliction* — Hebrew *asher yedu ish nig'o u-makhovo* (אֲשֶׁר יֵדְעוּ אִישׁ נִגְעוֹ וּמַכְאֹבוֹ) — particularizes the prayer. Each individual brings his own suffering.

Petition 5 (verses 32–33): the foreigner. *When a foreigner, who is not of your people Israel, comes from a far country for the sake of your great name and your mighty hand and your outstretched arm, when he comes and prays toward this house, hear from heaven... and do according to all for which the foreigner calls to you, in order that all the peoples of the earth may know your name*. Hebrew *ve-gam el-ha-nokhri asher lo me-amkha Yisrael hu* (וְגַם אֶל־הַנָּכְרִי אֲשֶׁר לֹא מֵעַמְּךָ יִשְׂרָאֵל הוּא). This is the prayer's most universalist moment. Solomon is asking Yahweh to hear *non-Israelites* who pray toward the temple. Sara Japhet treats this as one of the Chronicler's most remarkable theological inclusions.

Petition 6 (verses 34–35): going to battle. *Hear from heaven their prayer and their plea, and maintain their cause*.

Petition 7 (verses 36–39): exile. *If they sin against you — for there is no one who does not sin — and you are angry with them and give them to an enemy, so that they are carried away captive to a land far or near, yet if they turn their heart... if they repent with all their heart and with all their soul... and pray toward their land... and the house that I have built for your name, then hear from heaven your dwelling place their prayer and their pleas, and maintain their cause and forgive your people who have sinned against you*.

The exilic petition is the prayer's longest and most theologically loaded. Hebrew *ki ein adam asher lo yecheta* (כִּי אֵין אָדָם אֲשֶׁר לֹא יֶחֱטָא, "for there is no one who does not sin"). The exilic frame is unmistakable. The Chronicler is writing for a community that has *just lived through* exactly this scenario. The prayer anticipates the very reality the post-exilic readers had experienced. Mark Boda, Knoppers, and Williamson all recognize that the exilic petition functions as the prayer's theological-pastoral center for the Chronicler's audience: the prayer Solomon prayed at the dedication is the prayer the post-exilic community needed to know had been prayed in advance.

Verses 41–42 contain the Chronicler's most consequential adjustment of the Kings parallel. 1 Kings 8:50–53 closes with a long exodus-based appeal: *Yahweh chose Israel out of all the peoples of the earth when he brought our ancestors out of Egypt*. The Chronicler replaces this with a quotation from Psalm 132:8–10: *And now arise, O LORD God, and go to your resting place, you and the ark of your might. Let your priests, O LORD God, be clothed with salvation, and let your saints rejoice in your goodness. O LORD God, do not turn away the face of your anointed one! Remember your steadfast love for David your servant*. Hebrew *al tashev penei meshikhekha zokhrah le-chasdei David avdekha* (אַל־תָּשֵׁב פְּנֵי מְשִׁיחֶךָ זָכְרָה לְחַסְדֵי דָוִיד עַבְדֶּךָ).

The substitution is deeply significant. Where Kings closes with the exodus-foundation of Israel, the Chronicler closes with the Davidic covenant. The prayer's theological weight is shifted from Mosaic-deliverance to Davidic-promise. Williamson, Klein, and Knoppers all treat this as a deliberate theological move: the Chronicler is reframing the temple-prayer as fundamentally about the Davidic dynasty's continuation, with the *anointed one* (Hebrew *mashiach*, מָשִׁיחַ) as the prayer's closing concern. The post-exilic community, living without a Davidic king, is being told that the prayer prayed at the temple's dedication includes a petition for the anointed-one's vindication that has not yet been answered.

## Lens 2: The Literature

The chapter is structured as four movements: blessing of the assembly (verses 3–11), prayer of incomparability and Davidic promise (verses 12–17), theological pivot on divine dwelling (verses 18–21), seven petitions (verses 22–39), and Davidic-covenant closing (verses 40–42).

The seven-petition structure is the prayer's most recognizable literary feature. The number seven is the standard biblical completeness-number; the seven scenarios cover the major categories of Israel's possible distress: judicial, military-defeat, drought, agricultural-pestilential, foreigner, military-aggression, exilic. The form *if X, hear from heaven and Y* is repeated with variations seven times. Each petition is theologically self-contained but cumulative — the prayer builds toward the climactic exilic petition (verses 36–39), which is the longest and most heart-rending.

The Hebrew literary features include the recurring *peh* and *yad* (mouth and hand) imagery, the *shama* and *tefillah* refrain, the seven-fold petition pattern, and the prayer's tightly structured rhetoric of address. Solomon addresses Yahweh by name, recalls the covenant, articulates the conditional, and closes with the Davidic appeal. The prayer is composed in elevated covenantal Hebrew, drawing on Deuteronomic vocabulary (covenant-keeping, walking-in-the-law, *shamor mishmar*) and Davidic-royal vocabulary (anointed-one, throne, dynasty).

The platform-detail of verse 13 (Chronicler-only) is a small architectural-narrative element with disproportionate theological weight. Solomon visible to all, raised above the people, kneeling. The image will inform every subsequent Christian and Jewish liturgical theology of public prayer-leadership.

## Lens 3: The Larger Story

The Jewish reception of Solomon's temple-dedication prayer is one of the deepest streams in the entire rabbinic-liturgical tradition. The Talmud (*Berakhot* 7a–8a, 26b, 29a–32a) treats the prayer as the foundation of the entire theology of *tefillah* — directional prayer, *kavvanah* (intentionality), and the principle that prayer offered toward the temple is heard. The daily Amidah, prayed three times daily, is grounded in the principle Solomon articulates: prayer offered with one's heart turned toward the temple-mount is received by Yahweh. After the temple's destruction, the synagogue inherits this directional principle: synagogues are built so the congregation prays toward Jerusalem.

Rashi, Radak, and Abarbanel all emphasize the *foreigner-petition* (verses 32–33) as a remarkable inclusion. Rashi: even a foreigner who comes for the sake of Yahweh's name is heard. Radak: the temple's universalist scope is implicit in the prayer's structure. Abarbanel, writing in the Iberian Jewish context where conversion-to-Judaism was forbidden under Christian rule, treats the foreigner-petition as a charter for Yahweh's hearing of all who turn toward him, regardless of formal status.

The exilic petition (verses 36–39) is liturgically central in Jewish tradition. The Yom Kippur service includes prayers that explicitly draw on its language. The *Avinu Malkenu* prayer ("Our Father, Our King") echoes its repentance-and-forgiveness pattern. The post-temple Jewish community read this prayer as Solomon's prayer-on-behalf-of-the-future — including their own future, the exile they were living through.

Modern Jewish scholarship — Sara Japhet, Israel Knohl, Ehud Ben Zvi, Isaac Kalimi — has emphasized how the Chronicler's adjustments make the prayer theologically tighter and more pastorally usable for the post-exilic community. Japhet treats the closing Psalm 132 quotation as the Chronicler's most decisive theological move in the entire book.

The Christian reception is equally deep. Jerome's Vulgate translation of the prayer became the basis for the entire medieval Latin theological-liturgical inheritance. The petition-form (*if X happens, then Y*) shaped Christian prayer-theology, particularly the litany-tradition. Cassiodorus's *Expositio Psalmorum* repeatedly references Solomon's prayer as the model for liturgical petition. Augustine, in *Confessions* and the *City of God*, draws on the prayer's central insight — *will God indeed dwell with man on the earth?* — for his theology of the inner God who is present everywhere yet meets us specifically in worship.

Aquinas's *Summa Theologiae* (II-II q. 83) on prayer takes Solomon's prayer as the paradigm of public liturgical petition. Calvin's 1561 commentary on 2 Chronicles devotes extensive attention to the prayer, treating it as the foundation for Reformed temple-theology and the principle that prayer offered through the mediator (the king-priest figure of Solomon, foreshadowing Christ) is heard by God. Calvin's treatment of the foreigner-petition (verses 32–33) emphasizes the universal scope of God's hearing — a key Reformed doctrine.

The temple's architectural foreshadowing of Christ runs through the New Testament. Jesus's statement *destroy this temple, and in three days I will raise it up* (John 2:19) — with the evangelist's commentary *but he was speaking about the temple of his body* (John 2:21) — is the most direct christological appropriation. Hebrews 9–10 develops the temple-Christ typology systematically, treating Christ as the high priest who enters the heavenly sanctuary with his own blood. The New Testament's appropriation of the temple-prayer-tradition is mediated through this Christ-as-temple theology: Christians pray *in Christ* the way Israel prayed *toward the temple*.

The foreigner-petition (verses 32–33) has had a particularly significant Christian afterlife as a charter for Christian mission and inclusion. The early Christian recognition that Gentiles could pray to Israel's God (Acts 10's Cornelius narrative, Acts 15's Jerusalem council, Paul's Letter to the Romans on Gentile inclusion) draws implicitly on this petition's logic. N. T. Wright, in *The New Testament and the People of God* and *Jesus and the Victory of God*, has emphasized how the foreigner-petition prepares the theological ground for the Gentile-inclusive ekklesia. Christopher Wright, in *The Mission of God*, treats the petition as one of the Old Testament's clearest charters for missionary theology.

The exilic petition (verses 36–39) has had its own Christian afterlife. Daniel 9 (the great penitential prayer) is structured on its model. Nehemiah 9 follows the same pattern. The New Testament's penitential-prayer tradition (1 John 1:9, James 5:16) inherits the same theological grammar. Christian liturgical-confession traditions trace their structure to this exilic petition: turn-the-heart, repent-with-all-your-heart-and-soul, pray-toward-the-true-temple, and Yahweh hears.

## Lens 4: The Theological Intent

The Chronicler's agenda in chapter 6 is to render Solomon's temple-dedication prayer as the theological-liturgical foundation for the entire sweep of 2 Chronicles, with the seven petitions providing the prayer-grammar that subsequent kings will or will not follow, and the closing Davidic appeal preparing the post-exilic community for their own situation. Three tensions deserve careful naming.

**The first tension: the temple-prayer theology versus modern critiques of sacred-place religion.** Solomon's prayer rests on the conviction that the temple is the directional focus of prayer — the place toward which prayers are offered, the locus of divine hearing. Modern sensibilities often resist this kind of locative-theology, preferring a God who is equally accessible everywhere.

**Position 1: Locative-temple theology (Catholic, Orthodox, Reformed varieties).** The temple-prayer logic is preserved in Christian theology through different mediations. Catholic eucharistic theology takes the consecrated altar and the sanctuary as the place where Christ's body is sacramentally present. Eastern Orthodox theology takes the church-building as a microcosmic temple where the *theophany* of liturgy occurs. Reformed theology, while resisting strong locative claims, still affirms that *gathered worship* is the privileged site of God's meeting his people. All three traditions inherit the Chronicler's logic — the locative dimension matters — even when the specific location shifts.

**Position 2: Non-locative spiritual reading.** John 4:21–24 (*the hour is coming when neither on this mountain nor in Jerusalem will you worship the Father... God is spirit, and those who worship him must worship in spirit and truth*) is the Christian charter for non-locative worship. The temple-prayer logic, in this reading, was provisional. Christ is the new temple, but his being-the-temple means that worship-location no longer matters. Every place is now the temple-mount. Some Reformed and most evangelical-charismatic traditions take this view in some form.

**Position 3: Christological-temple reading.** The temple-prayer logic is fulfilled in Christ. Prayer is now offered *in Christ* — through him, with him, in him — the way Israel's prayer was offered *toward the temple*. The locative dimension is preserved but transferred from a building to a person. Christian prayer is therefore *christo-locative*: directed toward Christ as the place of divine meeting. Hebrews 4:14–16 and 10:19–22 articulate this view. N. T. Wright, Catholic eucharistic theology, and Eastern Orthodox theology all converge here.

**Steelman of Position 3.** The strongest case is that the Chronicler's prayer is preserved in its structural logic by being *transposed* into Christ. The temple was the directional focus of prayer because it was the place where Yahweh's name dwelt and where Yahweh's presence met his people. In Christian theology, Christ is the place where God's name dwells (Matthew 1:21–23 — *Immanuel, God with us*) and where God's presence meets humanity (John 14:9 — *whoever has seen me has seen the Father*). Prayer offered *in Christ's name* (John 14:13–14, 16:23–24) is the New Testament's transposition of prayer offered *toward the temple*. The locative dimension is not abolished; it is christologically realized. This reading honors both the Chronicler's logic (prayer needs a focus) and Christ's claim (he is the new temple).

**Where this leaves us on this tension.** The temple-prayer theology is not abolished by Christ; it is fulfilled. Christian prayer has a directional focus — Christ — that operates structurally as the temple operated for Israel. The Chronicler's prayer-grammar is therefore not obsolete but transformed. Christian readers who recognize this can pray the prayer of 2 Chronicles 6 with full integrity, substituting Christ for the temple as the place of divine meeting. Jewish readers continue to pray toward the temple-mount, holding the locative tradition in its original form. Both inherit the Chronicler's central insight: prayer needs a place, even when the place is a person.

**The second tension: the foreigner-petition and universalist scope.** Verses 32–33 ask Yahweh to hear the prayers of foreigners who come from a far country. This is one of the Old Testament's most universalist moments, and it has been read in two distinct ways.

**Position 1: Christian-mission reading.** The foreigner-petition is the Old Testament's charter for the inclusion of the nations in God's saving purpose. The petition anticipates the Great Commission (Matthew 28:18–20) and the church's mission to the Gentiles (Acts, Pauline epistles). It is the seed of New Testament universalism. Christopher Wright, John Goldingay, and most evangelical missiologists take this view.

**Position 2: Jewish-particularist-with-universal-blessing reading.** The petition does not advocate for foreign conversion to Israel; it asks Yahweh to *hear* foreigners who *recognize* his name. This is a softer universalism — Israel remains the elect people, but Yahweh's hearing extends to those outside. Jon Levenson and many Jewish scholars take this view, emphasizing that the petition does not erase Israel's particular election but extends Yahweh's responsiveness beyond it.

**Position 3: Synthesis reading.** Both readings honor different dimensions of the petition. The Christian-mission reading captures its forward-looking trajectory; the Jewish-particularist reading captures its immediate post-exilic context. The petition is both — a Yehud-immediate hope that even foreign worshipers (Persians, neighbors, traders) might recognize Yahweh, and a forward-looking trajectory that the early church would extend into full Gentile inclusion. N. T. Wright takes roughly this view in *The Mission of God's People*.

**Steelman of Position 2.** The strongest case for the Jewish-particularist reading is that the petition is set within a prayer offered for *Israel* — the seven petitions cover Israel's possible distresses, with the foreigner-petition as a single inclusion. The petition does not erase the distinction between Israel and the nations; it asks Yahweh to be *responsive* to those who turn toward his name. The post-exilic community would have understood this as a recognition that Yahweh's lordship extends beyond Israel without thereby dissolving Israel's particular election. The Christian-mission reading risks reading the petition as more programmatic than the text supports.

**Where this leaves us.** The petition is genuinely universalist in scope but particularist in framing. It asks Yahweh to hear foreigners; it does not ask Yahweh to make them Israelites. The Christian church, in extending this principle to its full mission-theology, has rightly recognized the trajectory while sometimes flattening the distinction. The Jewish liturgical tradition, in maintaining the temple-mount focus while including non-Jewish worshipers in its theology of Yahweh's hearing, has held both dimensions together. Both inheritances are theologically legitimate.

**The third tension: the exilic petition and the Chronicler's structural-eschatology.** Verses 36–39 anticipate the exact scenario the post-exilic readers had just lived through. The Chronicler is writing for an audience that has experienced the exile; he places in Solomon's mouth, at the temple's dedication, a prayer that anticipates their experience. This is theologically and literarily extraordinary.

**Position 1: Predictive-prophecy reading.** Solomon, by divine inspiration, prophesied the exile. The prayer is a genuine prediction. Conservative-traditional readings take this view.

**Position 2: Compositional-shaping reading.** The Chronicler, writing after the exile, composes Solomon's prayer to include the exilic petition as a way of framing the post-exilic experience as already-anticipated and already-prayed-for. The prayer is theologically constructed to address the Chronicler's audience. Critical scholars (Williamson, Knoppers, Klein) take this view.

**Position 3: Both-and reading.** The historical Solomon may well have prayed something like this prayer (Kings preserves a parallel that lacks only minor adjustments). The Chronicler shapes the prayer for his audience but does not invent it from whole cloth. The exilic petition reflects Israel's covenantal-conditional theology (Deuteronomy 28–30 already anticipates exile-and-return), so the petition has deep roots in Israel's theological tradition even if its specific framing is post-exilic.

**Where this leaves us on this tension.** The exilic petition functions for the post-exilic community as a recognition that what they have lived through was always within the prayer-life of Israel. Their suffering was prayed-for in advance. Their repentance was prepared-for in the dedication-liturgy. The Chronicler's gift to his community is the assurance that the prayer Solomon prayed at the temple's dedication was *also* the prayer they needed to pray, and that Yahweh's hearing extends through the centuries to include them. Whether this reflects predictive prophecy, theological composition, or both, the pastoral effect is the same: the post-exilic community is invited to read Solomon's prayer as their own prayer, prayed in advance.

**Where this leaves us.** The chapter is the Chronicler's theological-liturgical center of gravity. Every subsequent narrative in 2 Chronicles will be measured against the prayer's framework: when kings pray rightly, the temple-prayer logic is activated; when they fail, the prayer's conditional-Davidic-promise comes into question. The post-exilic community inherits this prayer as the theological grammar for their continued life with Yahweh, and the closing Davidic appeal (verses 41–42) keeps the messianic hope alive even when no Davidic king sits on the throne.

**Closing question.** Solomon prayed for the foreigner. He prayed for the exile. He prayed for the prayer-of-each-person-knowing-his-own-affliction. The temple's most exalted moment was a prayer that included people who would not visit Jerusalem in their lifetime, situations the audience would not face for centuries, and individual sufferings the king himself would never know. What does it mean to pray a prayer whose answers will be received by people you will never meet? The Chronicler's post-exilic readers were the answer to one of Solomon's petitions — the exilic one — and they did not know it until they read this chapter. Whose prayer are you currently the answer to, without knowing it? And whose prayer are you praying, the answer to which will arrive long after you?

## The Thread

Solomon stands on a bronze platform in the court of the temple, before all the assembly of Israel, and kneels with his hands spread toward heaven. He blesses Yahweh for fulfilling the Davidic promise, names the impossibility of God dwelling on earth, and asks anyway: that Yahweh's eyes may be open day and night toward this house. He prays seven petitions — for oath-takers, for the defeated, for the drought-stricken, for the famine-afflicted, for the foreigner, for the warrior, for the exile. He closes with words from Psalm 132: *do not turn away the face of your anointed one; remember your steadfast love for David your servant*. The Chronicler has replaced Kings's exodus-conclusion with a Davidic-messianic appeal, and his post-exilic readers — living without a king, with a modest second temple, in a Persian-period Yehud — are being told that the prayer prayed at the temple's dedication has not yet received its full answer, and that the *anointed one* whose face Yahweh is asked not to turn away is still on the way. The temple is the place toward which prayer is offered; the prayer is the form in which God meets his people; the Davidic hope is the prayer's unfinished business; and the seven petitions are the framework for every subsequent crisis in 2 Chronicles and beyond.
