# Deuteronomy 31: Be Strong and Courageous

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

Deuteronomy — Hebrew *Dĕbārîm*, "words" — closes with a dense succession narrative in chapters 31-34: Joshua's commissioning, the writing of the Torah, the Song of Moses (ch. 32), the Blessing of Moses (ch. 33), and the death of Moses (ch. 34). Chapter 31 is the hinge. It brackets the covenantal sermon that has occupied chapters 4-30 and turns the book toward the transition from Moses to Joshua, from spoken instruction to written Torah, from the plains of Moab to the Jordan crossing. Jeffrey Tigay (JPS Torah Commentary) notes that the chapter reads as a carefully constructed bridge: three parallel commissionings of Joshua (vv. 3, 7-8, 23), two depositions of the written law (vv. 9, 24-26), and a prophetic pre-announcement of the song that will occupy chapter 32.

The political and material setting is still the plains of Moab, east of the Jordan, Moses at 120 years — the symbolic sum of three generations of forty years (Egypt, Midian/Sinai, wilderness), also the Mesopotamian "full span" of a patriarch. Moses's declaration "I am no longer able to go out and come in" (v. 2) is standard ancient Near Eastern idiom for military leadership — the king or commander "going out and coming in" before the troops (compare 1 Sam 18:13-16 on David; 1 Kgs 3:7 on Solomon). The phrase signals abdication of command, not physical senescence; Deut 34:7 famously insists that "his eye was not dim, nor his vigor abated."

The critical dating question for Deut 31 is bound up with the broader Deuteronomistic History (DtrH) hypothesis. Martin Noth (Überlieferungsgeschichtliche Studien, 1943) argued that Deuteronomy, through 2 Kings, formed a unified exilic-era historiographical work; Deut 31 belongs to his framework of introductions and conclusions that stitch the long history together. The three-fold charge to Joshua ("Be strong and courageous… the LORD is with you… he will not leave you or forsake you," vv. 6, 7-8, 23) is echoed in almost identical language at Joshua 1:5-9, a deliberate narrative handoff that Noth and his successors — Frank Moore Cross (Canaanite Myth and Hebrew Epic), Richard Nelson (OTL Deuteronomy), Thomas Römer (The So-Called Deuteronomistic History) — treat as a compositional seam. Weinfeld (AB) sees the septennial-reading prescription in vv. 10-13 as a late-monarchic or exilic innovation reflecting actual liturgical practice.

Conservative readers — Daniel Block (NIVAC), Peter Craigie (NICOT), McConville (Apollos) — counter that the transition language is inherent to the historical moment of Moses's death and fits a Mosaic-era compositional core, while granting that the final canonical shape may have been finished later. Dennis Olson's monograph *Deuteronomy and the Death of Moses* (1994) bridges the debate: he reads chapters 31-34 as a theological meditation on the death of Moses as a paradigm for every generation's transition, whether composed early or late.

Archaeologically, the "tent of meeting" (*ʾōhel môʿēd*, v. 14) and the "ark of the covenant" (*ʾărôn bĕrît-YHWH*, v. 9, 25) connect to the tabernacle traditions of Exodus-Numbers; the practice of depositing treaty or law documents "beside" or "before" a cult object has Hittite, Assyrian, and Egyptian parallels. Hittite treaties of Muršili II and Suppiluliuma specify that copies be deposited before the images of the gods and read aloud periodically. Neo-Assyrian practice (cf. the Esarhaddon succession treaty tablets found at Tell Tayinat and Nimrud) is similar: treaty tablets are placed in temples and read publicly. Moshe Weinfeld catalogued these parallels extensively.

Five Hebrew terms anchor the chapter. First, *ḥăzaq wĕʾĕmāṣ* ("be strong and be courageous," vv. 6, 7, 23) — a paired imperative that becomes Joshua's charter (Josh 1:6, 7, 9, 18) and resonates through the Deuteronomistic History. Second, *tôrâ* ("law, instruction," vv. 9, 11, 12, 24, 26) — here specifically "this Torah," meaning the written book Moses has composed. Third, *haqhēl* ("assemble," v. 12) — the septennial convocation, which gives its name to the later rabbinic *Hakhel* ceremony (Mishnah Sotah 7.8, Maimonides Hilchot Ḥagigah 3). Fourth, *ʿēd* ("witness," vv. 19, 21, 26) — the Torah and the Song both function as legal witnesses. Fifth, *šîrâ* ("song," vv. 19, 21, 22, 30) — the specific genre of the coming Deut 32, distinct from *tôrâ*.

## Lens 2 — The Literature

Deuteronomy 31 is a succession narrative interwoven with two depositary ceremonies. Its structure is pedagogically elegant: Moses addresses the people (vv. 1-6), then Joshua (vv. 7-8), then writes and deposits the Torah (vv. 9-13); YHWH speaks at the tent of meeting, commissioning Joshua directly and pre-announcing the Song (vv. 14-23); Moses finishes writing and deposits the Torah beside the ark, with a final dark prophecy (vv. 24-29); and Moses begins to speak the Song (v. 30), a cliffhanger into chapter 32.

The genre is *succession narrative* — a well-attested ancient Near Eastern form. The Egyptian Instruction of Amenemope, the Akkadian Advice to a Prince, and later the farewell addresses of the patriarchs (Gen 49) and of David (1 Kgs 2) share the structure: a dying or retiring leader charges a successor, issues instructions, and predicts future events. What is distinctive in Deut 31 is the triangulation: Moses commissions Joshua, and YHWH separately commissions Joshua directly (v. 23), so that the succession is both human and divine. Dennis Olson reads this doubling as theologically weighted — Joshua's authority is derivative (from Moses) and direct (from YHWH), but never replaces the unique prophetic station that Moses occupied (a point made explicit in Deut 34:10).

Literary devices include: structured repetition (the threefold "be strong and courageous," the twofold deposition, the twofold prediction of apostasy in vv. 16-18 and 27-29); inclusio (Moses speaks at v. 1 and v. 30); a parenthetic narrative frame around the tent-of-meeting theophany (vv. 14-15); and the striking paradox of a book being completed "to the very end" (*ʿad tummām*, v. 24) while the book we are reading has four chapters to go — a textual self-reference that has occupied interpreters from the Talmud forward. Bavli Bava Batra 15a asks how Moses could have written Deut 34:5 ("So Moses… died there") and offers two answers: Joshua wrote the last eight verses, or Moses wrote them weeping as prophecy.

Textually, Deut 31 is stable across MT, SP, LXX, and the Qumran Deuteronomy manuscripts. 4QDeutb preserves portions of ch. 31 in readings that largely agree with MT. There is no crux comparable to Deut 32:8 or 32:43. The LXX occasionally smooths Hebrew idioms ("go out and come in" is rendered more explicitly as "lead and be led") and expands pronoun antecedents. The most interesting literary-textual observation, as Jeffrey Tigay and Ronald Hendel (Textual Criticism of the Hebrew Bible) note, is the careful lexical distinction the chapter maintains between *haTorah* (the law, vv. 9, 11, 12, 24, 26) and *haShirah* (the song, vv. 19, 21, 22, 30): these are two distinct written deposits, and the chapter refuses to collapse them. This distinction — Torah vs. Song — is a load-bearing beam of the book's self-understanding and critical to the literary reading of chapter 32.

Special attention is due to v. 26: "Take this Book of the Torah and put it *beside* (*miṣṣad*) the ark of the covenant." Exod 25:16 and 40:20 have the stone tablets placed *inside* the ark; 1 Kgs 8:9 confirms only the tablets were in the ark. Deut 31:26, by contrast, positions the written Torah "beside" or "alongside" the ark — a distinction preserved in rabbinic halakhah (Bavli Bava Batra 14a-b debates whether there was a ledge or shelf for the scroll) and in medieval commentators (Rashi reads *miṣṣad* as "on the side of," Ibn Ezra as "near"). The theological payoff: the tablets (Decalogue) are inside; the Torah scroll (Mosaic exposition) is alongside. Revelation and interpretation are distinguished yet joined.

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

**Jewish reception.** Second Temple sources received Deut 31 as the inauguration of Torah as book. Josephus (Antiquities 4.8.44-48) paraphrases the chapter, foregrounding the seven-year reading as a normative institution. Philo (Life of Moses 2.2) reads Moses's farewell as the paradigm of the philosophical death — the wise man handing off to his disciple. The Qumran community, which copied Deuteronomy more than any other Torah book (at least 33 manuscripts or fragments), took the chapter's "deposit-and-read" logic seriously: the community's Rule of the Community (1QS) mandates continual reading of Torah in thirds of the night. Ben Sira 46:1-6 celebrates Joshua as Moses's successor and war-leader, drawing on the commissioning language of Deut 31 and Josh 1.

Rabbinic interpretation developed Deut 31 in at least three directions. First, the *Hakhel* ceremony: Mishnah Sotah 7.8 details the septennial assembly at the Temple courtyards during Sukkot of the post-sabbatical year, when the king would read selected passages of Deuteronomy (typically 1:1-6:9, 11:13-21, and portions of chapters 14, 26, 27-28) to the gathered people. Maimonides (Hilchot Ḥagigah 3) codifies the practice and notes that it re-enacts Sinai: the king reads, the people listen as if receiving the Torah anew. The modern state of Israel has attempted partial re-enactments in 1945, 1952, 1959, 1966, 1973, 1980, 1987, 1994, 2001, 2008, 2015, and 2022 at the Western Wall. Second, the *authorship of the last eight verses* of Deuteronomy: Bavli Bava Batra 14b-15a debates whether Moses wrote them in tears as prophecy or Joshua completed the book, a debate that takes Deut 31:24 ("Moses finished writing") as its datum. Third, the *Torah-beside-ark* debate: Bavli Bava Batra 14b asks whether the scroll was inside or beside, with R. Meir and R. Judah on opposing sides.

Medieval commentators extend these threads. Rashi on 31:17 reads "I will hide my face" (*hastēr ʾastîr*) as a theological term for divine concealment during exile, a reading that Maimonides (Guide 3.51) and Joseph Albo (Sefer Ha-Ikkarim 4) develop into a full theology of *hester panim*. Ramban on 31:9 and 31:26 insists on the Torah-scroll's physical beside-ness as testimony to its status as canonical interpretation of the Decalogue. Ibn Ezra notes the archaic idioms in the chapter and raises (cautiously) the possibility that Joshua wrote the narrative frame. Sforno on 31:19 emphasizes that the Song (ch. 32) was given specifically so that it could "live unforgotten in the mouths of their offspring" — a catechetical function that Heschel and Leibowitz later elaborate.

Modern Jewish scholarship — Sacks (Covenant & Conversation Deuteronomy), Heschel (Prophets), Levenson (Sinai and Zion), Nechama Leibowitz (Studies in Deuteronomy), Zornberg (Moses: A Human Life), Fishbane (Biblical Interpretation in Ancient Israel) — has elaborated the theological architecture of the chapter. Fishbane reads vv. 9-13 as the charter of *teaching as transmission*: the septennial reading inserts the Torah into generational memory. Sacks treats the chapter as a political theology of leadership succession without dynasty — a pattern uniquely non-monarchical in the ancient Near East. Zornberg reads the chapter as Moses's meditation on his own absence, the anxiety of the teacher who will not be there to correct the students.

**Christian reception.** Patristic readers treated Joshua (*Yeshua*) typologically from Origen onward. Origen's 26 Homilies on Joshua open with the exegetical claim that Joshua is called "Jesus" in Greek for a reason: he is the type of Christ, the successor of Moses who does what Moses could not — bring the people into the land. Deut 31's commissioning (esp. vv. 7, 23) is read as the type of Christ's investiture. Augustine in Contra Faustum and elsewhere treats Joshua as the figural Christ crossing the Jordan. Cyril of Alexandria's Glaphyra on Deuteronomy and on Joshua elaborates this typology systematically.

The typology structures Hebrews 3-4, which explicitly distinguishes the "rest" Joshua did not ultimately give from the "sabbath rest" that remains for the people of God. "For if Joshua had given them rest, God would not have spoken of another day later on" (Heb 4:8). The Deuteronomic succession narrative is read in Hebrews as a deliberate sign of incompleteness: Joshua gives conquest but not eschatological rest. This is a distinctly Christian rereading that the Jewish tradition does not share — for Jewish readers, the Deut 31 succession is complete on its own terms, because Torah is given.

Hebrews 13:5 cites Deut 31:6/8 — "He will never leave you nor forsake you" — as a word of pastoral assurance to believers under pressure. The citation abstracts the phrase from its conquest setting and generalizes it. Medieval and Reformation Christian spirituality returned to this verse repeatedly: Bernard of Clairvaux, Luther in his Letters, and Calvin's Commentary on Deuteronomy all cite it as the covenantal floor of Christian assurance.

Reformation readers — Luther's Lectures on Deuteronomy (1525), Calvin's Commentary, the Puritan commentators — took Deut 31 as central for a theology of the written Word. Luther especially loved the chapter: the deposit-and-read structure (v. 9-13, 24-26) becomes for Luther a proto-scriptural theology in which God binds himself to the written word read publicly. Calvin draws from vv. 10-13 a theological charter for the public reading of Scripture as ecclesial practice, a view that shapes the Reformed liturgical tradition.

Modern Christian critical scholars — Brueggemann (Abingdon Deuteronomy), McConville (Apollos), Block (NIVAC), Craigie (NICOT), Nelson (OTL), Patrick Miller (Interpretation), Dennis Olson (Deuteronomy and the Death of Moses 1994), Thomas Römer (The So-Called Deuteronomistic History) — treat the chapter as the Deuteronomist's theological bridge. Olson's thesis is particularly influential: Deut 31-34 stages the death of Moses as a theological crisis and resolution, the moment when Torah as teaching becomes Torah as book, surviving its teacher. Brueggemann reads the chapter as the Deuteronomist's confession that Torah cannot guarantee fidelity — the Song (ch. 32) is commissioned precisely because the Torah alone will not prevent apostasy.

**Where they converge and diverge.** Jewish and Christian readings converge on Deut 31 as the inauguration of Torah as written canon and on the "be strong and courageous" charge as foundational for faithful succession under divine accompaniment. They diverge on Joshua: Jewish tradition reads Joshua as the faithful disciple who completes Moses's mission in its own terms; Christian tradition reads Joshua as the figural Christ whose name ("Yeshua"/"Jesus") makes the typology irresistible. They also diverge on septennial *Hakhel*: for Judaism, a live liturgical institution; for Christianity, a general charter for public reading of Scripture.

## Lens 4 — The Theological Intent

**Tension 1: Does Torah guarantee fidelity, or does Torah require a Song to supplement its witness?** Deut 31 contains a striking juxtaposition. Vv. 9-13 inaugurate the written Torah and prescribe its septennial reading — the instrument by which Israel will "learn to fear YHWH" and "keep all the words of this Torah." But vv. 16-21 predict Israel's apostasy as a certainty, and YHWH himself commissions the Song (ch. 32) as a supplementary witness: "this song shall confront them as a witness" (v. 21). The Torah that has just been written down is explicitly insufficient to prevent the failure.

The *high-Torah* position (classical rabbinic Judaism, Rambam in Guide 3.31-49, Calvin's high doctrine of the sufficiency of Scripture, the Westminster tradition) reads Torah as fully sufficient: the Song supplements pedagogy, not efficacy. The Torah really does what YHWH says it does; Israel's apostasy is a matter of will, not of any inadequacy in the instrument.

The *pedagogical-insufficiency* position (Brueggemann, Olson, much modern critical scholarship) reads the juxtaposition as the Deuteronomist's sober admission that even Torah, even perfectly deposited and publicly read, does not guarantee fidelity. The Song is theologically necessary because Torah is theologically limited — it instructs, but it does not regenerate. This reading dovetails with Jer 31:31-34 and the new-covenant tradition: the old covenant's instruments, including Torah, await a deeper interiority.

The *Pauline/Augustinian* position goes further. Paul reads "the letter kills" (2 Cor 3:6) as a claim that Torah as written instrument is intrinsically limited — not failed, but eschatologically oriented toward the Spirit-written Torah (Jer 31:33, Ezek 36:26-27). The Song in Deut 31 is a witness against Israel's inability, not a pedagogical supplement.

The *steelman* is the pedagogical-insufficiency position, because it takes v. 21's "I know what they are inclined to do" as a sober anthropological claim the text actually makes. The high-Torah position has to soften v. 21 into a conditional statement. **Where this leaves us:** Torah is given as sufficient for its covenantal purpose (pedagogy and witness), but the text itself acknowledges that fidelity requires more than reading. The Song will supply part of that "more"; the eschatological heart-circumcision of Deut 30:6 will supply the rest.

**Tension 2: What kind of authority passes from Moses to Joshua, and what remains with Moses alone?**

The *full-succession* position reads Joshua as the full successor: the triple commissioning (vv. 3, 7-8, 23), the investiture language "be strong and courageous," and the transfer of the Spirit (Num 27:18-23; Deut 34:9) establish Joshua as complete heir. This reading fits the Deuteronomistic narrative logic: Joshua 1 opens by reinstating Joshua with the exact formula.

The *partial-succession* position reads Joshua as inheriting leadership and military command but *not* Moses's prophetic-revelatory station. Deut 34:10 is the controlling text: "There has not arisen a prophet since in Israel like Moses, whom YHWH knew face to face." Moses's prophetic office is unique. Joshua leads, but the Torah is Moses's permanent voice in Israel. Maimonides systematizes this in the thirteen principles: Moses's prophecy is categorically distinct.

The *christological-fulfillment* position (patristic and Reformation readings) reads the Deut 31 succession as deliberately partial because it points beyond itself. Joshua as type cannot give eschatological rest (Heb 3-4); only the antitype can. The partial succession in Deut 31 is a theological signpost.

The *steelman* is the partial-succession position. It takes seriously Deut 34:10, respects the text's own careful distinction between Torah and Song and between Moses and Joshua, and avoids the over-realized readings in both directions. **Where this leaves us:** authority in biblical leadership is always partial. Even Joshua, fully commissioned, does not replace Moses; even a Song, fully inspired, does not replace Torah; even Torah, fully written, does not replace the need for covenantal renewal.

**One unresolved question:** YHWH tells Moses that Israel *will* apostatize (vv. 16-21). Moses tells Israel that he *knows* they will corrupt themselves after his death (v. 29). Why, then, is Torah given? If failure is foreknown and certain, what is the function of the instrument that cannot prevent it? The chapter refuses to answer. Brueggemann calls this the "pathos of pedagogy" — instruction offered in the foreknowledge of its partial reception. But the question remains open, and it is the generative question of the rest of the Deuteronomistic History.

## The Thread

Joshua 1:5-9 is the most direct echo: "Be strong and courageous… no man shall be able to stand before you… I will not leave you or forsake you." The Deuteronomist deliberately re-uses the Deut 31:6-8, 23 formula at the opening of Joshua to signal narrative continuity. More subtly, 1 Chronicles 22:11-13 and 28:20 have David charging Solomon with the same formula — "be strong and courageous… he will not leave you nor forsake you" — re-using the Deut 31 succession script to frame the transition from David to Solomon and from conquest to Temple.

Less obvious: Nehemiah 8. Ezra's reading of the Torah at the Water Gate on the first day of the seventh month, with all the people gathered — men, women, and children who can understand — is widely read as a late post-exilic *Hakhel* re-enactment of Deut 31:10-13. The parallels are precise: public assembly, reading of Torah, festival context (Sukkot begins two weeks later and is observed in Neh 8:13-18), children included. Tigay, Weinfeld, and rabbinic tradition all note the echo.

A third thread: Matthew 28:18-20 (the Great Commission). Jesus commissions his disciples with formulae that echo Deut 31 and Josh 1 filtered through the succession tradition: "All authority has been given to me… go therefore… I am with you always, to the end of the age." The final clause — *egō meth' hymōn eimi pasas tas hēmeras* — parallels Deut 31:6/8 LXX ("he will not leave you") and structures the Great Commission as a new succession narrative, with Jesus as the Moses-and-Joshua figure commissioning the disciples. Richard Hays and Dale Allison both trace the echo.

A fourth, non-obvious thread: Paul in 2 Timothy 4:1-8. Paul's farewell to Timothy self-consciously adopts the Mosaic succession form: a dying teacher charges his successor ("I charge you in the presence of God… be sober-minded"), deposits the written word ("bring the scrolls, especially the parchments," v. 13), and faces the end ("the time of my departure is at hand"). Scholars from Luke Timothy Johnson to N.T. Wright read 2 Tim 4 as the Pauline Deut 31.
