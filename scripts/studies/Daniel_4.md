# Daniel 4: The Tree, the Beast, and the Royal Decree

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

Daniel 4 is among the strangest chapters in the Hebrew Bible at the level of literary form: it is a **first-person royal decree** issued in Nebuchadnezzar's name (vv. 1–3, 34–37) that frames a third-person narrative of his madness (vv. 28–33) inside which sits a first-person account of his dream and its interpretation (vv. 4–27, 34–37). The form is unusual but not without parallel. Persian-period royal inscriptions (the Bisitun inscription of Darius I, c. 520 BC) and Neo-Babylonian building inscriptions of Nebuchadnezzar himself (the *Wadi Brisa* inscription, the *East India House* inscription) use the first-person royal-decree form, and the chapter seems to have absorbed the convention. **Carol Newsom** (OTL, 2014) treats the form as a deliberate literary borrowing from Achaemenid royal proclamation; **Stephen Miller** (NAC) reads it as an authentic royal decree preserved by Daniel.

The chapter's central historical problem is that there is no Babylonian record — none — of Nebuchadnezzar suffering a madness or a multi-year incapacity. His reign is well documented in the *Babylonian Chronicles*, in his own building inscriptions, and in the cuneiform astronomical diaries; none mentions a seven-year absence. The dossier is striking by its silence. **John J. Collins** (Hermeneia, 1993), **Carol Newsom** (OTL), **Lester Grabbe**, and **Maurice Casey** treat that silence as effectively decisive against historical reading. Conservatives — **Stephen Miller** (NAC), **Donald Wiseman** (*Nebuchadrezzar and Babylon*, 1985), **Gleason Archer**, **Joyce Baldwin** (TOTC, 1978) — counter that royal annals routinely omit humiliating episodes (Sennacherib's defeat at Jerusalem in 701 BC is missing from his own annals; Cyrus's death in battle is largely absent from Persian record), that the seven-year ("seven times") period is plausibly figurative or short, and that Nabopolassar's earlier illness or even Nebuchadnezzar's late-life infirmity could account for the silence.

The strongest external datum is the **Prayer of Nabonidus** (4QPrNab, four fragments found in Cave 4 at Qumran, paleographically dated to about 100 BC and reconstructed by J.T. Milik in *RB* 1956). The text, in Aramaic, has Nabonidus — Nebuchadnezzar's eventual successor and the last Neo-Babylonian king — relate that he was afflicted by an "ulcer" or wasting disease for seven years during his retreat to Tema (Teima) in the Arabian desert; that he prayed to the gods of silver and gold and bronze and iron and wood and stone and was not healed; that a Jewish *gazer* (diviner, possibly read as "exorcist") "forgave my sin" and counseled him to declare an honor decree to the Most High God. The narrative parallel to Daniel 4 is structurally exact: a Babylonian king afflicted with a seven-period illness, a Jewish wise man who interprets and effects the recovery, a closing royal confession of the Most High. Crucial difference: in 4QPrNab the king is **Nabonidus**, not Nebuchadnezzar. Nabonidus did historically retreat to Tema for roughly ten years (553–543 BC), an event corroborated by the **Nabonidus Chronicle** (BM 35382), the **Verse Account of Nabonidus** (BM 38299), and the **Harran Stelae** (Sippar texts). His prolonged absence from Babylon was widely remembered as scandalous and was used by the Cyrus-supporting Marduk priesthood to justify the Persian conquest. **Collins** and **Newsom** treat 4QPrNab as the strongest single argument that Daniel 4 is a literary reworking of a Nabonidus tradition rebound onto the more famous Nebuchadnezzar — likely because by the late Persian and Hellenistic periods, Nebuchadnezzar had become the Babylonian king-of-ill-fame in Jewish memory and the typological consolidation of Babylonian misrule onto his name was natural. **Childs** (in *Introduction to the Old Testament as Scripture*, 1979) suggests a third option: the canonical Daniel uses an older tradition theologically without committing to the historical specifics — a canonical-shape reading that distinguishes the chapter's truth-claim from its historical-reportage claim.

The **watcher** (Aramaic *ir*, עִיר) figure of vv. 13, 17, 23 is a Persian-period angelological innovation. The term is otherwise rare in the Hebrew Bible (it appears only in Daniel 4) but is central to **1 Enoch** 1–36 (the Book of the Watchers, c. third-second century BC), where the Watchers are the angelic class — including the fallen Watchers of 1 Enoch 6–11 who consort with human women in a midrashic expansion of Genesis 6. **Collins** has tracked the Daniel-Enoch relationship in *The Apocalyptic Imagination* (1984, rev. 1998); the watcher-figure is one of several pieces of evidence locating Daniel and 1 Enoch in overlapping Hellenistic-period angelology. The "decree of the watchers, the decision by the word of the holy ones" (v. 17) is a striking double appeal — heavenly council deliberating in plural — that has fed Jewish and Christian angelology for two millennia.

Key terms: *ir* (עִיר, "watcher," vv. 13, 17, 23 — a divine-council messenger figure); *iddanin* (עִדָּנִין, "times," v. 16, 23, 25, 32 — the "seven periods of time," indeterminate as to whether years, seasons, or symbolic units); *Illa'ah* (עִלָּאָה, "Most High," vv. 17, 24, 25, 32, 34 — the chapter's preferred divine title, a deliberately exalted form for pagan-imperial address); *gazrin* (גָּזְרִין, "diviners/astrologers," v. 7) — the cognate of the term in 4QPrNab.

## Lens 2 — The Literature

The chapter is structured as a royal-decree-frame around a court tale. Vv. 1–3 are the decree opening — "King Nebuchadnezzar to all peoples, nations, and languages." Vv. 4–27 are the first-person dream-and-interpretation. Vv. 28–33 are a third-person narrative of the dream's fulfillment — the only third-person section. Vv. 34–37 return to first-person doxology and decree closing. This frame structure — a royal decree containing a dream containing a humiliation narrative containing a confession — is one of the most layered literary forms in the Old Testament.

The **chiastic pairing** in the Aramaic block (chapters 2–7) places chapter 4 with chapter 5: chapter 4 is the humbled king who repents; chapter 5 is the unhumbled king who falls. **A. Lenglet** (1972), Collins, and Newsom all use the chiasm. The pairing is significant: chapter 4 is the *positive* outcome of imperial pride confronted, chapter 5 the *negative*. Read together, they constitute the book's diptych on imperial hubris.

The dream-image of the cosmic tree is itself a Mesopotamian and Levantine commonplace. **Ezekiel 31** has Pharaoh as the cosmic cedar of Lebanon, with branches that shelter the nations and roots that drink from the great deep, cut down by foreigners. Daniel 4 reworks the Ezekiel oracle into prose narrative; the verbal echoes are too dense to be coincidence. The cosmic-tree image is also widespread in Ancient Near Eastern royal ideology — Assyrian palace reliefs feature the stylized "sacred tree" with the king flanking it; Mesopotamian cylinder seals show the tree as the center of the cosmos. The tree's reach to heaven and visibility to the ends of the earth (vv. 11, 22) is the standard imperial self-image; the chapter takes the image and inverts it.

The leitwort is *Illa'ah* (Most High), which appears nine times in the chapter — a deliberate concentration. The repeated phrase "the Most High rules the kingdom of men and gives it to whom he will" (vv. 17, 25, 32) is the chapter's theological refrain, repeated three times as a structural marker.

Canonical placement: Daniel 4 sits inside the Aramaic block (2:4b–7:28). The Old Greek (LXX) recension of chapter 4 differs significantly from the Theodotion and Masoretic versions — the LXX has additional details about the Tema-like withdrawal, fewer doxological elements, and a different ordering of materials. **Collins** treats the LXX of Daniel 4 as preserving an older form of the tradition, closer in some respects to 4QPrNab; the Masoretic version is a later, more theologically integrated form. The textual situation is one of the messier instances of Old Greek vs. Theodotion divergence in the Bible.

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

The chapter sits in the canonical arc as the central instance of the *humbled-king* theme. Pharaoh of Exodus refuses humbling and is destroyed. Nebuchadnezzar of Daniel 4 is humbled, repents, and is restored. Belshazzar of Daniel 5 refuses humbling and is destroyed within hours. The pattern recurs in 2 Kings 20 (Hezekiah humbled and given fifteen more years), in 2 Chronicles 33 (Manasseh humbled and restored), and is the structural counter-image to Pharaoh and Antiochus IV. The pride-and-fall arc is one of the most explicit moral patterns in the Bible and Daniel 4 is its most extended narrative instance.

**Jewish interpretive trajectory.** Second-Temple Jewish reading was direct. **4QPrNab** is the parallel tradition. **Josephus** (*Antiquities* 10.10.6) retells the story with measurable cleanup of the supernatural register. **The Bavli** (Sanhedrin 92b–94a, Pesahim 94a) treats Nebuchadnezzar's humiliation as the prototype of imperial-pride judgment, with extended midrashic elaboration of the seven-times period. **Saadia Gaon's** commentary, **Rashi**, **Ibn Ezra**, and **Abrabanel** all read the chapter as historically grounded — the medieval Jewish tradition is conservative on the historicity question — and the king's confession of the Most High is read as a partial conversion, possibly the first stage of an eschatological-universal recognition. Modern Jewish reading (Collins's interlocutors **Lawrence Wills** in *The Jew in the Court of the Foreign King*, 1990; **Aaron Koller**) has tended to follow the critical line, treating the chapter as Hellenistic-period reworking of older Babylonian tradition and reading it as Maccabean-era political theology against Antiochus IV.

**Christian interpretive trajectory.** **Hippolytus's *Commentary on Daniel*** (c. AD 200) reads the chapter as the pattern of imperial pride and divine humbling, with anticipation of Christ's preaching that "whoever exalts himself will be humbled" (Matt 23:12). **Jerome's *Commentary on Daniel*** (407) takes the chapter as historical and as a moral lesson on pride, against Porphyry's earlier critical reading. **Augustine** in *City of God* 14.13 treats Nebuchadnezzar's pride as a paradigmatic instance of the *libido dominandi* — the love of domination — that drives the *civitas terrena* (the earthly city) and is the root sin. The Augustinian reading places Daniel 4 at the foundation of Western theological anthropology of pride. **Aquinas** (*Summa Theologiae* II-II, q. 162, on pride) follows the Augustinian line while distinguishing pride from magnanimity (q. 129); the Catholic ethical tradition uses Daniel 4 as illustration of the disordered love-of-self that is the root of every sin against right reason.

**Calvin's *Lectures on Daniel*** (1561) treats the chapter as historically grounded and as a model of God's particular providence. **Luther's *Preface*** (1530) reads it more politically — as a warning to the secular princes of his day. **Jonathan Edwards's** sermons on humility (notably "Charity Contrary to a Proud Spirit" in *Charity and Its Fruits*, 1738) draw on Daniel 4 as primary illustration. The Reformation-Reformed tradition has used the chapter as a primary text for total-depravity anthropology — pride as the root sin from which all other sins flow.

The critical reading (Bertholdt, Driver, Charles, Montgomery, Rowley, Collins, Newsom, Davies, Casey, Grabbe) treats the chapter as Hellenistic-period reworking of the Nabonidus tradition, designed to encourage Maccabean-era Jewish readers under Antiochus IV's pressure: the empires fall when their kings make themselves divine; God will humble Antiochus as he humbled the original Babylonian king-of-the-tradition.

Where the trajectories converge: all readings — Jewish, patristic, Reformation, modern critical — treat the chapter as a paradigmatic statement of imperial pride and divine sovereignty. Where they diverge: on the historicity of Nebuchadnezzar's specific madness, on whether the chapter is an individual moral exemplum or a political-theological judgment on imperial systems as such, and on whether the king's confession represents conversion in any meaningful sense.

## Lens 4 — The Theological Intent

**Tension 1: The historicity question.** The Prayer of Nabonidus parallel is the central data point and the readings divide. **Collins, Newsom, Grabbe, Casey:** Daniel 4 is a literary reworking of an older Nabonidus tradition, rebound onto Nebuchadnezzar; the canonical chapter is theologically powerful but does not commit the canon to the historicity of a Nebuchadnezzar madness. **Miller, Archer, Wiseman, Baldwin (the conservative line):** the silence in Babylonian records is consistent with royal annals' general practice of omitting humiliating episodes; the seven "times" is plausibly a short period of incapacity at the end of Nebuchadnezzar's reign; the parallel to 4QPrNab is interesting but does not overturn the chapter's straightforward historical claims. **Childs (canonical reading):** the canonical Daniel uses the tradition theologically — the chapter's truth-claim is theological, not historical-reportage; the question is whether God humbles imperial pride, and the answer is yes, regardless of whether the specific incident occurred to Nebuchadnezzar or Nabonidus.

**Steelman the conservative reading.** The critical reading assumes that the silence in Babylonian records is decisive, but royal annals are not neutral histories — they are propaganda. Sennacherib does not record his defeat at Jerusalem; the *Annals of Tukulti-Ninurta* gloss over reverses. A multi-year incapacitation of Nebuchadnezzar would be the kind of thing his successors and his own court would suppress; absent direct evidence (which we would not expect to find), the silence is not evidence of absence. The 4QPrNab parallel is intriguing but cuts both ways: it could be that the Daniel author reworked a Nabonidus tradition, or it could be that an older Nebuchadnezzar tradition was reworked onto Nabonidus by the 4QPrNab author, or the two figures could have suffered analogous episodes that were independently traditioned. The conservative reading is not stupid; it is a serious historiographical position.

**Where this leaves us:** the question of historicity is unsettled and is unlikely to be settled by future evidence; the chapter's theological work is unaffected by the conclusion. A reader who must have settled historicity in order to read the chapter has placed the burden of meaning on the wrong load-bearing column.

**Tension 2: Pride as theological category.** **Augustine** in *City of God* 14.13 places pride (*superbia*) at the foundation of the fall and treats it as the root of every other sin: "What is pride but a perverse desire of height? For it is a perverse height to forsake the bond of unity and to set up a base of authority in oneself." The Augustinian reading reads Daniel 4 as the most explicit biblical narrative of *superbia* and its remedy — humbling. **Aquinas** (*ST* II-II, q. 162) follows Augustine and distinguishes pride from magnanimity (q. 129): magnanimity is the right ordering of self-regard to the genuinely great deeds the agent is capable of; pride is the disordered ascription of greatness to oneself unrelated to the genuine good. Catholic ethical formation has used the distinction to recover a *positive* theology of self-respect and noble action; pride is not the same as self-confidence.

The Reformers' total-depravity reading (Calvin, *Institutes* 2.1; the Synod of Dort, 1618–19; Jonathan Edwards) takes pride more strictly as the architectural form of *all* sin: every sin is a refusal of God's lordship and an arrogation of self-sovereignty, of which Daniel 4 is the most pictorial instance. The Reformed reading is darker than the Catholic on the question of whether magnanimity can survive the fall in any uncomplicated form.

The Maccabean political reading (Collins, Newsom) reads the chapter not principally as individual moral instruction but as political-theological judgment on imperial systems: Nebuchadnezzar is the archetype of every Antiochus, every Caesar, every empire that mistakes its own arrangements for the cosmic order. The pride is *systemic*, not merely individual — the imperial structure as such cultivates and rewards the pattern that the chapter narrates and judges. The chapter, on this reading, is closer to the prophetic critique of Babylon (Isaiah 47, Jeremiah 50–51) than to wisdom-literature individual moralism. **Steelman the political reading**: it is not that the individual reading is wrong but that the chapter's frame — a royal decree to all peoples, nations, and languages — pushes outward from the king's individual psychology to the empire's systemic logic. To read the chapter only as personal moral exemplum is to miss what the genre and the frame are doing.

The Eastern Orthodox reading (Theodoret of Cyrrhus's commentary; the Byzantine Fathers more broadly) tends toward the synthesis: pride is both individual and systemic; the chapter is both moral instruction and political-theological judgment; the king's restoration is both personal repentance and a sign of God's mercy on the imperial structure. The Orthodox emphasis on *theosis* — divinization through grace — gives a particular reading: pride is the *false* divinization, the seizing of what can only be received; the chapter's humbling is the necessary preparation for the king's right reception of the divine name.

**Where this leaves us:** the chapter teaches both individual and political-theological lessons, and the reader who chooses one reading to the exclusion of the other has narrowed the chapter. Augustine, Calvin, and Aquinas read it correctly as individual moral instruction; Collins and the political readers read it correctly as systemic critique. Both readings are needed, and the chapter's frame — a king issuing a decree to "all peoples, nations, and languages" — itself insists on both registers.

**One unresolved question.** If pride is the root sin and Nebuchadnezzar's confession is the model of its remedy, what does it mean that the king's repentance is initiated only by the loss of his reason and the experience of being driven out among the beasts — and what does that suggest about whether the proud can repent without something analogous?

## The Thread

The Joseph parallel is here too — a king's troubled dream, the failure of the *hartummim*, a Hebrew interpreter — but the deeper Old Testament thread is **Ezekiel 31**, where Pharaoh of Egypt is the cosmic cedar of Lebanon, sheltering the nations, cut down by foreigners. Daniel 4 reworks Ezekiel 31 as narrative; the verbal echoes (the tree's reach to heaven, its visibility to the ends of the earth, the beasts under it, the birds in its branches) are too dense to be coincidence. **Isaiah 14**, the taunt-song over the king of Babylon ("How you have fallen from heaven, O Day Star, son of Dawn!"), is the third intertext — the imperial pride genre at its most concentrated.

Forward to the New Testament, three lines run from Daniel 4. First, **Acts 12:20–23**: Herod Agrippa I, accepting acclamations as a god, is struck down "because he did not give God the glory" — the structure is Daniel 4 in miniature, with the chapter's theological judgment intact and the repentance-and-restoration arc removed. Second, **Luke 1:51–52** in the Magnificat: "He has scattered the proud in the imagination of their hearts; he has put down the mighty from their thrones." Mary's song is theologically the heir of Daniel 4 — the same God who humbled Nebuchadnezzar humbles every imperial-pride structure, and the Christ who is to be born is the inversion of the Nebuchadnezzar form. Third, **Philippians 2:5–11** — Christ "did not count equality with God a thing to be grasped, but emptied himself, taking the form of a servant" — is the inverse of Daniel 4, and the structural negative-image: Nebuchadnezzar grasps and is humbled; Christ does not grasp and is exalted. The chapter's theology of pride and humbling is the negative space against which the New Testament's Christology of self-emptying is drawn.
