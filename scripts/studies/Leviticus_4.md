# Leviticus 4: Blood on the Horns of the Altar

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

Leviticus — Hebrew *Wayyiqrāʾ* (וַיִּקְרָא, "and he called") from 1:1 — continues from the completed tabernacle of Exodus 40. Chapter 4 opens a new section within the worshipper-manual (Lev 1-5): having legislated the voluntary offerings of chs. 1-3 (burnt, grain, peace), the code now turns to mandatory expiatory offerings. Chapter 4 treats the sin offering (*ḥaṭṭāʾt*) proper; chapter 5 continues with additional cases and the guilt offering (*ʾāšām*). The sin offering, far more than the preceding three, is the engine of the sacrificial system and the hinge on which Day of Atonement (Lev 16) turns.

Critical debate over dating follows familiar lines. Wellhausen (*Prolegomena*, 1878) placed P post-exilic; Kaufmann, Haran, and Milgrom argued pre-exilic. Israel Knohl's *Sanctuary of Silence* (1995) distinguished P from the Holiness School (H, Lev 17-26). The *ḥaṭṭāʾt* legislation of Lev 4 is assigned to P proper. Milgrom's *Anchor Bible Leviticus* (1991-2000) devotes several hundred pages to Lev 4-5 and argues that the procedural and terminological details of the chapter preserve genuinely archaic ritual practice — particularly the graduated treatment of blood disposal — that would be unintelligible if retrojected from Second Temple practice. Baruch Levine's *JPS Leviticus* concurs on a pre-exilic core.

The chapter's central terminological question is the meaning of *ḥaṭṭāʾt* (חַטָּאת). The traditional rendering "sin offering" construes it as expiation for the offerer's sin. Milgrom's major contribution — worked out over decades and summarized at length in his commentary — argues the term is better rendered "purification offering." The offering does not purify the worshipper; it purifies the sanctuary. Sin, in P's conception, generates a kind of ritual pollution (*ṭumʾâ*) that attaches to the sanctuary from a distance; the offering cleanses the sanctuary, and only thereby forgives the worshipper. Milgrom developed this from the piel form *ḥiṭṭēʾ* ("to de-sin, to purge") and the striking fact that in Lev 4 the blood is never applied to the worshipper but always to cultic surfaces. Jay Sklar (*Sin, Impurity, Sacrifice, Atonement*, 2005) accepted much of Milgrom's analysis but argued for a more layered reading: *kipper* (atonement) is both purgative (cleansing the sanctuary) and ransoming (paying for the offerer's life). Nobuyoshi Kiuchi (*The Purification Offering in the Priestly Literature*, 1987) pressed against Milgrom, arguing the offering does both at once and that the priest-as-bearer of sin is an older strand. The debate has reshaped the field.

The graduated structure is the chapter's architectural signature. Four cases are specified, each for a different class of sinner, each with a different offering and — critically — a different disposition of the blood. The grading maps onto a social-ritual hierarchy:

- **Anointed priest** (*hakōhēn hammāšîaḥ*, v. 3): bull. Blood is carried INSIDE the sanctuary, sprinkled seven times before the veil, applied to the horns of the incense altar (inner court), with the remainder poured out at the base of the bronze altar.
- **Whole congregation** (*kol-ʿădat yiśrāʾēl*, v. 13): bull. Identical blood treatment to the priest's.
- **Tribal chief** (*nāśîʾ*, v. 22): male goat. Blood applied only to the horns of the bronze altar (outer court), remainder poured at its base. No inner-sanctuary sprinkling.
- **Common person** (*nefeš mēʿam-hāʾāreṣ*, v. 27): female goat or female lamb. Same outer-altar blood treatment.

Milgrom's interpretation of this graduated structure is cosmologically precise: sin contaminates the sanctuary in proportion to the status of the sinner. A priest's sin penetrates to the inner sanctum (his proximity to holy things conducts pollution); a chief's or commoner's sin contaminates only the outer court. The blood is applied where the pollution has reached — it is a graduated cleansing operation. The theological implication is radical: Israel's leaders carry greater ritual weight, and their sin does more damage.

All four cases concern unintentional sin (*bišgāgâ*, בִּשְׁגָגָה, from the root *šgg* meaning to err, to wander). The distinction between *šĕgāgâ* and high-handed sin (*bĕyād rāmâ*, Num 15:30 — "with a high hand") is foundational. Unintentional sin is covered by the *ḥaṭṭāʾt*; defiant sin is not. Numbers 15:30-31 is explicit: "whoever acts with a high hand, whether native or sojourner, reviles the LORD, and that person shall be cut off from among his people." The sacrificial system has a limit; deliberate rebellion against God sits outside it. This boundary will become critically important in Hebrews 10:26.

Material culture. The bull (*pār*) is the premium offering, marking the seriousness of priestly sin or collective sin. Goats (male for the chief, female for the commoner) are the standard middle-tier. The female lamb option for the commoner makes the offering accessible to households of moderate means; chapter 5 extends accessibility further. Four-horned altars of the sort Lev 4 requires are archaeologically attested. The Arad altar (Yohanan Aharoni's excavations, Iron II), the Beersheba four-horned altar (reassembled from ashlar blocks found reused in Stratum II), and the Dan altar all conform to the shape Lev 4 presupposes. The horns are the apex of the altar's sanctity — contact points where blood makes atonement (cf. Ex 29:12, Lev 8:15). Seymour Gitin's work on horned altars in Iron Age II Philistia provides broader context.

Key terms to carry:

- *Ḥaṭṭāʾt* (חַטָּאת) — sin / sin offering / purification offering. Milgrom: "purification offering."
- *Bišgāgâ* (בִּשְׁגָגָה) — unintentionally; inadvertently.
- *Kipper* (כִּפֵּר) — atone; cover; wipe; ransom. Meaning contested. Milgrom favors "purge"; Sklar "purge and ransom"; traditional Christian reading "cover / atone." The Arabic cognate *kafara* means "cover"; the Akkadian *kuppuru* (a standard Mesopotamian ritual-cleansing term) means "wipe clean."
- *Nāśîʾ* (נָשִׂיא) — lifted-up one; tribal chief; in later texts, prince. Not king — Lev 4 is pre-monarchic in its setting.
- *Hakōhēn hammāšîaḥ* (הַכֹּהֵן הַמָּשִׁיחַ) — the anointed priest; the high priest.

Manuscript evidence. 4QLeva (4Q17) preserves portions of Lev 4 with only orthographic variation. The LXX renders *ḥaṭṭāʾt* as *peri hamartias* ("for sin"), a choice Paul will pick up in Rom 8:3 — God sent his Son "as a sin offering" (*peri hamartias*) — shaping the NT reception. The Samaritan Pentateuch and Peshitta align closely with MT.

ANE parallels. Hittite Instructions for Temple Officials preserve the closest procedural parallels to Lev 4's graduated blood disposal — specific contamination requires specific ritual responses, with senior officials bearing heavier liability. Mesopotamian *kuppuru* rites (the verbal root is cognate with Hebrew *kipper*) use blood and other purgatives to wipe clean cultic surfaces. Emar ritual texts document graduated offerings for different classes of offender. Israel's code is distinctive in monotheizing the structure — one God, one sanctuary — and in tying blood-purgation to the specific theology of life-in-blood (Lev 17:11), but the ritual grammar is recognizably West Semitic.

## Lens 2 — The Literature

The genre is priestly ritual jurisprudence in casuistic form, with an unusually elaborate graduated structure. The chapter is organized by sinner-class, with each of the four cases following the same procedural skeleton (presentation, hand-laying, slaughter, blood manipulation, fat disposal, declaration of forgiveness) but with deliberate variation at each step.

The literary device of repetition-with-variation is pressed to an architectural extreme. Each of the four cases repeats the core procedure; the variations are the interpretive signal. The first two cases (priest and congregation) share identical blood treatment — suggesting, as Wenham (NICOT) notes, that priestly sin and congregational sin are theologically parallel: the priest represents the congregation, so his inadvertent sin has the same reach as the people's collective sin. The last two cases (chief and commoner) share the outer-altar treatment, but differ in the animal (male vs. female, goat vs. lamb). The grading is not accidental; it is the chapter's sermon.

Two refrains give the text its cadence. The declaration *vĕkipper ʿālāyw hakōhēn vĕnislaḥ lô* ("the priest shall make atonement for him, and he shall be forgiven," vv. 26, 31, 35) is the chapter's formal conclusion, marking that the ritual has worked. The first two cases lack the "he shall be forgiven" formula in its full form (v. 20 has it; v. 10 and 12 do not), because the priest and congregation cases include the additional complication of the carcass being carried outside the camp and burned on the ash heap — a procedure whose theological logic (Lev 6:30; Heb 13:11-12) reserves the most serious *ḥaṭṭāʾt* animals for extramural disposal, unlike the outer-court cases where the priest eats the meat.

The second refrain, *bišgāgâ* ("unintentionally," vv. 2, 13, 22, 27), appears in each case and marks the chapter's boundary condition. Every case presupposes inadvertent error; none addresses deliberate sin.

The chapter's structural elegance has not escaped commentators. Mary Douglas in *Leviticus as Literature* (1999) treats Lev 4 as a ring-composition whose "concentric" structure mirrors the spatial arrangement of the sanctuary — inner-court blood for inner-circle sinners, outer-court blood for outer-circle sinners. Milgrom resists the ring-composition analysis but agrees on the spatial-ritual correspondence. Wenham (NICOT) and Hartley (WBC) both read the structure as pedagogical: Israel learns, by participating in the graduated ritual, that sin's damage radiates from the sanctuary outward in proportion to the sinner's standing.

Textual variants are minor. 4QLev fragments preserve portions in close agreement with MT. The LXX's use of *peri hamartias* for *ḥaṭṭāʾt* is consistent throughout and becomes the lexical bridge to NT usage. The Samaritan Pentateuch occasionally adjusts *nāśîʾ* (chief) in ways that reflect post-exilic political categories, but these are scribal and not substantive.

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

**Jewish Interpretive Trajectory.** Second Temple reception of Lev 4 is dense with halakhic elaboration. Philo (*Special Laws* 1.226-246) treats the graduated sin offerings as a moral-philosophical gradation: the soul's errors require differing remedies depending on the agent's knowledge and standing. Josephus (*Antiquities* 3.230-232) gives procedural detail, noting particularly the carcass-burning outside the camp. The Temple Scroll (11Q19, cols. 16-17) elaborates Lev 4's legislation considerably for the Qumran community's reconstructed temple. Jubilees touches the distinction between inadvertent and deliberate sin (Jub 21:7-10).

Rabbinic treatment is codified in the massive Mishnah Zevachim (sacrifice), with Keritot dealing specifically with the *ḥaṭṭāʾt* cases and Horayot treating the sin of the court and the high priest — an entire tractate devoted to Lev 4's graded structure. The Bavli on Horayot works through each of the four cases at length, with particular attention to the interplay between inadvertent error, collective liability, and the high priest's unique position. Sifra provides the verse-by-verse expansion. Rabbi Akiva and Rabbi Ishmael debate the scope of *šĕgāgâ* — whether it covers only errors of fact or also errors of law — a distinction that shapes halakhic practice for centuries.

Rashi (on 4:2) glosses *bišgāgâ* carefully: unintentional sin means committing an act one would not have committed had one known it was forbidden. This sets the boundary for sacrificial efficacy. Rashi's comment on 4:3 — the high priest's bull — emphasizes the weight his sin carries: "when the priest who is anointed sins, it brings guilt upon the people," drawing out the representative function. Ibn Ezra is typically terser but agrees on the graduated structure. Ramban (Nachmanides) develops a substitutionary reading at length in his comments on Lev 1 and 4, arguing explicitly that the animal dies in the offerer's place — the hand-laying transfers identification, the death satisfies justice, and the worshipper is forgiven. This is the clearest pre-Christian substitutionary theology of sacrifice in the Jewish tradition, and it has been influential on later Christian atonement thought (via Aquinas, who knew Ramban through Latin translations).

Maimonides (*Guide of the Perplexed* 3.46) gives a characteristically rationalist reading: the sacrifices are pedagogical concessions that direct Israel's religious impulses away from ANE idolatry. In *Hilchot Shegagot* he codifies Lev 4 practice for the restored Temple — a meticulous legal framework for unintentional sin. Bahya ben Asher, Sforno, and the medieval commentators add layers. Nehama Leibowitz (*Studies in Vayikra*) focuses on the theological weight of unintentional sin, arguing against trivializing it — the Torah takes inadvertence seriously because actions have consequences regardless of intent. Jonathan Sacks (*Covenant and Conversation: Leviticus*) reads the graduated structure as a theology of leadership: those who represent the community bear greater ritual weight for their errors. Avivah Zornberg treats Lev 4 through the lens of repentance and self-knowledge.

**Christian Interpretive Trajectory.** Patristic engagement with Lev 4 is substantial, focused primarily on the extra-camp disposal of the priestly sin offering and its typological connection to Christ. Hebrews 13:11-12 — "the bodies of those animals whose blood is brought into the holy places by the high priest as a sacrifice for sin are burned outside the camp. So Jesus also suffered outside the gate" — sets the pattern for patristic reading. Origen devotes two of his *Homilies on Leviticus* (Hom. 2 and 3) to the sin offerings, reading the four cases allegorically: the priest's sin is the sin of the teacher, the congregation's sin is the sin of the church, and so forth, each requiring graduated repentance. Cyril of Alexandria in *Glaphyra on Leviticus* develops the extra-camp typology at length, connecting Lev 4's burning outside the camp with Christ's crucifixion outside Jerusalem's walls. Augustine touches the sin offering at *City of God* 10 and *Against Faustus* 19, reading the graduated structure as teaching the seriousness of sin. Chrysostom and Theodoret follow.

Medieval exegesis develops these lines. Aquinas in *Summa Theologiae* I-II q. 102 a. 3 ad 4-6 treats the *ḥaṭṭāʾt* typologically, and in a crucial move at ST I-II q. 76 draws on the Levitical category of *šĕgāgâ* to develop his theology of *ignorantia invincibilis* — invincible ignorance — which becomes foundational for Catholic moral theology. The graduated liability of Lev 4 (a high priest is more culpable than a commoner for the same act) shapes Aquinas's thinking about the relation between agent standing and culpability. The Glossa Ordinaria compiles the patristic readings. Hugh of St. Victor and the twelfth-century Victorines develop the typological connection.

Reformation exegesis shifts the focus. Calvin's *Commentary on the Last Four Books of Moses* (1563) reads Lev 4 as teaching the seriousness of sin and the inadequacy of any sacrifice short of Christ's. Calvin emphasizes that the graduated structure means leaders bear more responsibility — a point with obvious ecclesiological freight for the Reformed tradition. Luther's treatment is more occasional but draws the same Christological conclusion. Later Reformed theology, particularly John Owen's *The Death of Death in the Death of Christ* (1648), treats the OT sin offerings as the typological substructure of penal substitution: each Levitical *ḥaṭṭāʾt* was a finite, repeated instance of what Christ's death accomplished once-for-all (cf. Heb 9-10).

Critical scholarship since Wellhausen has reshaped the field. Milgrom's argument that *ḥaṭṭāʾt* is "purification offering" rather than "sin offering," and that the offering cleanses the sanctuary rather than the worshipper, has been widely absorbed. Mary Douglas in *Purity and Danger* (1966) and *Leviticus as Literature* (1999) provides the anthropological framing — sin as contamination, sanctuary as boundary-maintaining cosmos. Sklar, Kiuchi, Hartley (WBC), and Wenham (NICOT) work the historical-grammatical and theological middle. Among contemporary theological commentators, Ephraim Radner (Brazos) reads Lev 4 figurally with heavy dependence on Hebrews; L. Michael Morales (*Who Shall Ascend*) places the chapter in P's broader ascent-and-access theology; Sklar's NIVAC volume is pastorally accessible; Christopher J. H. Wright reads it in the frame of Israel's missional theology; Brueggemann treats it in *Theology of the Old Testament* under "Israel's countertestimony."

**Where they converge and diverge.** Both traditions recognize the graduated structure as theologically significant — sin's damage is proportional to the sinner's representative standing. Both recognize the unintentional-vs-deliberate distinction as foundational. Both recognize that *ḥaṭṭāʾt* is not merely punitive but restorative. The divergence comes at the question of Christ. For Jewish interpretation, the sin offering system is suspended with the Temple; repentance (*tĕšûvâ*), prayer, and good deeds replace the sacrificial mechanism awaiting messianic restoration (Maimonides, *Hilchot Teshuvah*). For Christian interpretation, Hebrews 9-10 frames Lev 4 as typologically fulfilled in Christ's once-for-all sin offering, with the extra-camp burning particularly pressed (Heb 13:12). Catholic and Orthodox theology retain a sacrificial language in the Eucharist; Reformed theology insists on finality; Lutheran theology on the real presence without sacrificial repetition.

## Lens 4 — The Theological Intent

**Tension 1: Why does unintentional sin need atonement at all?** Modern moral intuition draws a sharp line between intention and consequence — an action done in ignorance is less culpable than one done deliberately. But Lev 4 requires full ritual atonement for inadvertent sin, as if the act's objective reality were itself the problem, regardless of the agent's mind.

The Jewish halakhic reading (Rashi, Rambam, Ramban, codified in Mishnah Shegagot and Bavli Horayot) takes the objective reality seriously. Sin damages the moral-cosmic order regardless of intent; *šĕgāgâ* still produces *ṭumʾâ* (pollution), and the pollution must be addressed. Ramban in his comment on Lev 4:2 presses the idea that the soul itself bears the impress of its acts, even the inadvertent ones, and that the offering restores the soul's orientation. Nehama Leibowitz develops this: the Torah refuses to let Israel dismiss consequences with "but I didn't mean to." The *ḥaṭṭāʾt* is the ritual infrastructure for taking objective consequence seriously.

Milgrom's critical reading presses the cosmological frame. Sin contaminates the sanctuary; the contamination is real regardless of intent. The sanctuary, like a physical space, can be polluted by accident as well as design. The *ḥaṭṭāʾt* cleanses the sanctuary so that God's presence can remain. Mary Douglas develops this anthropologically: the sacred space is a world-maintaining structure, and any breach — intentional or not — threatens the structure's integrity.

A Catholic reading through Aquinas takes the tension productively. Aquinas's doctrine of *ignorantia invincibilis* (ST I-II q. 76) argues that invincible ignorance excuses from formal sin but does not remove the act's objective disorder; inadvertent errors still require correction. The *ḥaṭṭāʾt* grammar becomes the infrastructure for Catholic moral theology's subtle distinctions between material and formal sin. Ratzinger (*Spirit of the Liturgy*) connects this to the church's objective liturgical order — the Eucharist is celebrated rightly regardless of the priest's subjective state, because the grace is objective. Reformed theology (Calvin, Owen) reads the chapter as teaching that sin is more serious than Israel's imagination grasps; even the honest mistake requires the death of an animal, and only Christ's blood can cover what even honest effort cannot escape. Lutheran theology with its doctrine of *simul iustus et peccator* reads the chapter as anticipating the honest believer's persistent, unknowing sinfulness — the Christian does not sin only when she means to. Orthodox theology through Schmemann reads *ḥaṭṭāʾt* as the ongoing ritual tending of a fallen cosmos, with human agency secondary to the cosmic disorder being repaired.

The steelman, rarely foregrounded, is the Milgrom-Douglas reading together: the chapter is not primarily about the offerer's moral psychology but about the sanctuary's ritual integrity. The *ḥaṭṭāʾt* is world-repair, not soul-repair; the soul's forgiveness is a downstream consequence of the cosmos being restored. This is less intuitive because it decenters the individual, but it fits the textual focus on blood-placement on cultic surfaces rather than on the worshipper.

**Where this leaves us:** unintentional sin matters because consequences matter. Intent softens culpability but does not dissolve damage. The *ḥaṭṭāʾt* is the ritual acknowledgment that the world — sanctuary, community, self — can be injured by what we did not mean to do, and that restoration is possible. Both traditions preserve this grammar; Christian theology locates the fullness of restoration in the cross, Jewish theology in repentance and the sanctified life.

**Tension 2: Why is the priest's sin offering identical to the congregation's?** The four cases descend in severity — priest, congregation, chief, commoner — except that the first two share identical procedures. Why should the single anointed priest's inadvertent sin call for the same bull, same blood treatment, same carcass disposal as the sin of the entire nation?

The Jewish halakhic reading (Mishnah Horayot, Rashi) takes this as evidence that the high priest is the corporate representative of Israel. His sin is, structurally, the nation's sin because he bears their standing before God. The parallel between v. 3 (priest's sin "bringing guilt on the people") and vv. 13-14 (congregation's sin) is textually explicit: the priest's error is already the people's error, by representation. Ramban develops this: the hand-laying on the animal and the blood-sprinkling before the veil are not private acts for the priest; they are national acts performed on his behalf.

Milgrom's critical reading converges: the priestly-congregational parallel is cosmological. Sin radiates pollution in proportion to standing; the high priest's proximity to the inner sanctuary means his sin reaches the same contaminating depth as the whole nation's collective sin. The ritual response is therefore identical. Baruch Levine frames it in terms of ritual topography: inner-court pollution requires inner-court blood, regardless of whether the source is one man or a million.

A Catholic reading presses the typological point. Aquinas in ST III q. 22 develops the priesthood of Christ on the Levitical frame; Christ is both high priest and people, and his sin offering (which he does not need for himself) is offered for the people as their representative. Hebrews 7:26-28 makes the contrast explicit: unlike the Levitical high priest who must offer for his own sins first, Christ "has no need, like those high priests, to offer sacrifices daily." The parallel between v. 3 and vv. 13-14 becomes the typological backdrop for the NT insistence that Christ is a superior priest. Orthodox theology (Zizioulas, *Being as Communion*) develops the ecclesial implication: the priest's state shapes the community's state because the church is not a voluntary association of individuals but a communal body organized through its representatives. Reformed theology (Calvin, and later the Westminster tradition) emphasizes the church's dependence on its leaders' fidelity — a pastoral note with obvious practical weight. Lutheran theology with its priesthood of all believers reads the single-priest representation as provisional, now extended to every believer.

The steelman is the Mary Douglas anthropological reading: hierarchical societies concentrate ritual weight at their apex because the apex holds the structure together. The priest's sin is the nation's sin not because he is morally superior but because structurally the integrity of the cult is routed through him. This is less intuitive because it dispenses with the individualistic moral reading, but it fits the textual structure.

**Where this leaves us:** representation is a load-bearing theological category. What a leader does ritually reaches as far as what the community does collectively. Christian tradition receives this as the grammar of Christ's priesthood; Jewish tradition preserves it as the ongoing weight of leadership. Both traditions agree that sin's reach is not reducible to the sinner's individuality.

**Unresolved question:** if *ḥaṭṭāʾt* is primarily sanctuary-purification (Milgrom) rather than sinner-expiation, what is happening when Hebrews 9:14 says Christ's blood purifies the conscience (*syneidēsis*) from dead works? Is the NT inverting the Levitical logic — moving the blood's reach from the sanctuary to the interior of the worshipper — or fulfilling it along a trajectory already implicit in texts like Psalm 51? The cleanest articulation of how Lev 4 maps onto Hebrews 9 still eludes both traditions.

## The Thread

Three non-obvious cross-references worth following:

- **Numbers 15:22-31** — the Priestly code's most explicit treatment of the *šĕgāgâ*-vs-high-handed distinction. Verse 30's "high hand" sin is placed directly against Lev 4's inadvertent sin, making clear that the sacrificial system has a ceiling. Hebrews 10:26 — "if we go on sinning deliberately after receiving the knowledge of the truth, there no longer remains a sacrifice for sins" — is citing Numbers 15, reading through Leviticus 4.
- **2 Chronicles 29:20-24** — Hezekiah's temple purification includes sin offerings for "all Israel," with the king, the princes, and the assembly all laying hands on goats. The chronicler re-enacts the Lev 4 graduated liability structure in a narrative of national repentance, showing that the legislation was operative in Israel's historical memory as a template for corporate renewal.
- **Ezekiel 40-48** — the visionary restoration temple includes a refined *ḥaṭṭāʾt* practice (Ezek 43:18-27, 45:18-25) that Milgrom argues represents a post-exilic revision of Lev 4 with notable differences: a graduated blood-application that emphasizes inaugural purification of the new altar over ongoing expiation. The relationship between Lev 4 and Ezek 40-48 is one of the major unsolved problems of priestly source criticism.
