# Jeremiah 11: The Covenant Sermon, the First Confession, and the Plot from Home

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

Jeremiah 11 sits at a hinge moment in the prophet's career, and the dating question has unusual stakes. The "words of this covenant" that Jeremiah is commanded to proclaim (vv. 2–3, 6) are most plausibly the rediscovered Torah-scroll of Josiah's reform (2 Kgs 22–23, 2 Chr 34–35), found in the temple in Josiah's eighteenth year — 622 BC by standard chronology. The vocabulary is Deuteronomic: *kol divrei ha-berit* ("all the words of the covenant"), the iron-furnace-of-Egypt formula (v. 4 = Deut 4:20), the milk-and-honey land oath, the curse-formula (*arur ha-ish asher lo yishma*, "cursed is the man who does not hear"). The chapter reads as if Jeremiah, called in 627 in the thirteenth year of Josiah (Jer 1:2), is now five years into his ministry preaching the Deuteronomic Reform itself — a young priest from Anathoth proclaiming the rediscovered law in the streets of Jerusalem.

The Anathoth plot (vv. 18–23) likely belongs to the same period or shortly after. Anathoth is the Levitical city three miles northeast of Jerusalem, in Benjamite territory, traditionally identified with Ras el-Kharrubeh (south of modern Anata). The genealogical record connects Jeremiah's family to Abiathar — the priest whom Solomon banished from the Jerusalem priesthood after the succession crisis (1 Kgs 2:26–27) and exiled to Anathoth, his ancestral land. If Jeremiah descended from Abiathar's line, then his priestly family had been excluded from the Jerusalem temple service for nearly four centuries when Jeremiah was called. Some scholars (Albright, Bright) have argued that the Anathoth plot in Jer 11 reflects a tension specifically over the Josianic Reform's centralization of cult in Jerusalem, which abolished the local Levitical sanctuaries — including any cult activity at Anathoth. On this reading, Jeremiah's hometown plotted against him because his preaching of the Reform threatened their priestly livelihood. The reading is speculative but historically plausible.

Material culture: Late Iron II Judah, the same world as chs. 7–10. The Lachish ostraca (1935–38), contemporary with Jer 34:7 in the late-monarchy context, preserve the language of the period. The Jerusalem bullae corpus — Baruch ben Neriah, Gemariah ben Shaphan, Jehucal ben Shelemiah, Pashhur ben Immer, all from the City of David excavations — places Jeremiah's interlocutors in our hands. The "altars to Baal" multiplied "as many as the streets of Jerusalem" (v. 13) are not Jeremianic hyperbole: archaeological evidence of late-monarchy Yahwism syncretized with Baal-cult and Asherah-veneration is plentiful (Khirbet el-Qom, Kuntillet Ajrud, Arad). Standing-stone altars (*matzevot*), incense altars (the Arad shrine had two), and high-place installations dot the eighth- and seventh-century landscape.

The covenant ceremony language (vv. 3–5) reflects the ANE suzerain-vassal treaty form: identification of suzerain, historical prologue (Egypt deliverance), stipulations, blessings and curses, witness invocation, deposit of treaty document. Hittite second-millennium treaties and seventh-century neo-Assyrian vassal-treaties (the Esarhaddon Succession Treaty, 672 BC, especially) provide formal parallels that Moshe Weinfeld (*Deuteronomy and the Deuteronomic School*, 1972) traced exhaustively. The Esarhaddon treaty's curse formulas are echoed structurally and sometimes verbally in Deuteronomy 28, and Jer 11's "cursed is the man" language stands inside this covenant-treaty tradition.

Authorship and dating remain contested. **Jack R. Lundbom (Anchor Bible 1999–2004)** treats the chapter as substantially Jeremianic. William Holladay (Hermeneia 1986/89) locates the prose sermon (vv. 1–17) in Jeremiah's preaching of the Deuteronomic Reform around 622–609. Robert P. Carroll (OTL 1986) reads it as later Deuteronomistic prose composed by the Source C redactor. William McKane (ICC) treats it as part of the rolling corpus. The "confessions" of Jeremiah (the lament-prayers in 11:18–23, 12:1–6, 15:10–21, 17:14–18, 18:18–23, 20:7–18) have been intensively analyzed since Walther Baumgartner's *Die Klagegedichte des Jeremia* (1917). Their authenticity is generally accepted (with various editorial reworking) even by source-critical scholars who reject most of the prose as redactional.

Key terms: *berit* (בְּרִית, "covenant"), the chapter's central word, used eight times in vv. 2–10; *kol divrei ha-berit* ("all the words of the covenant," vv. 2, 3, 6, 8), a Deuteronomic technical phrase; *qesher* (קֶשֶׁר, "conspiracy," v. 9), with political-treason connotations; *kabes* (כָּבֶשׂ, "lamb," v. 19), the slaughter-image that becomes Christological; *anshei Anatot* (אַנְשֵׁי עֲנָתוֹת, "men of Anathoth," v. 21), the prophet's hometown turned against him.

## Lens 2 — The Literature

The chapter divides cleanly into two parts: the covenant sermon (vv. 1–17) and the first of Jeremiah's confessions (vv. 18–23). The sermon is Deuteronomistic prose, dense with Deuteronomic vocabulary, structured as oracle-with-narrative-frame. The confession is Hebrew lament-poetry, *qina*-adjacent in rhythm, indebted to the Psalms genre of individual lament (Pss 3, 4, 5, 6, 13, 22, etc.) but with prophetic-vocational specificity.

The covenant sermon (vv. 1–17) develops in five movements: command to proclaim (vv. 1–5), historical retrospect (vv. 6–8), present apostasy named as conspiracy (vv. 9–13), prohibition of intercession (v. 14), and the olive-tree oracle (vv. 15–17). The structure performs a covenant lawsuit (*riv*-pattern, see Mic 6, Hos 4): summons, recital of the covenant relationship, indictment, sentence. The final "olive tree" image (v. 16) is striking — Yahweh once called Israel a "green olive tree, beautiful with good fruit," which Yahweh now sets fire to. The image will be picked up by Paul in Rom 11's olive-tree allegory, with significant theological consequences.

The first confession (vv. 18–23) is one of the clearest examples of the Jeremianic lament genre. Brueggemann calls it "the inaugural confession" because it sets the pattern: revelation by Yahweh that the prophet's enemies are plotting (v. 18), recall of the prophet's innocent unknowing (v. 19, *ke-keves alluf yuval li-tevoach*, "I was like a gentle lamb led to the slaughter"), the enemies' actual plot in their own words (v. 19b, *nashchitah etz be-lachmo*, the "destroy the tree with its fruit" line — note the parallel to v. 16's olive tree), and the appeal for divine vengeance (v. 20). The structure recurs in chs. 12, 15, 17, 18, 20.

The lamb-led-to-slaughter image (v. 19) is the most famous single line in the chapter. It is verbally close — though not identical — to Isa 53:7 (*ke-seh la-tevach yuval*). The relationship between the two has been debated since Origen. The Hebrew uses different sheep-vocabulary (*keves* in Jer 11, *seh* in Isa 53), but the slaughter-image and the innocence-theme overlap. Both passages were read Christologically by patristic and medieval exegetes; both became central to the early Christian articulation of suffering-servant theology, with Acts 8:32 quoting Isa 53 (not Jer 11) as the Ethiopian eunuch's text.

Devices: leitwort (*shema*, "hear," appears in vv. 2, 3, 4, 6, 7, 8, 10, 11, 14 — the chapter is a sustained meditation on hearing and not-hearing). Inclusio: vv. 1–5 set up the covenant call to "hear," and vv. 18–23 close with Yahweh "hearing" the plot against the prophet who tried to make Judah hear. Sound-pun: the covenant is *kol divrei* and the conspiracy is *qesher* — the people refuse the words of the covenant and *make their own words* into a plot.

Genre note: the prohibition of intercession (v. 14, *al titpallel be'ad ha-am ha-zeh*, "do not pray for this people") echoes 7:16 and 14:11. This is unusual in prophetic literature — Moses, Samuel, and most prophets intercede for the people; Jeremiah is forbidden to. The triple repetition of the prohibition makes it one of the chapter's structural anchors.

Textual notes: the LXX of ch. 11 is generally close to the MT, without the kind of large divergence we saw in ch. 10. The chapter is comparatively stable in transmission.

Canonical position: the chapter sits at the close of "Scroll One" of Stulman's structural reading, the doom-announcement section (chs. 1–25), and inaugurates the confessions sub-genre that runs through the book.

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

Backward in the canon: Deuteronomy 27–30's covenant-curse-and-blessing structure is the explicit substrate of vv. 3–8. The "iron furnace of Egypt" formula (v. 4) is from Deut 4:20. The milk-and-honey-land oath (v. 5) is the Pentateuch's foundational promise (Exod 3:8, 17; Deut 6:3; etc.). The Anathoth-Abiathar genealogy (1 Kgs 2:26–27) sets up the hometown rejection. The Mosaic intercessor-tradition (Exod 32:11–14, Num 14:13–19, Deut 9:18–29) is the background against which Yahweh's prohibition of Jeremiah's intercession (v. 14) reads as covenant withdrawal.

Forward in the canon: Romans 11's olive-tree allegory grows directly from Jer 11:16's olive-tree oracle, with Paul reading the *cutting off* and *grafting in* of branches as the redemptive-historical sequence of Israel and the Gentiles. Hebrews 8's new covenant chapter is unintelligible without Jer 11's broken-covenant prelude. The prophetic suffering-servant tradition that culminates in Isaiah 53 takes one of its concrete shapes in Jer 11:19's lamb-image. The Lukan "no prophet is acceptable in his hometown" (Lk 4:24) reflects the Anathoth pattern.

**Jewish trajectory.** Sirach 49:6–7 places Jeremiah in the canonical sequence. Bavli Bava Batra 14b–15a discusses the book's authorship. The rabbinic tradition treats the Anathoth plot as evidence of the prophet's authentic call: *if his own town rejected him, he must be a true prophet* — an inverted criterion paralleled in the rabbinic tradition about Hillel and the prozbul. Targum Jonathan paraphrases the Aramaic with paraphrastic expansions of the covenant language. Rashi reads v. 19's "tree with its fruit" as referring to Jeremiah's life (the tree) and his prophetic words (the fruit). Radak (David Kimhi) attends to the historical context of Josiah's reform. Ibn Ezra notes the linguistic-grammatical features of the lament. Abrabanel, writing in late-medieval Iberia and post-expulsion Italy, reads the Anathoth plot through the prism of internal Jewish division — the prophet's worst enemies are not the imperial powers but the community closest to him.

**Christian trajectory.** Patristic: **Origen's *Homilies on Jeremiah*** reads v. 19 as a Christological prophecy, the lamb led to slaughter prefiguring Christ; Justin Martyr in his *Dialogue with Trypho* 72 cites Jer 11:19 alongside Isa 53 as evidence of crucifixion-prophecy, accusing the Jews of having removed v. 19 from their Scripture (a textual claim that does not survive critical examination — the verse is in MT and LXX both). **Jerome's *Commentary on Jeremiah*** discusses the Christological reading and the textual tradition. Tertullian (*Adv. Jud.*) and Cyprian use the verse extensively. Medieval: Aquinas cites the chapter in *Summa* III on the priesthood of Christ; Rupert of Deutz reads it Christologically throughout. Reformation: **Calvin's *Lectures on Jeremiah* (1563)** reads the covenant sermon as central to his covenant theology — the *foedus gratiae* runs through both Testaments, and Jer 11's broken covenant prepares for the new covenant of Jer 31. Calvin reads v. 19 as a true Christological type but is more cautious than the patristic tradition.

**Where they diverge.** Patristic and medieval Christian reading takes v. 19 as direct prophecy of Christ. Reformed reading takes it as type rather than direct prophecy. Modern critical reading (Carroll, McKane, Holladay) takes it as the prophet's autobiographical lament with no inherent Christological content. Jewish reading takes it as the prophet's lament over his own near-martyrdom at the hands of his hometown. The contemporary trauma-informed reading (Kathleen O'Connor, *Jeremiah: Pain and Promise*, 2011) reads the confession as testimony to the psychological cost of prophetic vocation — the lamb who *did not know* he was being plotted against is the figure of every prophet whose innocence is shattered by betrayal.

## Lens 4 — The Theological Intent

**Tension 1: The Anathoth plot and prophetic suffering.** The patristic Christological typology has the longer pedigree — Justin, Origen, Jerome, Cyprian all read the Anathoth plot as type of Christ's rejection by his own people, with Lk 4:24 as the explicit Gospel link. The typology works on multiple levels: the prophet's hometown rejects him; the prophet is innocent; the prophet's enemies plot to "cut him off from the land of the living, that his name be remembered no more" (v. 19) — a phrase that could be Christological (cf. Acts 3:14–15), or could be a generic ANE curse formula, depending on reading. Justin's claim that v. 19 was originally a "messianic" verse the Jews removed is historically false but theologically illustrative: the early church saw Christ in Jeremiah, and Jeremiah's suffering was, for them, a rehearsal of the Cross.

The modern critical reading (Carroll, McKane, Holladay) treats vv. 18–23 as autobiographical lament reworked by Deuteronomistic editors to legitimate prophetic vocation. The chapter's purpose, on this view, is not Christological but ecclesiological — to validate the suffering of the prophetic office in the post-exilic community. The Anathoth plot is a real historical event that gave the Deuteronomistic editors a paradigm for how true prophets are received: by rejection, even from their own.

The trauma-informed reading (O'Connor, Stulman) takes a third path: the confession is testimony to the psychological cost of speaking truth in a community that rejects it. Jeremiah is not a Christological figure but a real human being whose vocation cost him his home. The contemporary application is to whistleblowers, dissidents, prophets within communities that reject them — and to the recovery work of attending to the wound such rejection inflicts.

Steelman the Christological typology fairly: the New Testament reads the Hebrew Bible Christologically as a matter of theological interpretation, and Jer 11:19's verbal closeness to Isa 53:7 is not coincidence but pattern. The lamb-image is part of a sustained scriptural figure — innocent victim, silent suffering, redemption through unrequited love — that the church reads coming-to-fullness in Christ. To dismiss the typology as anachronism is to refuse the way the Christian canon itself reads. Steelman the trauma reading: the typology can become a way of skipping over the historical Jeremiah's actual pain. If we read every prophetic suffering as type-of-Christ, we may miss what the prophet was actually feeling. Both readings together — typology *and* trauma-honesty — preserve the chapter's full force.

Where this leaves us: typology is honest reading inside Christian theology, but typology that erases the historical prophet betrays the chapter. The lamb in v. 19 is Jeremiah, who was nearly killed by his hometown — and who, the church believes, prefigures Christ, who *was* killed by his.

**Tension 2: The prohibition of intercession.** Verse 14 — *do not pray for this people, or lift up a cry or prayer on their behalf, for I will not listen* — is one of the most theologically disturbing lines in the prophets. It is repeated twice more (7:16, 14:11). What does it mean that prophetic prayer is *closed* against the people? The Mosaic tradition is built on the opposite: Moses interceding on Sinai (Exod 32), Samuel interceding for Israel (1 Sam 12:23 — "far be it from me that I should sin against the Lord by ceasing to pray for you"), the priestly office defined as standing between God and people. Jeremiah's prohibition is the systematic reversal of this tradition.

The Catholic intercessory tradition reads the prohibition as a *historical* limit case, not a permanent norm. The Marian and saintly intercession that runs through the church is grounded in the persistent biblical pattern of prayer-for-others (Job 42:8 — Job prays for his friends; Paul's intercessions throughout the epistles; the church's *oratio fidelium*). The Reformed reading treats the prohibition more sharply: there are points at which divine providence has determined judgment, and prayer cannot reverse it. Calvin in the *Institutes* (3.20) treats prayer as primarily aligning the worshiper with God's will rather than changing God's mind, and the Jeremiah-prohibition fits this framework — prayer that contradicts divine decree is not heard. The Jewish *teshuvah* tradition is interesting here: even in the worst-case scenarios, the rabbinic tradition holds out the possibility of repentance reopening the divine ear. Maimonides (Hilkhot Teshuvah) treats teshuvah as theologically irreversible — it always reopens the relationship — which seems to contradict Jer 11:14. The rabbinic reading typically treats the prohibition as conditional on continued idolatry; if the people repent, the prohibition lifts.

Steelman the absolute-prohibition reading: there are situations in which a community has so corroded its covenant relationship that intercession no longer functions, because the people on whose behalf the prayer would be offered no longer exist as covenant partners. The late-monarchy Judean community had gone after other gods so thoroughly that the *people* in any covenantal sense had dissolved; what remained was a population, not a community of faith. The prohibition is not divine indifference but covenantal honesty — the relationship that intercession assumes is no longer there to be addressed.

Steelman the conditional-prohibition reading: the prohibition is rhetorically intense but theologically conditional. The whole prophetic ministry is the offer of repentance; the prohibition is the warning that if repentance does not come, intercession cannot save. The repeated prohibition (7:16, 11:14, 14:11) is not three iron walls but three increasingly urgent warnings. Even in Lamentations — the book that follows Jer 11's logic to its end — prayer continues, and the Tisha B'Av tradition keeps praying.

Where this leaves us: the prohibition of intercession is real and theologically serious. It cannot be domesticated into a cheerful tradition of always-pray-for-everyone. But it sits inside a canon that ends with a new covenant promise (Jer 31), and the larger arc preserves intercession's possibility even when this chapter forbids it.

The unresolved question that lands hard: if Yahweh's love for Israel is steadfast (the chesed of 9:24), then what kind of love forbids prayer on the beloved's behalf? Either the prohibition is rhetorical hyperbole, or divine love operates by a logic that includes refusal — a love that says *no* to the request to keep loving in the same way. The prophetic tradition seems to say the latter, and it is harder to absorb than most preaching admits.

## The Thread

Deuteronomy 27–30 — the explicit substrate of vv. 1–8, the covenant blessing-and-curse pattern that Jer 11 is now applying. 1 Kings 2:26–27 — the Abiathar exile to Anathoth that explains why Jeremiah's hometown might have a particular grievance against the Jerusalem priesthood whose cause Jeremiah is now preaching. Romans 11:17–24 — Paul's olive-tree allegory, growing directly out of Jer 11:16, where the cutting off and grafting in of branches becomes the redemptive-historical figure for Israel and the Gentiles. Hebrews 8:8–13 — the new covenant of Jer 31:31–34 cited in full, intelligible only as the *answer* to the broken covenant of Jer 11. Lamentations as a whole — the book Jer 11's broken covenant produces, the lament-tradition that the prohibition of intercession (v. 14) does not finally close.
