# Acts 4: The Sanhedrin Trial, the Cornerstone, and the Common Purse

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

Acts 4 carries us from the public space of Solomon's Portico into the chambers of the Sanhedrin — the supreme Jewish judicial-religious council of Roman Judea. The arrest party of 4:1 is precisely calibrated to first-century temple politics: **the priests** (the temple priesthood, dominated by Sadducean families), **the captain of the temple** (***stratēgos tou hierou***, στρατηγὸς τοῦ ἱεροῦ — the Hebrew *segan ha-kohanim*, the second-ranking temple official, with police authority over the temple precincts), and **the Sadducees** (the priestly-aristocratic party who, per Josephus *Antiquities* 18.1.4 and Mark 12:18, denied resurrection). The Sadducean annoyance at "**proclaiming in Jesus the resurrection from the dead**" (4:2) thus has a precise ideological edge: the apostolic preaching attacks both the legitimacy of the priestly establishment that had executed Jesus and the Sadducean denial of resurrection in general.

The high-priestly family named in 4:6 is the Annas dynasty whose ascendancy dominated Judean politics from c. 6 to 65 CE. **Annas** (Hebrew *Hanan*, *Ananus*) was high priest 6–15 CE; deposed by the prefect Valerius Gratus, he retained de facto influence as patriarch of the family — Josephus *Antiquities* 20.9.1 documents that five of his sons and his son-in-law **Caiaphas** (high priest c. 18–36 CE) all held the office. The **Caiaphas ossuary**, discovered in 1990 in the Peace Forest south of Jerusalem with the inscription *Yehosef bar Qayafa* ("Joseph son of Caiaphas"), almost certainly belonged to this Caiaphas — one of the most secure archaeological identifications of a New Testament figure. The "**John and Alexander**" of 4:6 are otherwise unattested, though some scholars (Bruce, Witherington) have proposed identifications with later Annas family members named in Josephus.

The Sanhedrin's composition — **rulers, elders, scribes** — corresponds to the three constituencies described in Mishnah *Sanhedrin* (priestly aristocracy, lay nobility, and Pharisaic scribal experts), though the historical reliability of the rabbinic Sanhedrin description for the first century has been debated since **Hugo Mantel's** *Studies in the History of the Sanhedrin* (1961). Joachim Jeremias's *Jerusalem in the Time of Jesus* and Lawrence Schiffman's *Who Was a Jew?* lay out the contemporary scholarly consensus: a body of seventy-one members, meeting in the Chamber of Hewn Stone (***lishkat ha-gazit***) on the south side of the Temple Mount, with capital jurisdiction limited under Roman administration.

Greek terms: ***akrogōniaios*** (ἀκρογωνιαῖος, 4:11), "**cornerstone**," draws on Psalm 118:22 LXX and stands in a developed early Christian Christological tradition that includes Mark 12:10, Matthew 21:42, Luke 20:17, 1 Peter 2:6–7, and Ephesians 2:20 — the **cornerstone Christology** is one of the most widely attested early Christian readings of a single Old Testament text. ***Onoma*** (ὄνομα, 4:7, 10, 12, 17, 18, 30), "**name**," is the chapter's controlling word — the eight occurrences enact the apostolic claim that the *name* of Jesus is the operative locus of salvation, healing, and authority. ***Parrhēsia*** (παρρησία, 4:13, 29, 31), "**boldness**," is a classical Greek civic virtue — the right of the free citizen to speak frankly in the assembly — that the apostles exercise as ex-fishermen ("**uneducated, common men**," ***agrammatoi kai idiōtai***, ἀγράμματοι καὶ ἰδιῶται, 4:13). Marguerat in *The First Christian Historian* notes the deliberate Lukan irony: agrammatoi yet exegete; *idiōtai* yet bold.

Textually, Codex Bezae expands at 4:18 (adding "to whom there is salvation through anyone other than Christ") and at 4:31 (adding "with boldness to anyone who wished to believe") — engage **Bruce Metzger's** *Textual Commentary* and **Eldon Epp's** *Theological Tendency of Codex Bezae*.

## Lens 2 — The Literature

Acts 4 enacts the Lukan literary architecture in unusually compressed form: arrest (4:1–4), trial-and-speech (4:5–22), prayer (4:23–31), and **second summary statement** (4:32–37). The chapter is structurally paired with Acts 5:17–42, which reprises the trial-arrest-prayer-summary pattern with Sanhedrin escalation, and the doubling itself is one of Tannehill's structural keys in *The Narrative Unity of Luke-Acts* vol. 2.

Peter's third speech (4:8–12) is the shortest of the major Petrine kerygmatic addresses but also the most concentrated. Its citation of **Psalm 118:22** ("the stone that was rejected by you, the builders, which has become the cornerstone") follows Jesus's own use of the same text in the synoptic tradition (Mark 12:10–11 and parallels) — one of the strongest cases that Acts preserves pre-Lukan tradition (engage **C.H. Dodd's** *The Apostolic Preaching* and **According to the Scriptures*; Marshall, Bruce, Witherington side with Dodd, while Conzelmann, Haenchen, and Pervo treat Psalm 118 here as Lukan redaction). The **"no other name"** declaration of 4:12 is one of the most theologically loaded sentences in the Lukan corpus and has become a controlling text for Christian exclusivism.

The **community prayer** of 4:24–30 is structurally remarkable: a citation of **Psalm 2:1–2** (LXX) followed by an applied historical interpretation (4:27–28 — Herod, Pilate, Gentiles, peoples of Israel) and a petition for boldness and signs. **Loveday Alexander's** *Acts in Its Ancient Literary Context* analyzes this as a cognate of the synagogal *piyyut* tradition — Psalm-citation followed by interpretive application — adapted for early Christian liturgy. Whether this prayer preserves an actual liturgical formula of the early Jerusalem church (Marshall, Witherington) or is Lukan composition (Conzelmann, Haenchen, Pervo) remains debated.

The **second summary** of 4:32–37 expands the first (2:42–47) with new emphases: the unity of "**one heart and soul**" (***mia kardia kai psychē***, μία καρδία καὶ ψυχή — drawing on Greco-Roman friendship ideal of *koinōnia*; Aristotle, *Nicomachean Ethics* 9.8 and Cicero *De Amicitia*), the apostolic "great power" giving testimony to resurrection, the absence of "needy persons" (***endeēs***, ἐνδεής, 4:34) — explicitly echoing Deut 15:4 LXX's covenantal-blessing formula. Marguerat in *The First Christian Historian* reads the summary as a deliberate **Lukan literary topos** that paints early Christianity in the colors of both Greco-Roman utopian community and Mosaic covenantal blessing. **Justo González's** *Faith and Wealth* analyzes the economic-theological substance of this summary.

The introduction of **Barnabas** (4:36–37 — Joseph called Barnabas, "son of encouragement," a Levite from Cyprus) is a Lukan literary signal: figures who will become major are typically introduced first in a small narrative episode (cf. Saul at 7:58, John Mark at 12:12). Barnabas will reappear at 9:27 (introducing Paul to the apostles), 11:22–26 (sent to Antioch), 13:1–14:28 (the first missionary journey), and 15:36–39 (the parting from Paul).

The **Western Text** of Acts 4 expands at 4:31 (the trembling of the place is enhanced) and elsewhere; Codex Bezae also has a subtler heightening of Pilate's responsibility in 4:27.

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

**Jewish interpretive trajectories**. Acts 4 is one of the chapters where contemporary post-Holocaust Jewish-Christian scholarship has worked carefully. Adele Reinhartz's writing on Lukan-Jewish polemic and **Amy-Jill Levine's** *Jewish Annotated New Testament* both press Christian readers to see the Sanhedrin trial as **inner-Jewish prophetic conflict** in the mode of Jeremiah's confrontations with the priestly establishment, not as simple "Jews-versus-Christians." The high-priestly family's resistance is to a movement still itself wholly Jewish, attending the temple at the hours of prayer (3:1) and continuing in the apostolic teaching-and-prayers pattern that emerged from second-temple Judaism. **Lawrence Schiffman**'s *Who Was a Jew?* on the **birkat ha-minim** is essential context: the synagogue-curse against heretics, which formalized Jewish-Christian separation, lay decades in the future of the events narrated. Daniel Boyarin's *Border Lines* analyzes how the boundary between "Christian" and "Jew" was being negotiated through such episodes long before either category was clearly fixed. Pamela Eisenbaum and **Matthew Thiessen** *A Jewish Paul* extend the work into the Pauline corpus.

**Christian patristic reception**. **John Chrysostom's** Homilies 10–11 on Acts treat chapter 4 with characteristic emphasis on the apostles' boldness as a model for Christian witness under persecution; Chrysostom reads "uneducated, common men" not as deficiency but as proof that the power is from God. **Origen** in *Contra Celsum* defends the apostolic miracles against pagan critique by citing Acts 4. **Augustine** in *De Civitate Dei* develops the cornerstone Christology of 4:11 as the unifying principle of the church-as-city. **Bede the Venerable's** *Expositio Actuum Apostolorum* on chapter 4 offers the most extensive medieval treatment, working both the Christological and ecclesiological themes.

The **early monastic reception** of 4:32–35 is one of the chapter's most important interpretive trajectories. **Basil of Caesarea's** *Long Rules* explicitly grounds cenobitic monasticism in Acts 4 and 2, as does the **Augustinian Rule** (engage Augustine's *Sermon 356* on the apostolic life, which became foundational for the Augustinian canons regular). The text is the controlling biblical warrant for Catholic religious life — Benedictine, Franciscan, Dominican — and remains so today.

**Reformation**. **Calvin's** *Commentary on Acts* on chapter 4 emphasizes (a) the Christological exclusivity of 4:12 against late-medieval theological pluralism, (b) the legitimacy of civil disobedience grounded in 4:19–20, and (c) the descriptive-not-prescriptive reading of 4:32–37 (private property is preserved; the practice was voluntary). **Luther's** lectures on Acts develop the *parrhēsia* theme. Reformed resistance theology — engage **Theodore Beza's** *Right of Magistrates* (1574) and the broader Huguenot resistance literature — explicitly grounded its theology of resistance to tyrannical authority in Acts 4:19 and 5:29.

**20th-c. theological reception**. **Karl Barth** in *Church Dogmatics* IV/3.2 reads Acts 4:12 carefully, defending the Christological exclusivity while warning against converting it into a Christian triumphalism that makes the church the dispenser of salvation. **Dietrich Bonhoeffer's** *Ethics* and *Letters and Papers from Prison* draw repeatedly on Acts 4–5 in formulating his theology of resistance to Nazi tyranny — "we must obey God rather than men" (5:29) was one of the foundational texts of the Confessing Church's *Barmen Declaration* (1934). **John Howard Yoder's** *The Politics of Jesus* uses Acts 4 to ground his nonviolent-political reading of the gospel.

**Contemporary commentaries**. F.F. Bruce's NICNT, C.K. Barrett's two-volume ICC, Joseph Fitzmyer's Anchor Bible, Beverly Gaventa's Abingdon, Luke Timothy Johnson's Sacra Pagina, Craig Keener's encyclopedic four volumes, Ben Witherington's socio-rhetorical commentary, Eckhard Schnabel's ZECNT, Hans Conzelmann's Hermeneia, Ernst Haenchen, Richard Pervo's late-dating Hermeneia, David Peterson's Pillar, Daniel Marguerat, and I. Howard Marshall's TNTC anchor the contemporary discussion.

**Liberation, feminist, and postcolonial** readings press 4:32–37 and 4:12 in different directions. **Justo González's** *Faith and Wealth* and *Acts (Belief)* read 4:34's "no needy person among them" as the church's economic charter; **Allan Boesak**'s South African readings ground anti-apartheid theology in Acts 4–5; **Gustavo Gutiérrez's** *A Theology of Liberation* draws on the chapter's economic radicalism. Catholic social teaching from **Leo XIII's** *Rerum Novarum* (1891) through **John Paul II's** *Sollicitudo Rei Socialis* (1987) and *Laborem Exercens* (1981) and **Francis's** *Laudato Si'* (2015) engages the apostolic-community model. The **Anabaptist** tradition — Hutterites, Bruderhof, contemporary new-monastic communities — reads 4:32–35 as continuingly normative; engage the Schleitheim Confession (1527) and the foundational works of **Eberhard Arnold**.

## Lens 4 — The Theological Intent

**Tension 1: The exclusivity of 4:12 — "no other name."** "**There is salvation in no one else, for there is no other name under heaven given among men by which we must be saved**" is one of the New Testament's controlling exclusivist texts (cf. John 14:6, 1 Tim 2:5). Four positions:

*Christian exclusivism / restrictivism*: salvation is available only through explicit faith in Jesus Christ; those who have not heard or who reject the gospel are not saved; engage **D.A. Carson's** *The Gagging of God*, **John Piper's** *Let the Nations Be Glad*, the broader Reformed-evangelical missional tradition, and the **Lausanne Covenant** (1974). The strongest version of this rests on the explicit textual force of 4:12, the missional urgency of the New Testament, and the theological argument that Christ's atonement is uniquely necessary.

*Christian inclusivism*: salvation is **objectively** through Christ alone but **subjectively** available to those who do not explicitly know him through general revelation, conscience, or implicit faith; engage **Vatican II's** *Lumen Gentium* 16 and *Nostra Aetate* (1965), **Karl Rahner's** "anonymous Christians" (*Theological Investigations*), much of contemporary Catholic theology, and evangelical voices including **John Stott** (in his later writings) and **Clark Pinnock's** *A Wideness in God's Mercy*. The strongest version rests on the universal-scope claims of 1 Tim 2:4 ("God desires all people to be saved"), the Cornelius narrative (Acts 10:34–35), and the moral-theological argument about those who never heard.

*Christian pluralism*: the major world religions are alternative paths to salvation; Christ is the way Christians follow but not the only way; engage **John Hick's** *An Interpretation of Religion*, **Paul Knitter's** *No Other Name?*, and the broader pluralist theology of religions. The strongest version rests on the moral and pastoral problem of religious diversity, the empirical evidence of saintliness across traditions, and the theological argument against tribalism. Most evangelical and traditional Catholic readings reject this position.

*Particularist-with-mystery*: Christ is the way, but the scope of his work and the mode of its application remain in some measure mysterious; engage **Lesslie Newbigin's** *The Gospel in a Pluralist Society*, *The Open Secret*, and the missional-theological tradition that follows. The strongest version preserves Christological exclusivity while resisting the move from "Christ is the only way" to "we know who is saved."

The strongest version of each: exclusivism takes seriously 4:12's textual force; inclusivism takes seriously the universal-scope claims; pluralism takes seriously the moral-pastoral pressure; the particularist-with-mystery position takes seriously both the textual claim and the limits of human eschatological knowledge.

*Where this leaves us*: 4:12 cannot be evaded; its force is precisely Christological — salvation is uniquely accomplished in Christ's name. What 4:12 does not entail is the further claim that we know exhaustively who is or is not saved; it makes a claim about the means of salvation (the name of Jesus) without specifying the application's scope. The text supports Christological exclusivity but does not require restrictivism in its narrowest form.

**Tension 2: Civil disobedience and "we must obey God rather than men."** "**Whether it is right in the sight of God to listen to you rather than to God, you must judge, for we cannot but speak of what we have seen and heard**" (4:19–20) and its sharper restatement at 5:29 ("we must obey God rather than men") have been the foundational New Testament texts for Christian civil-disobedience theology. Three positions:

*Lutheran two-kingdoms / submission-default*: civil authority is divinely ordered (Rom 13:1–7); resistance is justified only when civil authority commands explicit denial of the gospel; engage Luther's *On Temporal Authority* and the dominant strand of Lutheran political theology.

*Reformed resistance theology*: lesser magistrates may resist tyrannical higher authority; private citizens may disobey but ordinarily not actively resist; engage **Theodore Beza's** *Right of Magistrates*, the Huguenot *Vindiciae Contra Tyrannos*, John Knox's writings, and the broader Reformed tradition that flowered in the American and Dutch resistances.

*Anabaptist-pacifist / radical witness*: the church's witness is fundamentally non-violent and non-political; Acts 4–5 models patient witness and willingness to suffer rather than resistance through political means; engage Yoder's *The Politics of Jesus*, the Anabaptist tradition from Schleitheim onward, and **Bonhoeffer**'s qualified development.

The strongest version of each: the Lutheran position takes seriously the New Testament's broader teaching on civil submission; the Reformed position takes seriously the historic pattern of legitimate resistance to tyranny; the Anabaptist position takes seriously the apostolic refusal to use violence even in self-defense.

*Where this leaves us*: Acts 4:19–20 and 5:29 do not provide a complete political theology but do establish a non-negotiable: when human authority commands disobedience to God, the believer obeys God. The mode of disobedience (witness-and-suffering rather than violent resistance) is exemplified by the apostles themselves.

*One unresolved question*: does the absence of any apostolic engagement with the structures of Roman or Jewish power — beyond bearing witness when interrogated — constitute Luke's complete political theology, or is it a function of the early Jerusalem community's specific situation? Acts will not resolve this; the church has lived in the question ever since.

## The Thread

The cornerstone Christology of 4:11 reaches back to **Psalm 118:22** and forward to **1 Peter 2:6–7** (the most extensive cornerstone development) and **Ephesians 2:20** (cornerstone-and-foundation ecclesiology). The "**no other name**" of 4:12 reaches forward to **Romans 10:9–13** (Paul's universal call) and **Philippians 2:9–11** (every-knee-and-tongue confession). The civil-disobedience principle of 4:19–20 reaches forward to its sharper restatement at **Acts 5:29** and connects to **Daniel 3** (Shadrach, Meshach, Abednego) and **Daniel 6** (Daniel's prayer). The communalism of 4:32–37 reaches back to the first summary at **Acts 2:42–47** and forward to its shadow at **Acts 5:1–11** (Ananias and Sapphira) and to the famine relief of **Acts 11:27–30** and **Acts 24:17** (Paul's collection). Flag the introduction of **Barnabas** (4:36–37) — Luke's literary signal for a figure who will be central in chapters 9, 11, 13–15.
