# Romans 13: The Sword, the Tax, and the Coming Day

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

Romans 13 was dictated by Paul to Tertius in Corinth in the winter of 56–57 CE, in the third or fourth year of Nero's reign (Nero became emperor in October 54 CE and would not begin the persecution of Roman Christians until after the great fire of July 64 CE, recounted by Tacitus in *Annals* 15.44). The political horizon of the chapter is therefore not the bloody Nero of memory but the early "good Nero" — what Romans later called the *quinquennium Neronis*, the supposedly virtuous first five years of his reign under the tutelage of Seneca and Burrus. The state apparatus Paul writes about — the magistrates, the *publicani*, the urban prefect's *cohortes urbanae*, the lictors with their *fasces* — was not yet drenched in Christian blood. Robert Jewett's Hermeneia commentary argues persuasively that the chapter responds to a very specific political situation in Rome in 56–57 CE: a tax-revolt unrest among the urban *plebs* over the abuses of the indirect-tax farmers (the *publicani*), a crisis Tacitus reports at *Annals* 13.50–51, when in 58 CE Nero would actually consider abolishing all *vectigalia* (indirect taxes) before being talked out of it by his advisers. Paul, writing the year before, would have known that Roman believers — many of them artisans and freedpersons in the *insulae* of Trastevere and the Aventine — were caught between their resentment of the *portoria* (customs duties) and their precarious status as a recently re-formed community after the Claudian expulsion of 49 CE.

That expulsion is the second great political fact behind the chapter. Suetonius, *Life of Claudius* 25.4, records that "since the Jews were constantly making disturbances at the instigation of Chrestus, he expelled them from Rome" — almost certainly a garbled reference to intra-synagogue conflict over Christ. When Claudius died in 54 CE the edict lapsed, and Jewish believers like Priscilla and Aquila returned (Acts 18:2; Rom 16:3) to find a Gentile-majority house-church network that had matured in their absence. The Roman Christians Paul addresses in chapter 13 were therefore exquisitely sensitive to political visibility. One synagogue brawl, one tax riot, one report to the urban prefect that "those Chrestiani are at it again" could provoke a second expulsion. The chapter's pragmatic counsel — pay the *phoros*, pay the *telos*, give *phobos* and *timē* — is delivered to a community whose continued existence in the capital depended on not giving the authorities a pretext.

Three Greek terms govern the political vocabulary of 13:1–7. ***Exousia*** (ἐξουσία), "authority," appears five times in the first three verses and refers in Hellenistic Greek both to delegated political power and to the cosmic powers (cf. Rom 8:38, where *exousiai* are among the things that cannot separate from God's love — a deliberate semantic field Paul exploits). ***Hai exousiai hyperechousai*** (αἱ ἐξουσίαι ὑπερέχουσαι), "the higher / governing authorities" (13:1), is a Greco-Roman commonplace for civic magistrates; the participle *hyperechōn* is the standard term for officials of superior rank. ***Diakonos*** (διάκονος, 13:4), "servant" or "minister," is here applied to the pagan magistrate — startling, since the same word will name Phoebe in 16:1 and Christ himself in 15:8. Paul places the Roman official, Phoebe of Cenchreae, and Jesus Messiah in the same lexical category of God-deputed service. ***Machaira*** (μάχαιρα, 13:4), "sword," refers not to the legionary *gladius* of foreign war but to the magistrate's *ius gladii*, the power of capital punishment — a technical term for the proconsul's or prefect's coercive authority. ***Phoros*** (φόρος, 13:7), "tax," is direct tribute paid by provincials; ***telos*** (τέλος), in the same verse, is indirect customs duty (*portoria*). The pairing is exact and reflects intimate knowledge of Roman fiscal taxonomy.

The "armor of light" image (13:12) draws on standard Roman military equipment — the *lorica* breastplate, the *galea* helmet — but transposes it into apocalyptic register, where the coming Day is the Day of the Lord. Material culture confirms the metaphor's force: the Roman believer would have seen Praetorian Guards in full armor daily on the Palatine and the Castra Praetoria.

## Lens 2 — The Literature

The chapter is the second half of Paul's parenetic block (12:1–13:14, with chs 14–15 extending into a specific application). Structurally it divides into three units: civic obligations (13:1–7), the love command as the law's fulfillment (13:8–10), and the eschatological exhortation (13:11–14). The first unit is the most rhetorically distinctive: it is composed in the third person ("let every person be subject," "for he is God's servant"), in contrast to the second-person plural that dominates chs 12 and 14. James Dunn and others have noted this as evidence the unit may be a discrete piece of Pauline tradition deployed here, not improvised — possibly drawing on diaspora-Jewish wisdom about the gentile state (compare Wis 6:1–3; Sir 10:4; Prov 8:15–16; the prayer for the kingdom in m. Avot 3:2 attributed to R. Hanina, "pray for the welfare of the government").

Rhetorically, 13:1–7 is built on a chain of explanatory clauses (gar — "for" — appears six times in seven verses), giving the unit an axiomatic quality more typical of moral catechesis than letter-prose. Käsemann called it a *Schultradition*, a school tradition. The shift in 13:8 to direct second-person address ("Owe no one anything") is so abrupt that some have wondered whether 13:1–7 is an interpolation; the textual evidence, however, is unanimous in its inclusion in every manuscript and in every patristic citation. The wordplay on debt (*opheilete* in 13:7 and 13:8) is the hinge: the believer pays Caesar what is owed in coin, and pays the neighbor what is owed in love. Both are *opheilai*, but only the second is unending.

The love-as-fulfillment of the law passage (13:8–10) cites the second-table Decalogue commandments in an order — adultery, murder, theft, coveting — that follows the LXX of Deut 5 (where adultery precedes murder, against the Hebrew order Exod 20). Paul is using Greek scripture, as expected for a letter to Greek-reading Roman believers. The summary citation of Lev 19:18b ("you shall love your neighbor as yourself") closely parallels Jesus' summary in Mark 12:31 / Matt 22:39, and may reflect either Pauline knowledge of Jesus tradition or a shared rabbinic distillation (Hillel's golden rule in b. Shabbat 31a is a near contemporary).

The eschatological close (13:11–14) deploys the *kairos*–*hōra* doublet, the well-known light/darkness binary (cf. 1 Thess 5:4–8; the Qumran *Community Rule* 1QS 3:13–4:26 with its sons of light and sons of darkness), and the dressing imagery (*endysometha*, *endysasthe*) that Paul uses for baptismal incorporation into Christ (Gal 3:27). The eschatology is intense: the night is *proekopsen*, "far advanced," the day has *ēngiken*, "drawn near." Paul writes as a man who expected the parousia within a generation.

There is no major textual variant inside Romans 13 itself. The doxology problem (16:25–27) does not bear on this chapter, though the Marcionite recension that ended at 14:23 means Marcion's Romans contained chapter 13 in full. The earliest substantial Romans witness, P46 (c. 200 CE), preserves chapter 13 essentially as the modern critical text.

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

The canonical arc of Romans 13:1–7 stretches both backward into the Jewish theology of Gentile rule and forward into two thousand years of fiercely contested Christian political theology.

**The Jewish trajectory.** The chapter has deep roots in the Hebrew Bible's theology of pagan kingship as God's instrument. Isaiah calls Cyrus the Lord's "anointed" (Isa 45:1). Jeremiah commands the Babylonian exiles to "seek the welfare of the city" of their captivity (Jer 29:7). Daniel asserts that "the Most High rules the kingdom of mortals and gives it to whom he will" (Dan 4:17, 25). Wisdom of Solomon 6:3 tells kings, "your dominion was given you from the Lord." This stream — pagan power as God-permitted, even God-deputized, despite its idolatry — is what Paul inherits. The Jewish prayer-tradition for the welfare of the gentile sovereign (later codified in the *Mi sheberakh* for the government, with antecedents at Ezra 6:10 and Bar 1:11) presupposes the same theology. But there is also a counter-stream: the Maccabean tradition of armed resistance to Antiochus IV; the Daniel apocalypse's vision of Gentile empires as beasts to be destroyed (Dan 7); the first-century revolutionary parties (Zealots, Sicarii) who took the slogan "no king but God" with literal force and led Israel into the catastrophe of 66–70 CE. Paul writes a decade before the Jewish War, and his counsel of subjection cuts directly against the rising revolutionary temper of Judea.

**The Christian patristic trajectory.** The early Fathers used Romans 13 to argue, against pagan accusation of disloyalty, that Christians were the empire's most reliable subjects (Justin *1 Apology* 17; Tertullian *Apology* 30–33; Origen *Contra Celsum* 8.65–75 — though Origen also explicitly says Christians do not serve in the army). The Constantinian turn made the chapter a charter for the *imperium Christianum*: Eusebius reads Constantine into Romans 13. Augustine's *City of God* (esp. books 19–22) gave the chapter its most enduring patristic frame: earthly rulers maintain a relative justice that restrains the worst evils of fallen humanity, but they are not the *civitas Dei* and their authority is provisional. Augustine famously tied 13:13–14 to his own conversion in *Confessions* 8.12: in the garden at Milan, hearing the child's voice chanting *tolle lege*, he opens Paul at random and reads "not in orgies and drunkenness, not in sexual immorality and sensuality, not in quarreling and jealousy, but put on the Lord Jesus Christ." His conversion is a Romans 13 conversion. The verse becomes the West's paradigmatic narrative of grace breaking into a divided will.

**Medieval.** Aquinas, *De Regno* and *Summa Theologica* II-II q.42 and q.104, parses the chapter through Aristotelian-natural-law lenses: human authority is ordered to the common good, derives ultimately from God, but loses binding force when it commands what is contrary to divine or natural law (*lex iniusta non est lex*). The Two-Swords doctrine (Boniface VIII's *Unam Sanctam*, 1302) read the chapter as authorizing both spiritual and temporal authority, with the temporal subordinate.

**Reformation.** Luther's two-kingdoms doctrine (*Temporal Authority*, 1523) drew sharply on Romans 13: the magistrate wields the sword in the kingdom of the left hand, and the Christian as Christian belongs to the kingdom of the right, but the same person inhabits both. Calvin's *Institutes* 4.20 is the most extensive Reformation treatment: magistrates are a divine institution, but lower magistrates may resist a tyrannical higher magistrate without sin (4.20.31). This opened the door for the resistance theology that followed: Theodore Beza's *Right of Magistrates* (1574), the anonymous Huguenot *Vindiciae contra Tyrannos* (1579), and Samuel Rutherford's *Lex Rex* (1644), all of which used Romans 13 to authorize armed resistance to ungodly rulers — a reading that fed directly into the English and American revolutionary traditions. The Anabaptist Schleitheim Confession (1527) read the chapter to the opposite end: the sword belongs outside the perfection of Christ, and the believer must not wield it.

**The twentieth-century crucible.** Romans 13 was the most catastrophically misused biblical text of the twentieth century. The German Christian movement under Hitler invoked it to claim that obedience to the Führer was a Christian duty. Karl Barth's 1922 *Romans* commentary had already, before Hitler, read chapter 13 with eschatological dialectic — the state is a divinely appointed *Schein* (semblance) of order whose authority is real but penultimate, always open to the critique of the gospel. The Barmen Declaration of 1934, which Barth largely drafted, rejected the German Christian misreading: "We reject the false doctrine that the state, over and beyond its special commission, should and could become the single and totalitarian order of human life." Dietrich Bonhoeffer's *Ethics* and *Discipleship* developed the same line: the state is divinely ordained but its ordination is for the sake of the preservation of life and justice; when it becomes a *destroyer* of life it has forfeited the office. Bonhoeffer's participation in the Abwehr conspiracy against Hitler was theologically grounded in this reading. After 1945, Romans 13 in mainline Protestant theology has been read as a chapter that requires the parallel and corrective text of Revelation 13, where the same word *exousia* describes the beast that demands worship.

**The four modern interpretive schools** all leave fingerprints on chapter 13.

The **Old Perspective** (Augustine, Luther, Calvin, Hodge, Murray, Schreiner) reads the chapter as straight Christian political ethics. The civil magistrate is a divine institution; the Christian's default is obedience; resistance is permitted only in the narrow case where the magistrate commands sin (Acts 5:29). Murray's NICNT and Schreiner's BECNT exemplify the modern Reformed reading: 13:1–7 is a freestanding theology of the state.

The **New Perspective** (Sanders, Dunn, Wright) reads the chapter inside the larger argument about the people of God. For Wright (*Paul and the Faithfulness of God*; *Romans* in the New Interpreter's Bible), the chapter is not a timeless theology of the state but a pastoral instruction to a vulnerable Roman house-church about how to live faithfully under the empire that crucified the Messiah. Wright argues forcefully that the chapter is not a theological endorsement of empire — that would contradict everything Paul says about the *kyrios* Christ over against the *kyrios* Caesar — but a counsel of provisional subjection within a story whose climax is the kingdom of God supplanting all earthly *exousiai*. The "armor of light" passage (13:11–14) is the key: the political instructions are bracketed by an apocalyptic horizon that relativizes them.

**Apocalyptic Paul** (Käsemann, J. L. Martyn, Beverly Gaventa, Douglas Campbell, Brigitte Kahl, Neil Elliott) reads the chapter in tension with Paul's larger cosmology of defeated Powers. Käsemann's mid-twentieth-century essay "Principles of the Interpretation of Romans 13" (in *New Testament Questions of Today*) sets the agenda: the *exousiai* of 13:1 must be read against the *exousiai* of Rom 8:38 and 1 Cor 15:24, where they are cosmic powers being subjected by Christ. Käsemann reads the chapter as a sober, ad-hoc pastoral counsel — not a theology — that was never meant to be the normative Christian theology of the state. Brigitte Kahl (*Galatians Re-Imagined*) and Neil Elliott (*The Arrogance of Nations: Reading Romans in the Shadow of Empire*) push further, finding subversive irony in the chapter: Paul deploys imperial vocabulary (*exousia*, *diakonos*, *machaira*, *phobos*, *timē*) only to relativize it under the looming apocalyptic Day. Douglas Campbell, in *The Deliverance of God*, treats the chapter as practical wisdom for a community awaiting the final unveiling of God's apocalyptic act.

**Paul Within Judaism** (Mark Nanos, Pamela Eisenbaum, Matthew Thiessen, Paula Fredriksen) offers the most radical reframing. Nanos (*The Mystery of Romans*) argues that the *exousiai hyperechousai* of 13:1 are not the Roman imperial authorities at all but the synagogue authorities under whose communal jurisdiction the Roman gentile-Christian community continued to operate as a sub-group attached to the Jewish polity. Submission to authorities is, on this reading, submission to synagogue discipline as a faithful expression of gentile attachment to the people of Israel. The reading is a minority position but has forced the mainstream to take more seriously the synagogue context of the Roman house-churches.

The contemporary commentary tradition is rich. Cranfield's ICC remains the most thorough Reformed treatment; Fitzmyer's Anchor Bible balances Catholic political theology with rigorous philology; Moo's NICNT defends a traditional reading; Dunn's WBC presses the New Perspective; Jewett's Hermeneia is indispensable for the political and historical-contextual material on Roman taxation, the *publicani*, and the early Neronian situation. John Howard Yoder's *Politics of Jesus* (1972, rev. 1994) chapter on Romans 13 is the most influential Anabaptist reading of the twentieth century: *hypotassō* ("be subject") is not active obedience but ordered subordination — the same word the early creeds use of Christ's subjection to the Father in 1 Cor 15:28 — and is compatible with refusal to participate in violence. Stanley Hauerwas extends the Yoderian reading.

## Lens 4 — The Theological Intent

**Tension One: How absolute is the command of subjection?**

Position A — *Strict obedience.* The magistrate is divinely appointed; resistance is rebellion against God himself. The early Lutheran tradition and the German Christian misreading represent the extreme; Hodge and the Old Princeton Reformed tradition represent a more measured but still strongly subordinationist version. The steelman: Paul's language is unconditional. He does not say "submit to good rulers" but "submit to the governing authorities," and the rulers in view (Nero, the prefect, the *publicani*) were already deeply morally compromised. If Paul could write this about Nero's regime, then almost no plausible regime falls outside the chapter's command.

Position B — *Conditional obedience with the right of resistance.* Calvin, the Reformed resistance tradition (Beza, *Vindiciae*, Rutherford), Aquinas's natural-law qualification, and Bonhoeffer's wartime ethics. Submission is the default; resistance is permitted when the magistrate commands sin or becomes a destroyer of life. Acts 5:29 ("we must obey God rather than men") supplies the limiting principle. The steelman: the structure of the chapter itself implies the limit. The magistrate is a *diakonos* "for your good" (13:4); when the magistrate ceases to do good and instead does evil, the conditions for the office are voided. Bonhoeffer's question — what becomes of Romans 13 when the state becomes the beast of Revelation 13? — is biblically irresistible.

Position C — *Active non-resistance / Christian pacifism.* Anabaptist tradition, Yoder, Hauerwas. The chapter does not require positive obedience to all commands but ordered subordination to the structures of the world; the church witnesses to a different kingdom and refuses the sword in principle. The steelman: every patristic source before Constantine read the chapter alongside an absolute Christian refusal of military service and judicial bloodshed. The Yoderian reading recovers a real ancient consensus.

Where this leaves us: the chapter cannot bear the weight of a totalitarian reading without contradicting Paul's own apocalyptic horizon and the wider canon. It also cannot bear a frictionless revolutionary reading, since Paul's plain counsel is provisional submission. The truthful position is the one that holds together divine ordination, conditional limits, and the eschatological relativization of all earthly authority. Different communities will weight these differently, and the church needs all three witnesses.

**Tension Two: Is love the *fulfillment* or the *abolition* of the law?**

Position A — *Love fulfills the law's content while abolishing its form* (Lutheran). The Decalogue's second table is summed up in love; the ceremonial law and the civil law of Israel are abrogated; the moral law remains, now interiorized as love.

Position B — *Love is the law's substance and the law remains the proper form of love* (Reformed, with the threefold division of moral, ceremonial, judicial). The moral law is permanent, with love as its ground and goal; ceremonial and judicial law are fulfilled in Christ in the sense of being completed and superseded.

Position C — *Love is the apocalyptic horizon of life in the new creation; the Torah retains its full validity for Israel and gentile believers participate by way of love* (Paul Within Judaism reading). For Nanos, Eisenbaum, Thiessen: Paul does not abrogate Torah for Jewish believers; gentile believers fulfill the Torah's intent toward neighbor through love, without becoming Jews.

Where this leaves us: the chapter is not a marcionite text. Paul cites the Decalogue and Lev 19:18 as authoritative scripture and treats love as the *plērōma* (fulfillment) of *nomos*, not its replacement. The Torah remains the structuring grammar; love is the soul. Christian ethics that drift from this — toward either antinomian sentimentality on one side or moralistic legalism on the other — has lost the Pauline center.

One unresolved question: when Paul says "the night is far gone, the day is at hand," and writes a chapter of pragmatic political counsel under Nero's roof, what theological status does that counsel have when the day did not arrive in his generation? The chapter's ethics are framed by an imminent eschatology that did not unfold on the expected timetable. How do we read the political counsel of a man who thought the Lord might return before the next *publicani* census?

## The Thread

- Romans 12:1–2 — the *logikē latreia* and the renewed mind: chapter 13's ethics presuppose the transformed community of chapter 12.
- Romans 8:38 — the *exousiai* that cannot separate from God's love: Paul uses the same noun for cosmic powers that he uses for the magistrate.
- Leviticus 19:18 — the love-of-neighbor command that Paul names as the law's summation.
- Revelation 13 — the beast that demands worship: the canon's necessary correction-text for a misread Romans 13.

Flag forward: chapter 14's accommodation between weak and strong applies the love command of 13:8–10 to the concrete intra-community case. Chapter 15:25–28's collection for Jerusalem extends the love-debt across ethnic lines. Chapter 16's greetings demonstrate what the renewed community looks like in practice — including women named *diakonos* and *episēmoi en tois apostolois*, which complicates any narrowly hierarchical reading of "be subject."
