# Job 6: The Weight of Vexation and the Treachery of Brooks

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

Job 6 opens the poetic dialogue's second voice — the sufferer himself answering his first comforter — and its imagery presumes a very specific geography and economy. The "caravans of Tema" and "travelers of Sheba" (6:19) anchor the poem in the trading networks of the northwest Arabian peninsula. Tema (Teima) was a well-attested oasis on the incense road, documented in the eighth-century BC annals of Tiglath-pileser III and later fortified by the Neo-Babylonian king Nabonidus, who spent a decade of his reign there. Sheba, in the southern highlands of Yemen, was the southern terminus of the aromatics trade — frankincense and myrrh moving north to the Levantine markets. The land of Uz, where Job is placed (1:1), has been variously located in Edom (south of the Dead Sea), in the Hauran east of the Sea of Galilee, or in northern Arabia; the Septuagint appendix to Job identifies him with Jobab king of Edom (Gen 36:33). The "torrent-bed" (*nachal* נַחַל) metaphor in 6:15 is the precise Wadi ecology of that zone — seasonal streams that rage with snowmelt from the Transjordan highlands (Hermon, the Jebel Druze), then utterly vanish by midsummer, leaving thirsty caravans to die.

The material culture the chapter invokes is patriarchal and pastoral. Job's invocation of the "wild donkey" (*pere'* פֶּרֶא, 6:5) — the Syrian onager that ranged across the steppe until its extinction in the twentieth century — and the domesticated ox (*shor* שׁוֹר) draws a distinction native to a world where both animals were still a daily sight. The "juice of the mallow" (6:6, *rir challamut*) points to a foraged marsh plant, probably *Corchorus olitorius* or purslane, eaten as famine fare. The "arrows of the Almighty" (6:4) and "terrors of God" (*bi'uthey Eloah* בִּעוּתֵי אֱלוֹהַּ) belong to an older Near Eastern conception in which disease is a projectile from the divine world — a notion shared with Ugaritic descriptions of Resheph and with the Akkadian *šibtu* (plague-stroke) sent by Nergal.

The authorship and dating of Job sit at the center of a long scholarly quarrel. The patriarchal setting — wealth measured in livestock, no priesthood, a head-of-household who sacrifices on behalf of his own children (1:5) — could place the narrative world as early as the second millennium BC. But the *language* of the poem is another matter. Marvin Pope, in the Anchor Bible commentary, and David Clines, in the Word Biblical Commentary, have argued on linguistic grounds (Aramaisms, loanwords, syntax) for a post-exilic composition, perhaps in the fifth or fourth century BC. Carol Newsom, in *The Book of Job: A Contest of Moral Imaginations* (2003), treats it as a post-exilic polyphonic work engaging the crisis of Judah's collapse. On the other side, Robert Gordis and Francis Andersen maintain a pre-exilic provenance, perhaps seventh-century, pointing to features in common with Ugaritic poetry. Ezekiel 14:14, dated to the early sixth century, already names Job beside Noah and Danel as a legendary righteous man, which suggests that at least the Job *figure* was proverbial well before the exile — whatever the date of the poem's final form.

The comparative evidence from Mesopotamia is crucial here. *Ludlul bēl nēmeqi* ("I will praise the lord of wisdom"), the so-called Babylonian Job, is a Kassite-period (c. 1300 BC) first-person complaint from a sufferer named Shubshi-meshre-Shakkan whose gods have abandoned him; the *Babylonian Theodicy* (c. 1000 BC) is an acrostic dialogue between a sufferer and his friend that parallels the Joban debate with uncanny closeness; and the older Sumerian *Man and His God* (c. 2000 BC) already contains the motif of a righteous man suffering and pleading for explanation. The Ugaritic tablets from Ras Shamra (fourteenth century BC) supply divine-council scenes structurally similar to Job 1–2. And from Qumran, the 11QTargum Job (11Q10) — a first-century AD Aramaic paraphrase — testifies to the book's early circulation, while the Old Greek (LXX) Job is roughly 400 lines shorter than the Masoretic text, suggesting the Hebrew version itself underwent significant growth.

Five Hebrew terms in this chapter repay scrutiny. *Ka'as* כַּעַס (6:2), usually rendered "vexation" or "grief," carries the sense of a provoking anger mixed with sorrow — the word used for Hannah's rival in 1 Sam 1:6. *Hawwah* הַוָּה (6:2, "calamity") is ruin or disaster, often catastrophic collapse. *Hesed* חֶסֶד (6:14) — covenant kindness, loyal love — is the central relational word Job accuses his friends of withholding. *Ruach* רוּחַ (6:26) — "wind" or "spirit" — is what Job calls his own despairing speech, anticipating the *ruach* meditation of chapter 7. And *mokhiach* מוֹכִיחַ (implied from his appeal in 6:25 and explicit later in 9:33) is the forensic word for arbiter or umpire — the one who judges between parties in a *rib*, a legal dispute.

## Lens 2 — The Literature

Job 6 is poetry in the strictest Hebrew sense, governed by parallelism and by stanzaic clustering rather than meter in the Greek or English sense. Robert Alter, in *The Art of Biblical Poetry* (1985) and in his translation of the Wisdom Books (2010), stresses how the second half of each line in Hebrew verse characteristically intensifies, specifies, or sharpens the first. Verse 3 exemplifies it cleanly: "heavier than the sand of the sea" answers "weighed in the balances" with a hyperbolic concretion. Verse 12's "Is my strength the strength of stones, / or is my flesh bronze?" is synonymous parallelism that also climbs — from mineral to metal, from the inert to the forged. Verse 15's couplet "My brothers are treacherous as a torrent-bed, / as torrential streams that pass away" is synthetic parallelism that develops a single image across two lines and then across the five verses that follow (16–20), a sustained metaphor that Newsom identifies as one of the longest in the dialogues.

The chapter's macro-structure moves in four movements. Verses 2–7: the weight of grief and the reason for wild speech. Verses 8–13: the wish for death as release. Verses 14–23: the treachery of the friends compared to seasonal wadis. Verses 24–30: the demand for specific instruction and the claim of integrity. Norman Habel, in his 1985 Old Testament Library commentary, calls the whole speech a "lament with legal overtones," noting the forensic register that rises across the chapter and blooms in 6:29's "my vindication" (*tsidqi* צִדְקִי, literally "my righteousness"). Edwin Good, in *In Turns of Tempest* (1990), reads the speech as an extended ironic refutation of Eliphaz's pieties from chapter 5, inverting the comforter's images back on their author.

The wadi metaphor in 15–20 is one of the great sustained images in wisdom literature. J. Gerald Janzen, in his 1985 Interpretation commentary, notes the ironic reversal: friends, in the ancient Near East, were expected to be *hesed*-bearers precisely as a wadi was expected to be a bearer of water. Both betray expectation. The caravan trope makes the betrayal a fatal one: the travelers do not merely walk away disappointed; they "go up into the waste and perish" (6:18). David Clines reads the parallel of ice-dark water vanishing in summer heat (16–17) as a precise meteorological observation rather than a generic simile — snowmelt filling the Jordanian wadis only to disappear when travelers need water most.

The chapter is also rich in rhetorical questions, a form Job uses to expose the friends' complacency. "Does the wild donkey bray when he has grass, / or the ox low over his fodder?" (6:5) is a proverbial argument from nature: animals do not complain without cause, so neither do I. "Is there any taste in the juice of the mallow?" (6:6) turns on the tastelessness of famine food as a figure for Eliphaz's bland counsel. Habel catalogs twelve interrogatives in the chapter, each tightening the legal-rhetorical vise.

Wordplay and sound-patterning pervade the Hebrew. The *pe* and *shin* clusters in verse 2 — *l'u shaqol yikashel ka'asi* — make the line audibly heavy. There is a chiastic movement in 14 where "withholds" (*mas*) and "forsakes" (*ya'azov*) bracket "kindness from a friend" and "fear of the Almighty," setting the horizontal obligation (friend) in exact mirror to the vertical one (Almighty). The inclusio between 6:3's "my words have been rash" (*lá'u* לָעוּ) and 6:26's characterization of those words as *ruach* (wind) is a subtle frame: Job's speech is confessedly unrestrained but, he insists, not ungrounded.

Textual variants matter in two places. The phrase "my spirit drinks their poison" (6:4) — Hebrew *chamatham shotah ruchi* — is unusual; some scholars read *chamatham* as "their heat" rather than "their poison," and 11QTgJob renders it differently. In 6:10, "I would even exult in pain unsparing" depends on a difficult hapax *asalledah* (possibly "I would leap" or "harden myself"), which Pope renders "I would leap for joy," Clines "I would exult," and the LXX misses entirely. These variants do not change the chapter's argument but they register how frayed the text of Job is, even within the Masoretic tradition.

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

Job sits inside the wisdom corpus (Job, Proverbs, Qoheleth/Ecclesiastes) as its most searching interrogation, and chapter 6 specifically opens the long counter-argument that will occupy the poetic center of the book. In the canonical shape, Proverbs supplies the retributive grammar ("the wicked suffer, the righteous flourish") that the three friends will defend; Qoheleth will later, more laconically, show that grammar failing; Job shows a single sufferer in the teeth of that failure. Gerhard von Rad, in *Wisdom in Israel* (1970), treated Job as wisdom's self-critique — the genre turning on its own confidence.

### Jewish Interpretive Trajectory

In the Second Temple period, the *Testament of Job* (first century BC or AD) rewrote the sufferer as a proto-martyr whose patience is explicit and whose wife Sitis becomes a named character; 11QTgJob from Qumran shows the book already being intensively studied and paraphrased. Philo treats Job only in passing; Josephus does not name him but presumes his legend. The Babylonian Talmud in Bava Batra 15a–16b debates when Job lived (variously placed in the patriarchal age, the age of the Judges, or the Persian period), whether he actually existed, and whether he feared God only from fear and not from love — with Rava's famous ruling that Job "sought to turn the dish upside down," a rabbinic idiom for disturbing the moral order. The rabbis were uneasy with the figure; Baba Batra records the view that Job "blasphemed with his mouth." Pesikta Rabbati and later midrashim develop his story more sympathetically, sometimes linking him to the Exodus generation.

Medieval Jewish exegesis took up the chapter's sharper edges. Rashi (eleventh century) reads 6:4's "arrows of the Almighty" as the specific afflictions God has permitted, and glosses *ruach* in 6:26 tenderly — Job's despair speech is *wind*, to be heard with mercy and not parsed like testimony. Ibn Ezra draws out the meteorological realism of the wadi image. Maimonides, in *Guide for the Perplexed* III.22–23, treats the book of Job as the central philosophical text on providence, arguing that Job's early position is that of ordinary pious opinion, which he must outgrow to reach true understanding; in Maimonides's reading, each friend represents a philosophical school (Aristotle, the Mu'tazilites, the Ash'arites), and Job's own voice begins in raw pain and moves toward intellectual purification. Nachmanides (Ramban) emphasized the possibility of transmigration of souls as an explanation for innocent suffering, a reading Levenson notes as a distinctive medieval-Jewish move.

Modern Jewish readers have returned to Job with renewed force. Martin Buber, in *The Prophetic Faith* (1949), reads Job's complaint as the "thou" addressed to God in protest. Jon Levenson, in *Creation and the Persistence of Evil* (1988), sets Job within an Israelite cosmology in which chaos (*tohu*) is not vanquished but bounded, and God's power is not metaphysically omnipotent but covenantally engaged. James Kugel emphasizes Job's archaic features. Avivah Zornberg reads the chapter as a "poetics of the unbearable" — Job insisting his words be weighed, not judged for tone.

### Christian Interpretive Trajectory

Patristic readers approached Job through the lens of patience and typology. Gregory the Great's *Moralia in Job* (c. 579–595), across thirty-five books, is the most ambitious premodern commentary; Gregory reads 6:4's arrows allegorically as piercings of contrition, and the wadi image as the failure of worldly friendship compared with Christ's living water. Origen treated Job typologically as a figure of Christ's innocent suffering; Chrysostom's homilies read 6:14's *hesed* as a summons to mercy; Augustine cites Job throughout *City of God* as witness to providence.

In the high medieval period, Thomas Aquinas's *Expositio super Iob ad litteram* (c. 1261–1265) argued that the literal-historical sense of Job is primarily a defense of divine providence against the view that all things happen by fortune. Aquinas reads 6:4 as the legitimate cry of a creature whose suffering exceeds natural explanation; he insists Job's vehemence is not impiety but a struggle toward the light of providence that only the whirlwind speeches will supply.

The Reformation reopened Job as a book of the human conscience. Calvin's 159 sermons on Job, preached in Geneva between 1554 and 1555, treat chapter 6 as a portrait of the believer under Anfechtung — Luther's word for the terror of conscience under God's hiddenness — arguing that Job's wish for death (6:8–9) is both culpable and deeply human, to be met not with condemnation but with preaching. Luther himself found Job a congenial witness to the *deus absconditus*, the hidden God of his theology.

Critical scholarship from Wellhausen through Gunkel placed Job within the history of Israelite religion; Westermann, in *The Structure of the Book of Job* (1981), read the poetic core as a "dramatized lament." Brevard Childs's canonical approach treated the book as Israel's mature reckoning with retribution theology. David Clines's three-volume WBC commentary is the standard technical resource; Carol Newsom's *A Contest of Moral Imaginations* reads the book as polyphonic, refusing to give any character the final word. Gustavo Gutiérrez, in *On Job: God-Talk and the Suffering of the Innocent* (1987), reads from Latin American liberation theology: Job is the voice of the innocent poor, and his demand for a hearing is the paradigmatic political-theological demand. Ellen Davis, Susannah Ticciati, Norman Habel, John Hartley, and Francis Andersen round out the contemporary Christian conversation.

The deepest convergence between the two trajectories is the recognition that Job's speech is not blasphemy: both Maimonides and Aquinas insist on this. The sharpest divergence is eschatological. The Christian tradition has usually resolved innocent suffering by appeal to resurrection and the vision of God beyond death; the Jewish tradition, with conspicuous exceptions like Nachmanides, has characteristically kept the question terrestrial, insisting that whatever answer Job receives must come within the world. Levenson, Kugel, and Zornberg are pointed on this point: the book does not offer immortality as consolation, and to import it is to short-circuit the very question the poet is asking.

## Lens 4 — The Theological Intent

The theological vision of Job 6 turns on two real tensions that the chapter refuses to settle.

The first tension: is vehement protest *against* God compatible with fidelity *to* God? Job's demand that his grief be weighed in balances, his wish for God to crush him, his accusation that the friends have withheld *hesed* — all of this is addressed within a covenant grammar and within piety, not against it. But how far can protest go before it becomes the thing Eliphaz feared?

- The Catholic reading, following Aquinas's *Expositio*, distinguishes between the *matter* of Job's speech (legitimate grief) and its *mode* (sometimes intemperate); Aquinas refuses to call Job's words sin, seeing them as the pressure of providence being felt by an honest creature. The Catechism of the Catholic Church (CCC 304, on divine providence; CCC 312, on suffering) implicitly underwrites this: God permits true creaturely protest as part of his providence.
- Reformed (Calvinist) readers, led by Calvin himself, see in 6:8–13 the edge of Anfechtung — the soul's legitimate struggle under the hidden God — but would distinguish Job's clinging ("I have not denied the words of the Holy One," 6:10) from the impulse to despair. Protest is permitted, but within a frame of sovereign decree.
- Lutheran readers center the *deus absconditus*: Job, under the hidden God, speaks what the believer must speak, and only the hidden God can answer himself. The cross-shape of this is the ground of Luther's "theology of the cross" — God met in the place of protest, not above it.
- Wesleyan-Arminian readers, who hold a more cooperative view of grace, tend to emphasize the *relational* quality of Job's protest: because Job is free, he can push back; because God loves, he can bear the pushback.
- The Jewish reading (Maimonides, Levenson, Zornberg) is perhaps the most comfortable with the vehemence. Rashi's pastoral note — Job's speech is *wind*, to be borne — and Levenson's insistence that the divine-human relation in Israel is covenantal and thus dialogical, mean that protest is simply how the creature speaks to God. The steelman case I find least intuitive is this Jewish one, because it unsettles the Christian instinct to require a resolution. But it has the textual weight: the frame-narrator of Job 42:7 says Job spoke rightly of God, where the friends did not — which seems to validate the protest itself.

Where this leaves us: the chapter is a permission slip, cautiously written. Protest is not the opposite of faith but, under the conditions of unjust suffering, one of its honest forms.

The second tension: what does *hesed* require of those who come to the sufferer? Job's charge in 6:14 — "He who withholds kindness from a friend forsakes the fear of the Almighty" — binds horizontal and vertical obligation so tightly that failing at the horizontal is *ipso facto* failing at the vertical.

- Catholic moral theology (CCC 2447, on the works of mercy) locates this within the corporal and spiritual works, making presence to the sufferer a grave duty that cannot be satisfied by advice. Aquinas's treatment of *misericordia* in *Summa Theologiae* II-II.30 distinguishes it from a passion and elevates it to a virtue.
- Reformed readers, with their strong doctrine of the communion of saints and the law of love, read 6:14 as covenantal, binding the community to bear with one another and not to preach when presence is needed.
- Lutheran theology locates this under the *theology of the cross*: to stand with the sufferer is to stand where God is hidden, which is where God is met.
- The Jewish framework, drawing on *nichum aveilim* (the commandment to comfort mourners) codified in rabbinic law, institutionalizes a concrete practice: the comforter is to sit in silence and wait for the mourner to speak first. Eliphaz broke this rule; Job is correcting him.
- Critical scholarship (Newsom, Clines) reads 6:14 as the poet's explicit indictment of the friends and a programmatic statement for the dialogues: the whole book will unfold as a test of whether the friends can offer *hesed* rather than explanation, and they will fail.

Where this leaves us: *hesed* is a disposition of bearing with, not a technology of explanation. The friends brought answers. Job needed company.

An unresolved question: if Job's words in chapter 6 are *ruach* — wind, breath, spirit — and if the friends are wrong to weigh them as if they were testimony under oath, then what, exactly, is the mode of speech God hears? Because Job will later demand precisely that God treat his words as testimony, as evidence in a lawsuit. Is the difference between wind and witness only the difference between who is listening?

## The Thread

- **Psalm 22:1** — "My God, my God, why have you forsaken me?" shares Job 6's idiom of protest addressed *to* the very God who seems to have struck. Both refuse the piety that would smooth grief into acquiescence; both hold the address open.
- **Jeremiah 20:14–18** — Jeremiah's curse on the day of his birth is formally close to Job 6:8–13's wish to be cut off. The prophet and the sufferer share a permission the comforter does not understand: despair is a form of prayer when it is still directed toward the one who could end it.
- **Hosea 6:6** — "I desire *hesed* and not sacrifice" — the exact word Job accuses his friends of withholding is the word Hosea puts at the center of covenant. The friends' offering of theological sacrifices to Job's grief fails precisely the *hesed* test Hosea names.
- **Matthew 26:38** — "My soul is very sorrowful, even to death" — Gethsemane echoes Job 6:11's question ("What is my strength, that I should wait?") not as a doctrine of resurrection read backward but as a recognition that the Christian tradition receives Job's idiom of protest and puts it on the lips of its own central figure. Christology, at least here, is not the answer to Job but the continuation of his speech.
