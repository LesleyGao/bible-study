# Matthew 4: Wilderness Testing and the Light in Galilee

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

Matthew 4 unfolds across two distinct geographies: the **Judean wilderness** (4:1–11) and **Galilee** (4:12–25). The wilderness is the same arid hill-country east of Jerusalem that hosted John's ministry — terraced badlands dropping toward the Jordan Rift. The traditional site of Jesus's testing is Mount Quarantania (Jebel Quruntul) above Jericho, identified by Byzantine pilgrims and marked by the Greek Orthodox Monastery of the Temptation perched on the cliff face. The "**holy city**" of 4:5 is Jerusalem, with its Herodian Temple complex — Herod the Great's monumental rebuilding (begun c. 20 BCE, still under construction during Jesus's ministry per John 2:20) — and the "pinnacle" (πτερύγιον τοῦ ἱεροῦ) likely the southeastern corner overlooking the Kidron Valley, where the drop is approximately 450 feet to the valley floor. **Josephus** (*Ant.* 15.412) describes the dizzying view from this corner, noting that it would make a viewer faint.

**Galilee** in the early 1st century was a densely settled, religiously Jewish region under **Herod Antipas** (4 BCE–39 CE). Antipas had built **Sepphoris** as his early capital (rebuilt c. 4 BCE after Roman destruction) and **Tiberias** on the Sea of Galilee's western shore (founded 19 CE in honor of Tiberius). The region's economy combined agriculture (olive groves, wheat, barley), fishing, and craft trades. **Capernaum** (Hebrew *Kfar Naḥum*, "village of Nahum"), where Jesus settles in 4:13, was a small fishing town of perhaps 1,500 inhabitants on the northwest shore of the Sea of Galilee, along the **Via Maris** trade road connecting Damascus to the Mediterranean coast.

**Archaeology** at Capernaum is rich. **Virgilio Corbo** and **Stanislao Loffreda** (Franciscan excavations 1968–1985) uncovered a 1st-century basalt synagogue beneath the 4th–5th century white limestone synagogue still visible today, along with the *insula sacra* — a 1st-century domestic complex that became a Christian gathering place by the late 1st century and was incorporated into a 5th-century octagonal church. The traditional identification of one of these rooms as "Peter's house" is supported by Christian graffiti from the 2nd–4th centuries and continuous veneration from the 4th century onward. **James Strange**'s archaeological surveys of Galilee, **Eric Meyers**'s work on Sepphoris, and **Mordechai Aviam**'s work on the Galilean infrastructure all support a picture of densely Jewish, ritually observant communities (mikvaot, stone vessels, no pig bones in faunal assemblages). The lakeshore itself has yielded the **Galilee Boat** (Kinneret Boat) — a 1st-century wooden fishing vessel discovered in 1986 during a drought, 27 feet long and dated by carbon-14 and pottery to roughly the time of Jesus.

**The Sea of Galilee** (Hebrew *Yam Kinneret*; Lat. *Lacus Tiberiadis*) is technically a freshwater lake, 13 miles long and 8 wide, sitting 700 feet below sea level. The fishing trade was substantial — **Magdala** (Hebrew *Migdal Nunia*, "tower of fish") was the regional fish-processing center; **K. C. Hanson**'s and **Sean Freyne**'s work on Galilean economy documents the cooperative fishing partnerships ("companies" — *koinōnoi*, cf. Luke 5:10) that families like Zebedee's operated. The "**Decapolis**" of 4:25 was the Hellenistic city-league east of the Jordan (Scythopolis, Gerasa, Pella, Philadelphia, etc.), founded by Pompey c. 63 BCE — Greek-speaking, Greco-Roman in religious culture, and the geographical extension of "Galilee of the Gentiles" toward the East.

The **Pharisees and Sadducees** continue from chapter 3; the chapter's announcement of "great crowds" from "Galilee and the Decapolis, and from Jerusalem and Judea, and from beyond the Jordan" (4:25) reflects the trans-regional reach of an itinerant Galilean teacher attracting Aramaic-speakers from Galilee, Greek-speakers from the Decapolis, and Judean pilgrims along the Jordan corridor.

Five Greek terms anchor the chapter: ***peirazō*** (πειράζω, 4:1, 3) — "to test, tempt," semantically broad (can mean ethical seduction or covenant testing); ***ho diabolos*** (ὁ διάβολος, 4:1, 5, 8, 11) — "the devil," literally "the slanderer/accuser" (translating Hebrew *śāṭān* in LXX); ***basileia tōn ouranōn*** (βασιλεία τῶν οὐρανῶν, 4:17) — "kingdom of heaven," repeating 3:2; ***Galilaia tōn ethnōn*** (Γαλιλαία τῶν ἐθνῶν, 4:15) — "Galilee of the Gentiles," citing LXX Isa 8:23; and ***euangelion tēs basileias*** (εὐαγγέλιον τῆς βασιλείας, 4:23) — "gospel of the kingdom," the first occurrence of *euangelion* in Matthew.

## Lens 2 — The Literature

Matthew 4 is the Gospel's launching of public ministry proper, structured in three movements: the **temptation** (4:1–11), the **Galilean inauguration** (4:12–17), and the **first disciples and ministry summary** (4:18–25). The chapter's parallels are well-mapped: the temptation aligns closely with Luke 4:1–13 (the Q-source, with Matthew and Luke preserving the temptations in different orders), Mark 1:12–13 has only a two-verse summary; the Galilean withdrawal parallels Mark 1:14–15 and Luke 4:14–15; the call of the first disciples parallels Mark 1:16–20 closely, with Luke 5:1–11 expanding the scene; the ministry summary parallels Mark 1:39 and Luke 4:14–15, 6:17–19 with significant Matthean redactional expansion.

The **temptation order** — bread, temple, kingdoms in Matthew; bread, kingdoms, temple in Luke — has been debated for centuries. Most contemporary critical scholarship (Davies/Allison, Fitzmyer, Nolland) holds that Matthew preserves the more original order, with Luke rearranging to climax at Jerusalem (theologically central in Lukan geography). Matthew's order produces a **threefold ascent**: wilderness floor → temple pinnacle → very high mountain — a structural climbing that mirrors the **Moses-Sinai** typology (Exod 24, 33–34) and prepares for the Sermon on the Mount (chapter 5).

The **Mosaic-Israel typology** is central. **Forty days and forty nights** (4:2) directly echoes Moses's two 40-day fasts on Sinai (Exod 24:18; 34:28; Deut 9:9, 18) and Israel's 40 years in the wilderness (Num 14:33–34; Deut 8:2). All three of Jesus's responses cite **Deuteronomy** — 4:4 from Deut 8:3 (manna context); 4:7 from Deut 6:16 (Massah context); 4:10 from Deut 6:13 (Shema context). **Dale Allison**'s *The New Moses: A Matthean Typology* (1993) charts the parallels exhaustively and argues that Jesus is being presented as both a new Moses (re-receiving Torah) and as Israel-redoing-the-wilderness-test (where Israel failed at hunger, testing-God, and idolatry, Jesus succeeds). **Davies/Allison** *ICC* vol. 1 endorse and refine this. **B.W. Bacon**'s 1918 thesis on Matthew's five-discourse Pentateuchal structure finds prefiguration here in the three-fold Deuteronomic citation.

The **devil's quotation of Psalm 91:11–12** at 4:6 introduces a literary topos of major importance: **scripture-against-scripture** as theological method. The devil cites scripture; Jesus cites scripture in reply. Matthew is presenting the contest of interpretations, not the contest of scripture against unbelief. **Davies/Allison** note that the devil's citation omits the phrase "in all your ways" (Ps 91:11 — "to keep you in *all your ways*"), which would have framed the protection as covenant-bound rather than presumption-bound. **Richard Hays**'s *Echoes of Scripture in the Gospels* (2016) reads the temptation as Matthew's most pointed meditation on the hermeneutics of the Word.

The **fourth fulfillment formula citation** (4:14–16) quotes **Isaiah 9:1–2** (LXX 8:23–9:1): "Galilee of the Gentiles, the people sitting in darkness have seen a great light." This is programmatic: Matthew is announcing that Jesus's Galilean location, far from being incidental, is theologically loaded — the "Gentiles" connotation of Galilee anticipates the Great Commission's universal scope (28:19). **Stendahl**'s *School of St. Matthew* analyzed the citation's mixed text-form (closer to LXX than MT but with distinctive variants); Davies/Allison treat the philological details.

The **chapter structure**:
- 4:1–11: Three temptations + angelic ministration
- 4:12–17: Galilean withdrawal, Capernaum settlement, Isaiah citation, programmatic preaching
- 4:18–22: Call of Simon, Andrew, James, John
- 4:23–25: Ministry summary (teaching, preaching, healing) and crowd-gathering

Textual variants are minor; the chapter is well-attested across the principal manuscripts.

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

**Jewish interpretive trajectories.** Modern Jewish-Jesus scholarship engages the temptation differently than the Christian tradition. **Vermes** (*Jesus the Jew*, 1973) treats the wilderness testing as legendary haggadic construction analogous to rabbinic temptation-narratives (e.g., R. Akiba's testing); **Sanders** (*Jesus and Judaism*, 1985) brackets the temptation historically and focuses on the Galilean ministry as Jesus's substantive intervention in Jewish prophetic-eschatological tradition. **David Flusser**'s *Jesus* (1968/1997) reads the temptation typologically as Jesus's recapitulation of Israel's wilderness, intelligible within Jewish midrashic tradition. **Boyarin**'s *Jewish Gospels* (2012) reads the Son-of-God Christology in the temptation as legible within Second Temple divine-Messiah expectation. **Levine** in the *Jewish Annotated NT* (2nd ed. 2017) emphasizes the Deuteronomic citations as evidence of Jesus's Torah-grounded piety.

The **"Galilee of the Gentiles" citation** is engaged by Jewish scholarship as either (a) a Christian misappropriation of an Isaianic restoration prophecy that originally promised liberation *from* the Assyrian conquest of Zebulun and Naphtali, not Gentile inclusion (Levine), or (b) a legitimate exegetical move within ancient Jewish hermeneutics that read prophetic geography figurally (Boyarin). The historical reality of 1st-century Galilee — densely Jewish but adjacent to Hellenistic city-cultures — gave the phrase social texture independent of strict Isaianic exegesis.

**Christian interpretive trajectories.** Patristic engagement is dense. **Origen**'s *Commentary on Matthew* (preserved fragmentarily) and his *Homilies on Luke* read the three temptations as types of all human temptation — bread (lust of the flesh), pinnacle (lust of the eyes / pride of life), kingdoms (worldly ambition) — a tripartite scheme that **Augustine** (*De Vera Religione*, *De Sermone Domini in Monte*) develops with the 1 John 2:16 triad ("lust of the flesh, lust of the eyes, pride of life"). **Chrysostom**'s *Homily 13 on Matthew* engages the temptation extensively, emphasizing Jesus's victory as exemplary for the Christian struggle. **Hilary of Poitiers**'s *On Matthew* gives a typological reading paralleling Jesus's testing with Adam's failure and Israel's failure. **Jerome**'s *Commentary on Matthew* is more philological, attentive to the Deuteronomic citations.

**Aquinas**'s *Catena Aurea* on Matthew 4 gathers the patristic stream; his *Lectura super Matthaeum* and *ST* III.41 ("The Temptation of Christ") give Thomistic systematic treatment, addressing whether it was fitting for Christ to be tempted, the species of temptation, and the relationship between the three temptations and the *concupiscentia carnis / oculorum / superbia vitae* of 1 John 2:16. The Augustinian-Thomistic synthesis became standard in Catholic exegesis.

**Reformation** engagement: **Calvin**'s *Harmony* (1555) reads the temptation as Christ's victory over Satan on humanity's behalf, repudiating both Pelagian readings (Christ as moral example only) and antinomian readings (Christ's victory abolishes the need for Christian struggle). **Luther**'s sermons on the temptation emphasize the centrality of Scripture as the believer's weapon against the devil — *sola scriptura* enacted by Christ himself.

20th-century engagement: **Karl Barth**'s *CD* IV/1 §59.2 reads the temptation as the Son's "Yes" to the Father in the face of the demonic alternative — the moment in which the obedient Son freely chooses the way of the cross. **Bonhoeffer**'s *Cost of Discipleship* (1937) and his Christology lectures emphasize the "cheap grace" alternative the devil offers and the costliness of the obedient Yes. **Bultmann** treats the temptation narrative as legendary in the technical sense and theologically secondary to Pauline eschatology. **Joachim Jeremias**'s *Jesus' Promise to the Nations* (1958) engages the "Galilee of the Gentiles" citation as programmatic for Matthean missiology.

Contemporary commentary: **Davies/Allison** *ICC* vol. 1 (1988); **Ulrich Luz** *Hermeneia Matthew* vol. 1 (2007 ET) — particularly strong on reception history of the temptation in Christian art and spirituality; **R.T. France** *NICNT* (2007); **Craig Keener** *Matthew* (1999); **Daniel Harrington** *Sacra Pagina*; **Donald Hagner** *WBC*; **John Nolland** *NIGTC*; **Wilkins** *NIVAC*; **N.T. Wright** *Matthew for Everyone*; **D.A. Carson**; **Allison** *The New Moses* (1993). On Galilee: **Sean Freyne**'s *Jesus, A Jewish Galilean* (2004) and *Galilee, Jesus, and the Gospels* (1988); **Eric Meyers**'s archaeological work; **Mordechai Aviam**'s *Jews, Pagans and Christians in the Galilee* (2004). On discipleship: **Jack Dean Kingsbury**'s *Matthew as Story* (1986) reads 4:18–22 as the Gospel's first character-development scene establishing the disciples as central narrative actors. Postcolonial: **Warren Carter**'s *Matthew and the Margins* (2000) and *Matthew and Empire* (2001) read the temptation's third panel ("all the kingdoms of the world and their glory") as a Roman-imperial critique — the devil offers what Augustus already claims. Feminist: **Wainwright**'s *Toward a Feminist Critical Reading of the Gospel of Matthew* (1991) notes the silence on women in 4:18–22's call narrative as setting up the chapter's androcentric narrative pattern.

## Lens 4 — The Theological Intent

**Tension 1: What is at stake in the temptations? The Christological question of what Jesus could not have done.** The temptation narrative requires interpretation regarding Jesus's *posse* — what was Jesus capable of? Could he have turned stones to bread? Could he have thrown himself down? Could he have taken the kingdoms? The orthodox Christological tradition is divided.

The **non posse peccare position** (impeccability — much patristic and Reformed tradition; Aquinas; classical Lutheran tradition) holds that Christ, as the divine Son hypostatically united to a human nature, was *unable* to sin. The temptations are real psychologically and morally — Jesus genuinely felt hunger, was genuinely solicited — but they could not have succeeded. On this view, the temptation reveals Christ's perfect alignment with the Father, not the genuine possibility of failure.

The **posse non peccare position** (peccability — some patristic strands; certain modern theologians including some kenoticists) holds that Christ could have sinned but did not — that the temptation must be genuine in the sense that failure was metaphysically possible, otherwise the testing is theatrical rather than real. On this view, the temptation reveals Christ's free obedience as the basis of his salvific work, with the implication that the assumption-of-human-nature included the assumption of moral vulnerability.

The **kenotic position** (some 19th–20th c. Lutheran and Anglican theology — Gore, Forsyth, modern T.F. Torrance with caveats) holds that the divine Son in incarnation voluntarily limits the use of divine attributes that would prevent temptation, so that the obedience is genuine. The position has elegant features but raises questions about the unity of the divine person across the kenosis.

**The steelman of all three positions converges**: whatever the metaphysical specification, the *theological function* of the temptation is to establish that Jesus's obedience to the Father is a genuine moral achievement, that the Christ-event involves the obedient Son's victory in the human-Israelite sphere where Adam and Israel failed, and that this victory is appropriable by faith rather than merely admirable as moral example. **Karl Barth** (*CD* IV/1) urges that the metaphysical question is less important than the actual narrated victory: the Son says "Yes" to the Father in the face of the demonic alternative, and this Yes is the substance of salvation.

**Where this leaves us.** Matthew 4:1–11 is not asking us to resolve the *posse / non posse* debate. It is establishing that Jesus is the obedient Son where Israel was the disobedient son — that his life recapitulates and consummates Israel's vocation, that his use of Deuteronomy positions him as the Torah-faithful Israelite, and that the demonic alternative (bread without obedience, security without trust, kingdoms without the cross) is decisively rejected. The chapter is making a Christological claim, not solving a metaphysical puzzle.

**Tension 2: "Repent, for the kingdom of heaven is at hand" (4:17) — what does the kingdom's nearness mean?** Matthew's distinctive phrase ***basileia tōn ouranōn*** (kingdom of heaven) appears for the second time at 4:17, repeating verbatim John's preaching at 3:2. The interpretation of the kingdom's "nearness" (*ēngiken* — perfect tense, "has drawn near / is at hand") has divided theology since the 19th century.

The **consistent eschatology position** (Albert Schweitzer, Johannes Weiss; some modern interpretations) reads "the kingdom is at hand" as imminent apocalyptic — the kingdom would arrive within the lifetime of Jesus's contemporaries as a cosmic-political event. The non-arrival of the kingdom in this sense becomes the "delay of the parousia" problem, requiring theological reconstruction.

The **realized eschatology position** (C.H. Dodd) reads the kingdom as already present in Jesus's ministry — the *ēngiken* is best translated "has arrived" (Dodd's contested rendering). The kingdom is not future apocalyptic but present in Jesus's words and works.

The **inaugurated / "already-not yet" position** (Oscar Cullmann's *Christ and Time*; G.E. Ladd; modern majority view including Davies/Allison, France, Wright) reads the kingdom as inaugurated in Jesus's ministry but not consummated until the eschaton — the *ēngiken* preserves both senses, the kingdom drawing near and beginning to break in. This reading is supported by Matthew's larger usage (kingdom present at 12:28; kingdom future at 25:31–46), which requires a both-and rather than either-or.

**The steelman of all three positions** is that they are responding to genuine textual data — the imminent-apocalyptic strand, the realized-presence strand, and the inaugurated-but-not-consummated strand all find support in the Gospels. Matthew himself does not flatten these tensions but holds them together.

**Where this leaves us.** Matthew 4:17 is the programmatic announcement of Jesus's ministry, repeating John's herald-preaching at 3:2. The kingdom's nearness is the kingdom's *advent in the Christ-event* — not yet consummated, not merely future, not merely past, but breaking in through the words and works of Jesus and calling for the response of *metanoia* (repentance, turning). The chapter's structure — temptation, Galilean inauguration, disciple-call, ministry summary — performs what the announcement names: the kingdom is breaking in through this Jesus, in this place, calling these people.

**Unresolved question.** Why does Matthew preserve the **temptation order bread → temple → mountain** while Luke preserves bread → mountain → temple? The question is not just historical (which is more original?) but theological: what does Matthew's order *do*? **Davies/Allison** suggest the ascent (wilderness → temple → mountain) prepares the Sermon on the Mount; **France** suggests the climactic third temptation at the mountain frames the Great Commission's "all authority" claim (28:18) as Christ's hard-won, devil-rejected possession. The question of why Matthew arranged the temptations as he did invites further exegetical work.

## The Thread

Matthew 4 reaches back to **Deuteronomy 8** (Israel's wilderness testing), **Deuteronomy 6** (the Shema and its command-context), **Genesis 3** (Adam's failed testing), **Exodus 24, 34** (Moses's 40-day fasts), **Psalm 91** (the angelic-protection psalm the devil weaponizes), and **Isaiah 8:23–9:1** (Galilee of the Gentiles). The chapter reaches forward to **Matthew 5–7** (the Sermon on the Mount, the first major discourse, delivered on a mountain — the geographical climax of the ascent that began in 4:8), to **Matthew 9:35** (the ministry summary that essentially repeats 4:23 to bracket the Sermon and its surrounding miracle-cycles), to **Matthew 16:21–23** (Peter's "Get behind me, Satan" episode, where Jesus uses the same Deuteronomy-victory language against Peter's temptation to abandon the cross), and to **Matthew 28:16–20** (the Galilean Great Commission, in which the rejected mountain-kingdoms of 4:8 become the all-authority of the risen Lord and the all-nations of the church's mission). Flag forward: **Matthew 26:36–46** (Gethsemane) will return to the wilderness-testing pattern with a different outcome on the surface (anguished prayer, sleeping disciples) but the same essential victory (the obedient Yes), making the temptation in 4:1–11 the prefiguration of the cross's deepest moment.
