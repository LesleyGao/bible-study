# Psalm 57 — Go Deeper

## Lens 1: The Historical World

The superscription to Psalm 57 is unusually rich: "To the choirmaster: according to Do Not Destroy. A Miktam of David, when he fled from Saul, in the cave." Three layers sit inside this single line, and each one opens a different door into the ancient world. The phrase "Do Not Destroy" — Hebrew *al-tashchet* (אַל־תַּשְׁחֵת) — is almost certainly the name of a melody, the way later Christian hymnals labeled tunes "St. Anne" or "Hyfrydol." The same tune-marker reappears over Psalms 58, 59, and 75, suggesting an entire group of psalms once sung to one familiar melody, perhaps drawn from a vintner's song (compare Isaiah 65:8, "Do not destroy it, for there is a blessing in it"). The tune name itself preserves a fragment of an oral musical culture we otherwise cannot recover.

The genre marker *miktam* (מִכְתָּם) is more obscure. It tags six psalms (16, 56–60), all attributed to David, and ancient and modern scholars have proposed everything from "golden poem" (from *ketem*, gold) to "inscription" (from a possible Akkadian root *katāmu*, to cover) to "atonement-poem." The Septuagint translates it *stēlographia* — an inscription on a stone slab — which suggests the early Greek translators understood it as a psalm worthy of monumental display. Hermann Gunkel, who founded form criticism with his 1933 *Einleitung in die Psalmen*, classified Psalm 57 as an *Individualklagelied* (individual lament) with strong elements of *Vertrauenspsalm* (psalm of trust) and concluding *Danklied* (thanksgiving). The lament-to-trust-to-praise movement is precisely what Gunkel saw as the typical inner shape of the Israelite cry to God: complaint that resolves through covenant memory into doxology.

The historical setting "when he fled from Saul, in the cave" anchors the psalm to either the cave of Adullam (1 Samuel 22) or the cave at En-gedi (1 Samuel 24). Most modern critics, including Hans-Joachim Kraus and Marvin Tate, treat such superscriptions as later editorial additions during the post-exilic redaction of the Psalter — interpretive captions that read David's narrative onto liturgical texts. But the imagery does fit a fugitive's prayer: "in the shadow of your wings I will take refuge, till the storms of destruction pass by" (57:1), the speaker hidden, hunted, surrounded by lions and beasts whose teeth are spears. Whether or not the cave is biographically real, the cave has become a *literary* setting — the cramped stone chamber where the soul calls upon a God whose glory must rise "above the heavens."

Psalm 57 sits inside Book II of the Psalter (Psalms 42–72), the so-called Elohistic Psalter, where the divine name *YHWH* has been systematically replaced with *Elohim*. The substitution is uneven across the Hebrew Bible, but in Book II it is striking — *Elohim* appears roughly 200 times, *YHWH* only about 45. Some scholars (Joel Burnett, Frank-Lothar Hossfeld) suggest this reflects a northern provenance or a redactor uncomfortable with using the divine name in worship; others see a theological move toward universalism, *Elohim* being the name shared with the surrounding nations. Psalm 57 itself oscillates: *Elohim* in v. 1, 2, 3, 5, 7, 11; *Adonai* in v. 9. The Dead Sea Scrolls confirm the basic shape of Psalm 57 (it appears in the great Psalms scroll 11QPsa and fragments at Qumran), and the Septuagint preserves the same lament-to-praise structure with only minor variants.

Ancient Near Eastern parallels are strong. The "shadow of the wings" imagery in v. 1 echoes both Egyptian iconography of winged solar discs sheltering the king and Mesopotamian prayers in which the supplicant takes refuge under the wings of the deity (compare the prayer to Marduk preserved in Ludlul bēl nēmeqi). But the Israelite version subverts the royal trope: here it is the fugitive, not the enthroned king, who claims the divine shelter.

## Lens 2: The Literature

The psalm's architecture is built on Robert Lowth's parallelism — synonymous, antithetical, and synthetic — and on a single dramatic refrain that stitches the two halves together. Verses 5 and 11 are identical: *rumah al-shamayim Elohim al kol-ha'aretz kevodekha* (רוּמָה עַל־שָׁמַיִם אֱלֹהִים עַל כָּל־הָאָרֶץ כְּבוֹדֶךָ — "Be exalted, O God, above the heavens! Let your glory be over all the earth!"). This refrain functions as both *inclusio* (framing the praise-half) and as the structural pivot dividing the psalm into two strophes of roughly equal weight.

Strophe 1 (vv. 1–4) is lament-from-distress. The speaker piles up images of refuge (*chasah* twice in v. 1, a verb that means literally to seek shelter as a small animal under a rock or wing) and then turns to images of mortal threat — lions, fiery beasts (*lohatim*, "those who burn," a word used elsewhere of dragons and seraphim), human enemies whose teeth are spears. The parallelism in v. 4 is synthetic and intensifying: not just "lions" but "lions / fiery beasts / children of man / teeth-spears / tongue-swords." The danger is animal, demonic, and human all at once.

Strophe 2 (vv. 7–10) is confident praise, and the change is dramatic. *Nakhon libbi Elohim, nakhon libbi* (נָכוֹן לִבִּי אֱלֹהִים נָכוֹן לִבִּי — "my heart is steadfast, O God, my heart is steadfast") repeats the verb in a chiastic doubling. The speaker calls his own *kavod* (glory, here meaning his soul or inner self) to awaken alongside harp and lyre, and declares "I will awake the dawn" — *a'irah shachar* — a stunning reversal in which the worshiper rouses the morning rather than waiting for it. This is poetic priority: praise so intent it precedes the sun.

Three *selahs* punctuate the psalm (vv. 3, 6, and implied at the second refrain in v. 11). The exact musical or liturgical function of *selah* remains debated — pause, instrumental interlude, congregational response — but in Psalm 57 the *selahs* mark hinges: after the declaration of God's faithfulness (v. 3), after the trap-falls-on-the-trapper (v. 6), and after the final exaltation (v. 11).

A noteworthy literary fact: verses 7–11 are nearly identical to Psalm 108:1–5. The same is true of Psalm 60:5–12 reappearing as Psalm 108:6–13. Frank-Lothar Hossfeld and Erich Zenger, in their three-volume Hermeneia commentary, treat Psalm 108 as a deliberate editorial composition — a redactor's collage stitching the praise-half of Psalm 57 to the lament-half of Psalm 60 to create a third psalm for a new liturgical purpose. This is one of the clearest windows in the Hebrew Bible into how the Psalter was assembled: not as a closed corpus of independent compositions but as a living anthology where pieces could be recut and recombined. John Goldingay calls it "the editorial reuse of poetic material," and Erhard Gerstenberger sees it as evidence of cultic improvisation.

## Lens 3: The Larger Story

In the canonical Psalter, Psalm 57 sits inside the Davidic *miktam* cluster — Psalms 56, 57, 58, 59, 60 — five psalms that share genre, attribution, and historical superscriptions tied to David's flight from Saul and from foreign enemies. Read together, they trace a fugitive's progression from terror (56) to refuge (57) to imprecation against corrupt judges (58) to escape from Saul's house (59) to defeat and protest (60). Hossfeld and Zenger argue the cluster was deliberately edited as a David-anthology meant to teach the prayer-life of a king under siege.

In Jewish reception, Psalm 57 enters the *Midrash Tehillim* (a rabbinic anthology compiled across the seventh through twelfth centuries) which reads the cave as the literal cave of Adullam and the lions as Saul's commanders. The medieval commentators differ in emphasis: Rashi (eleventh-century France) glosses *al-tashchet* as a plea — David himself cried "do not destroy me" — turning the tune-name into prayer. Ibn Ezra (twelfth-century Spain) prefers the musical-incipit reading. David Kimchi (Radak, thirteenth-century Provence) reads v. 8's "I will awake the dawn" as David's habit of rising before dawn to play the harp, a detail later picked up in mystical traditions. In modern Jewish thought Martin Buber treats the psalm as a paradigm of the I-Thou cry from the depths; Abraham Joshua Heschel hears in v. 7 the *kavanah* (intentionality) of a heart that has resolved upon God; Jon Levenson sees the cosmic refrain (vv. 5, 11) as preserving the older Israelite vision of God enthroned above the heavens, and Nahum Sarna highlights the avian-shelter imagery as covenant language. Liturgically, Psalm 57 is recited during morning prayers in some Sephardic traditions and is associated with petitions for protection and rescue.

In Christian reception the psalm became one of the most beloved cave-prayers of the tradition. Augustine (*Enarrationes in Psalmos* 57) reads the cave christologically: the cave is the body of Christ in the tomb, the wings are the cross, the dawn is the resurrection. Athanasius's *Letter to Marcellinus* recommends Psalm 57 for moments of persecution. Cassiodorus (sixth century) calls it a perfect example of *tonus exultationis* — the elevation from sorrow to joy. Thomas Aquinas treats the steadfast heart of v. 7 as evidence of the infused virtue of fortitude. Martin Luther in his *Operationes in Psalmos* (1519–1521) makes Psalm 57 a paradigm of the *theologia crucis* — God's glory hidden in the cave of suffering. John Calvin's 1557 commentary emphasizes providence: God "fulfills his purpose for me" (v. 2) is for Calvin a pillar text on divine sovereignty in affliction. Hermann Gunkel, Sigmund Mowinckel (cult-functional reading), Claus Westermann (lament-to-praise as the deep grammar of biblical prayer), Hans-Joachim Kraus (royal-Davidic theology), Hossfeld and Zenger (editorial composition), Goldingay, Tate, and Gordon Wenham have all shaped the modern critical conversation. Walter Brueggemann's *Message of the Psalms* and *Spirituality of the Psalms* treats vv. 7–11 as an exemplar of the move from "disorientation" to "new orientation." Patrick Miller, William Brown, and Beth LaNeel Tanner have all underscored Psalm 57's pedagogical use as a model of trust-prayer.

In Christian liturgy Psalm 57 is appointed at Compline in the Benedictine office — the last prayer of the day, said in the gathering darkness, the cave of night. It has been the prison-prayer of countless Christians: Bonhoeffer in Tegel, Boethius in his cell, the Covenanters in Scottish caves, Vietnamese house-church believers under surveillance. The New Testament does not quote Psalm 57 explicitly, but its imagery — the shadow of God's wings (Matthew 23:37, Jesus over Jerusalem), the steadfast heart, the dawn that breaks (Luke 1:78–79) — runs through the Gospels and Acts.

## Lens 4: The Theological Intent

Two tensions deserve honest engagement.

The first is the doublet phenomenon: vv. 7–11 are nearly identical to Psalm 108:1–5. What does it mean that a Spirit-inspired text was apparently cut, pasted, and recombined by a later editor to make Psalm 108? Three positions exist. (1) The *traditional unitary view* (held in different forms by Aquinas, Calvin, and many evangelical commentators today) treats both psalms as Davidic compositions, with David himself or the Spirit through him reusing earlier material — much as a hymn-writer might quote his own previous lines. (2) The *redactional view* (Hossfeld-Zenger, Gerstenberger, Goldingay) sees Psalm 108 as a deliberate editorial composition by a post-exilic redactor for a new liturgical setting, and treats the doublet as evidence that the Psalter is an anthology rather than a collection of fixed compositions. The strongest version of this argument: the redactor wanted a praise-then-petition psalm for a particular feast, and cut Psalm 57's praise to Psalm 60's petition to make Psalm 108. (3) The *cultic-improvisation view* (Mowinckel, Kraus) treats both psalms as performance variants — the same singer, the same temple, different occasions, the parts moving fluidly. Steelman the redactional view: the doublet looks too clean to be coincidence; the seams in Psalm 108 are visible (the prosodic break at v. 6); and the canonical text itself preserves the evidence of its own editing, which is theologically significant — Scripture's inspiration is compatible with editorial layering. **Where this leaves us:** the doublet does not destroy Davidic authorship; it complicates a flat view of authorship and forces readers to take seriously the editorial work as itself part of the canon's life.

The second tension is the cave: the psalm imagines glory rising "above the heavens" (vv. 5, 11) from a place of physical entrapment. How does a hunted man, body pressed against stone, sing the cosmic praise? Augustine and Luther both read this as paradigmatic — the *theologia crucis* hides God's exaltation inside the experience of being crushed. C.S. Lewis, in *Reflections on the Psalms*, found in Psalm 57 evidence that the Psalter knows joy not as an alternative to suffering but as joy *underneath* it. Bonhoeffer's prison letters echo the steadfast heart of v. 7. The closing question: when your circumstances are a cave, can you wake the dawn before it comes — not by denying the cave but by singing through it?
