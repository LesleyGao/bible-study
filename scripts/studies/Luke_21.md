# Luke 21: The Widow, the Stones, and the Times of the Gentiles

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

The temple offering box of 21:1 was almost certainly one of the thirteen *shofarot* — trumpet-shaped receptacles described in *Mishnah Shekalim* 6:5 — set in the Court of the Women, the only court accessible to female worshippers. Each was inscribed with the purpose of its contents: shekel-dues, bird-offerings, freewill gifts, and so on. The 1968 excavations directed by **Benjamin Mazar** along the southern and southwestern walls of the Temple Mount, and **Ronny Reich** and **Eli Shukron**'s subsequent work on the Pilgrim Road that ascended from the Pool of Siloam to the Hulda Gates, allow us to picture the architecture concretely: a rich worshipper would have made his offering visible to the public; the widow's two copper *lepta* (the smallest Jewish coin in circulation, struck under the Hasmoneans and Herodians) would have made an audible clink at most.

The temple complex itself dominates the chapter. The "noble stones and offerings" of 21:5 included Herod's signature ashlar masonry — some of the foundation stones at the Western Wall measure over 12 meters long and weigh upward of 500 tons (the largest, the so-called "Western Stone," is approximately 13.6 meters and an estimated 570 tons). Josephus describes the Royal Stoa's white limestone ablaze in the Mediterranean sun (*Antiquities* 15.412), and the offerings included gilded grapevines, the gift of foreign monarchs, and the votive shields and crowns of victorious generals. The destruction of 70 CE — after a five-month Roman siege under **Titus** — left, as Josephus reports, "nothing to make those that came thither believe it had ever been inhabited" (*War* 7.3). Archaeology confirms the violence: the burnt house excavated by **Nahman Avigad** in the Jewish Quarter shows a kitchen frozen in mid-meal, with carbonized wood, smashed pottery, and the severed forearm of a young woman pinned beneath a fallen step; the Burnt House and Herodian Quarter museums display the material remains.

Pilate's prefecture (26–36 CE) provides the political context for "kings and governors" in 21:12. The historical persecution Luke envisions — synagogue floggings, imprisonment, and trial before procurators and client kings — is the very experience documented in Acts 4–28: Peter and John before the Sanhedrin, Stephen stoned, Paul before Felix, Festus, and Agrippa II. Luke writes from the far side of these events.

The "Jerusalem surrounded by armies" of 21:20 is the most historically specific datable reference in the Gospels. The Roman siegeworks erected by Titus in 70 CE — described by Josephus (*War* 5.466–510) and visible in the archaeological record at the southwestern corner of the Temple Mount and on the Mount of Olives — are the most plausible referent. The "trampling underfoot by the Gentiles" of 21:24 has been read as an explicit reference to the period after 70, when the city was ploughed under and rebuilt as the Roman colony Aelia Capitolina under Hadrian (135 CE).

Greek terms worth attention: ***lēpta*** (λεπτά, the "two small copper coins" of 21:2); ***bdelygma*** is conspicuously *absent* from Luke 21 — Mark 13:14 and Matthew 24:15 use ***bdelygma tēs erēmōseōs*** (βδέλυγμα τῆς ἐρημώσεως, "abomination of desolation," from Daniel 9:27, 11:31, 12:11), but Luke replaces it with the historically transparent ***Ierousalēm kykloumenēn hypo stratopedōn*** (Ἰερουσαλὴμ κυκλουμένην ὑπὸ στρατοπέδων, "Jerusalem surrounded by encamped armies," 21:20). ***Kairoi ethnōn*** (καιροὶ ἐθνῶν, "times of the Gentiles," 21:24) is uniquely Lukan and programmatic. ***Hypomonē*** (ὑπομονή, "endurance," 21:19) is the eschatological virtue Luke commends to the persecuted church.

## Lens 2 — The Literature

Luke 21 is Luke's version of the Synoptic eschatological discourse (Mark 13; Matthew 24–25). It is shorter than Matthew's, sharper than Mark's, and crucially distinct in three places where Lukan redaction is unmistakable.

The chapter opens with the **widow's two coins** (21:1–4). Luke takes this from Mark 12:41–44 with minor stylistic edits, but the placement is significant. In Mark, the widow follows immediately after the warning against scribes "who devour widows' houses" (Mark 12:38–40 = Luke 20:46–47). The juxtaposition raises a startling question pressed by **Addison Wright**'s 1982 *Catholic Biblical Quarterly* essay "The Widow's Mites: Praise or Lament?": is Jesus *commending* the widow's piety, or is he *lamenting* a temple system that has succeeded in extracting from her even the last coin she had to live on? Wright argues for the lament reading — the widow is a victim, not a hero — and **Joel Green** (NICNT) develops this thoughtfully, noting that the immediate sequel is Jesus's announcement of the temple's destruction. The widow's offering is the system's last triumph and its self-condemnation. **John Nolland** (Word) and **Darrell Bock** (BECNT) defend the traditional praise reading. The text does not finally settle the question; Luke leaves the juxtaposition for the reader to feel.

The **Olivet Discourse** itself (21:5–36) shows Lukan distinctives at three critical points. First, Luke replaces Mark's coded "abomination of desolation" (Mark 13:14) with the transparent military image: **"when you see Jerusalem surrounded by armies, then know that its desolation has come near"** (21:20). Lukan scholarship since at least **C.H. Dodd**'s 1947 essay "The Fall of Jerusalem and the 'Abomination of Desolation'" has divided over whether Luke is rewording Mark in light of his post-70 historical knowledge (the dominant view, held by **Fitzmyer**, **Bovon**, **Fitzmyer**, **R.T. France**) or whether Luke is preserving an independent tradition that goes back to Jesus himself (the view of **Wright** in *Jesus and the Victory of God* and **B.W.R. Pearson**, who note that the language is not specific enough to require a *vaticinium ex eventu* and that Jesus could plausibly have foreseen the city's military destruction).

Second, Luke alone has **"Jerusalem will be trampled underfoot by the Gentiles, until the times of the Gentiles are fulfilled"** (21:24). This is programmatic for **Hans Conzelmann**'s *Theology of St. Luke* (1954), which read Luke as having reorganized eschatology into a salvation-historical scheme of three epochs: the time of Israel, the time of Jesus, and the time of the Church (= the time of the Gentile mission). The "times of the Gentiles" is Luke's solution to the delay of the parousia: the church now has a calling to fulfill before the end. Conzelmann's reading was foundational and contested. **Helmut Flender**, **I. Howard Marshall**, and **C.H. Talbert** offered modifications; **Wright** rejected the basic frame, arguing that Luke's eschatology remains as "imminent" as Mark's but has been re-narrated around the cross-resurrection-Pentecost as the inaugurating events of the new age.

Third, Luke's eschatology is more cosmic and less local than Mark's. Where Mark 13 oscillates between the Jerusalem siege and the Son of Man's return, Luke separates them more cleanly: the Jerusalem material (21:20–24) is followed by a clearly cosmic and parousia-oriented section (21:25–28) marked by the temporal hinge "and there will be signs in sun and moon and stars."

There are no major textual variants disturbing the chapter, though some witnesses harmonize 21:24 toward parallel Synoptic readings.

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

The widow's offering ties into Luke's pervasive concern for the *anawim* — the poor, the marginalized, the powerless whose vindication is the heart of the kingdom. From Mary's Magnificat (1:46–55) to the beatitudes-and-woes (6:20–26) to the prodigal son (15) to Lazarus and the rich man (16), Luke insists that the kingdom inverts the world's hierarchy. The widow's two coins join Anna the prophetess (2:36–38), the widow of Nain (7:11–17), the persistent widow (18:1–8), and the women who funded Jesus's ministry (8:1–3) as part of Luke's distinctive portrait of women's vocation in the kingdom. **Turid Karlsen Seim**'s *The Double Message: Patterns of Gender in Luke-Acts* is foundational here.

The temple's destruction theme has deep canonical roots. Jesus's words echo Jeremiah's temple sermon (Jeremiah 7, 26), which threatened destruction for a temple that had become "a den of robbers" — language Jesus quoted at the temple cleansing (Luke 19:46, citing Jer 7:11 and Isa 56:7). The destruction of the First Temple in 587 BCE haunts the Lukan-Jesus prediction of the Second Temple's fall. **Christopher Seitz**, **Brevard Childs**, and **Walter Brueggemann** have all traced the prophetic-canonical pattern.

Patristic readings worked these themes hard. **Origen**'s *Commentary on Matthew* and homilies on Luke distinguished the destruction of Jerusalem from the parousia, anticipating modern partition readings. **Eusebius** (*Ecclesiastical History* 3.5.3) preserves the tradition that the Jerusalem church fled to Pella (in the Decapolis, modern Jordan) before the Roman siege — citing Luke 21:21 ("let those in Judea flee to the mountains") as the prophecy fulfilled. **Cyril of Alexandria**'s *Commentary on Luke* (Homilies 139–140) reads the chapter as both historical (the fall of Jerusalem) and typological (the consummation of the age). **Ambrose**'s *Exposition of Luke* (Book 10) draws moral lessons from the widow: "she gave more than all because she gave her all." **Augustine** (*Letter* 199, on the end of the world) expressly rejected attempts to date the parousia from this chapter.

**Aquinas** (*Catena Aurea* on Luke 21) preserves the patristic consensus: the temple's destruction is the type, the parousia is the antitype. **Calvin**'s *Harmony* on Luke 21 is masterful at distinguishing the two horizons without separating them; he holds that the Lukan distinctives are pastoral, written for a church that needed to be steadied against premature eschatological enthusiasm.

In modern scholarship, the chapter has been a battleground. **Albert Schweitzer**'s *Quest of the Historical Jesus* (1906) read the discourse as evidence of Jesus's failed apocalyptic hope. **C.H. Dodd**'s "realized eschatology" played down the futurist elements. **Joachim Jeremias** advocated "inaugurated eschatology." **George Caird**'s *The Language and Imagery of the Bible* read the cosmic language of 21:25–28 as metaphorical-political, not literal-cosmological — a reading **N.T. Wright** developed in *The New Testament and the People of God* and *Jesus and the Victory of God*: the "sun darkened, moon failing, stars falling" language of 21:25–26 is Old Testament prophetic imagery (Isaiah 13:10; 34:4; Ezekiel 32:7; Joel 2:10) for the fall of nations and the transition between ages, not a literal description of cosmic disintegration. **Dale Allison** (*The End of the Ages Has Come*) defends a more literal apocalyptic reading.

Among major Lukan commentators: **Joseph Fitzmyer** (Anchor Bible) is the gold standard on the redactional analysis. **Joel Green** (NICNT) is theologically sensitive and develops Wright's reading on the lament-not-praise interpretation of the widow. **John Nolland** (Word) is patient on the Greek and the Old Testament citations. **Darrell Bock** (BECNT) defends an evangelical-conservative reading. **I. Howard Marshall** (NIGTC) is philologically meticulous. **Luke Timothy Johnson** (Sacra Pagina) traces the chapter's literary architecture. **François Bovon** (Hermeneia) is invaluable on the Wirkungsgeschichte. **Robert Tannehill**'s *Narrative Unity of Luke-Acts* shows how Luke 21 sets up Acts.

**Karl Barth** (*Church Dogmatics* III/2 §47.5; IV/3 §69.4) drew on Luke 21's eschatological waiting as the horizon of the church's witness: the time between the resurrection and the parousia is the time of *Bezeugung*, witness-bearing (cf. 21:13, "this will be your opportunity to bear witness"). **Bonhoeffer**'s *Letters and Papers from Prison* worked through 21:36 ("stay awake at all times") as he faced his own death.

Jewish readings: **Jacob Neusner** has noted that the temple-destruction discourse, on Lukan reading, runs into the same problem the rabbis worked through after 70 — how does Israel survive the loss of the temple? The rabbinic answer was Torah, prayer, and acts of loving-kindness as the "three pillars" replacing sacrifice (Pirkei Avot 1:2). The Lukan answer is that the church now stands in the temple's place: its members are the new temple, and Pentecost (Acts 2) is the new descent of glory. **Amy-Jill Levine** has cautioned that this can slide into supersessionism if not held carefully.

## Lens 4 — The Theological Intent

**Tension 1: Is the widow's mite story commendation or critique?** The traditional reading, going back at least to **Ambrose**, takes the widow as a model of total self-giving devotion — a positive type of the Christian who holds nothing back. **Calvin** and most Reformation expositors followed this reading. A second position, advanced by **Addison Wright** and developed by **Joel Green** and **Pheme Perkins**, reads the saying as Jesus's lament over a temple system that has "devoured" even this widow — turning her piety into evidence not of her virtue but of the institution's predation. The juxtaposition with the immediately preceding warning against scribes "who devour widows' houses" (20:47) and the immediately following announcement of the temple's destruction (21:6) is too neat to be accidental. A third reading, more dialectical, holds both sides at once: the widow's act is genuinely beautiful, *and* the system that demands it is genuinely corrupt. Steelmanning each: the praise reading honors the widow's agency and refuses to reduce her to victim; the lament reading honors the prophetic edge of Jesus's whole ministry against religious extortion; the dialectical reading honors the irreducible complexity of poor people's piety in oppressive systems. *Where this leaves us:* the text underdetermines the choice. Luke does not explicitly mark Jesus's tone, and that ambiguity may be the most theologically faithful reading. The widow is not a mere object lesson for either the rich or the church; she stands as a question to both.

**Tension 2: How imminent is the eschatology of Luke 21?** A first position, developed by **Conzelmann**, sees Luke as having softened the imminence of Mark 13 to accommodate the church to a long historical timeline. The "times of the Gentiles" inserts an indefinite period between Jerusalem's fall and the parousia. Luke is the evangelist of the long haul. A second position, defended by **N.T. Wright** and others, denies that Luke softens Mark and instead argues that all three Synoptic discourses use prophetic-apocalyptic language to talk primarily about the fall of Jerusalem and the inauguration of the new age in the cross-resurrection-Pentecost. The "coming of the Son of Man" of 21:27 is, on this reading, primarily the vindication of Jesus through these events, not a future cosmic event. A third position, the traditional Christian reading from **Augustine** through **Aquinas** and beyond, takes 21:5–24 to refer primarily to 70 CE and 21:25–36 to refer to a still-future parousia, with the discourse moving from the type to the antitype. Steelmanning: Conzelmann takes seriously the Lukan-redactional differences from Mark; Wright takes seriously the Jewish prophetic-apocalyptic genre and the inadequacy of literal-cosmic readings; the traditional reading takes seriously the church's lived expectation of the Lord's return. *Where this leaves us:* Luke 21 is most fairly read as a discourse with two horizons that Luke does not fully separate. The Jerusalem-destruction prediction was vindicated in 70 CE; the parousia language remains genuinely future and genuinely indeterminate; "this generation will not pass away" (21:32) refers most plausibly to the generation that witnessed Jerusalem's fall (with "all things" interpreted as the Jerusalem-judgment material), while the parousia is held in suspension as the church's enduring hope.

One unresolved question: if the "times of the Gentiles" must be "fulfilled" before the parousia (21:24), what does that fulfillment look like, and how would we know it had arrived? Conzelmann's answer (the geographic spread of the gospel) and Paul's "until the full number of the Gentiles has come in" (Romans 11:25) are theologically suggestive but not historically operationalizable. The church has been waiting for two millennia, and the timetable remains opaque.

## The Thread

Luke 21:24's "trampled by the Gentiles, until the times of the Gentiles are fulfilled" looks back to Daniel's seventy weeks (Dan 9:24–27) and forward to Paul's olive-tree theology in Romans 11. The "stay awake" of 21:36 will be tragically inverted in Gethsemane (22:46), where the disciples cannot stay awake even for an hour. The widow's "all she had to live on" (21:4) anticipates Jesus's own giving of his entire *bios* on the cross — the parallel between the widow's *holon ton bion autēs* (21:4) and Jesus's life-poured-out is hardly accidental in Lukan composition. And the prediction "they will lay their hands on you and persecute you" (21:12) becomes, in Acts 4–28, the actual narrative of the apostles' suffering, with the discourse functioning as a programmatic preview of the second volume.
